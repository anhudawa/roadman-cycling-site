The Sella Ronda is a 58-kilometre loop around the Sella massif in the Italian Dolomites, crossing four passes that sit between 1,875m and 2,240m. On any given summer day, it is a spectacular ride shared with motorbikes, campervans, and tourist traffic. On Sellaronda Bike Day — one day each June when all four passes close to cars — it becomes one of the purest cycling experiences in Europe.

Four climbs. None of them monstrous individually. Each one between 8 and 15 kilometres, averaging 5-8%. The kind of gradients that feel manageable on fresh legs after a cappuccino in Corvara. The challenge is the loop itself: 1,780 metres of vertical gain with no flat sections to recover on, all of it above 1,400m, three summits above 2,100m, and thin air that you don't notice until the third pass when your legs stop answering as quickly as they did at the bottom of the first.

This is how to prepare for it across 12 weeks — the fitness, the pacing, the gearing, and the altitude awareness that turns the Sella Ronda from a hard day into a controlled one.

## Key Takeaways

- 58km, four passes, 1,780m of climbing — accessible but not a pushover
- 12-week build from a base of 6-8 hours/week
- 2.5 W/kg minimum, 3.2+ W/kg for a strong ride
- Counter-clockwise is the easier direction (gentler climbs, steeper descents)
- Compact gearing (34x34) is not optional
- All summits above 1,875m — altitude costs you 5-8% of sea-level power
- 90g carbs/hour from the first pass, more at altitude than you think you need

## The Loop: Four Passes, One Massif

The Sella Ronda traces a rectangle around the Sella group, a limestone massif that sits in the centre of the western Dolomites. Four valleys. Four passes connecting them. One loop back to where you started.

The four passes, in counter-clockwise order from Corvara:

| Pass | Summit | Distance | Avg Gradient | Character |
|------|--------|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Passo Campolongo | 1,875m | 5.8km | 6.0% | Short opener, steady gradient |
| Passo Pordoi | 2,239m | 9.4km | 6.8% | Long, exposed, iconic Giro summit |
| Passo Sella | 2,240m | 5.5km | 7.9% | Short and steep, kicks in the final 2km |
| Passo Gardena | 2,121m | 10.0km | 4.8% | Longest approach, gentlest gradient |

The total: roughly 58km and 1,780m of elevation gain. Most fit amateurs finish in 3-5 hours. That range is wide because the loop rewards patient pacing and punishes early aggression — which is exactly what makes it a good training target.

Starting points matter. Corvara is the most popular. Selva di Val Gardena, Canazei, and Arabba all work — the loop returns you to where you began. On Bike Day, the closed roads mean you can start from any valley town and ride the loop without traffic.

The Maratona dles Dolomites crosses some of the same ground but is a different beast — [138km, seven passes, 4,230m of climbing](/blog/maratona-dles-dolomites-training-guide). The Sella Ronda is a strong first step toward the Maratona, and the Bike Day format strips away the race pressure entirely. No timing. No numbers. Just you and the road.

## Clockwise vs Counter-Clockwise

Direction changes the ride.

**Counter-clockwise** (the standard recommendation): gentler gradients on the climbs, steeper descents. From Corvara, you climb Campolongo first — the easiest of the four — which serves as a warm-up. The approach to Pordoi from Arabba is the classic Giro route: long, steady switchbacks with the gradient rarely exceeding 7%. Sella from Canazei is short and sharp. Gardena back to Corvara is the longest approach but the gentlest.

**Clockwise**: steeper climbs, more gradual descents. Gardena from Corvara becomes a steeper affair, the Sella drops down the more gradual side, and the Pordoi descent becomes the long Arabba side. Stronger riders choose this for the added climbing intensity.

If this is your first time on the loop, go counter-clockwise. The pacing is more forgiving, the warm-up climb is easier, and the descent off Gardena back into Corvara is the finest 10 kilometres of the day.

## Fitness Benchmarks: What the Sella Ronda Asks of You

The Sella Ronda is not the [Maratona](/blog/maratona-dles-dolomites-training-guide). It does not demand 4.0 W/kg or six-hour endurance. But it is four consecutive Dolomite passes with no recovery flats, and the altitude amplifies the effort. Here is what the numbers look like.

| W/kg (FTP) | Expected Finish | Experience |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| Below 2.0 | 5.5-6+ hours | Survival pace, lowest gear, stop at every summit |
| 2.5 | 4.5-5 hours | Comfortable but disciplined — pace is everything |
| 3.0 | 3.5-4 hours | Strong ride, able to push the final two passes |
| 3.5+ | Under 3 hours | Racing pace, climbing at threshold |

Use the [W/kg calculator](/tools/wkg) to get your number. Then set your zones with the [FTP zones tool](/tools/ftp-zones). The [Climbing Time Estimator](/tools/climb-time) will give you a projected ascent time for each pass based on your power and weight, and the [Race Predictor](/tools/race-predictor) will estimate your total loop time including the descents and transitions between passes.

**What the passes actually demand:**

No single pass on the Sella Ronda is a 60-minute threshold effort. Campolongo takes 15-25 minutes. Pordoi takes 30-50 minutes. Sella takes 15-25 minutes. Gardena takes 25-40 minutes. Individually, each is manageable. The cumulative effect — four climbs back-to-back with no flat recovery between them — is what separates riders who finish strong from riders who grind the last pass wondering why their legs stopped working.

**Altitude.** All four summits sit above 1,875m. Three are above 2,100m. Pordoi and Sella both touch 2,240m. At 2,000m, the air holds roughly 20% less oxygen than at sea level. [Professor Seiler](/guests/stephen-seiler)'s research on training intensity distribution matters here — riding at what feels like Zone 2 at sea level can drift into Zone 3 at altitude. Your heart rate runs 5-10 bpm higher at the same wattage. Your sustainable power drops 5-8%.

If you are coming from a sea-level training base, plan for that power loss and pace accordingly. For the specific climbing technique that matters on these gradients — cadence, position, pacing psychology — the [climbing guide](/blog/cycling-climbing-tips-stop-getting-dropped) covers the fundamentals.

## The 12-Week Training Plan

Three blocks of four weeks: base, build, peak. The Sella Ronda is more accessible than the Maratona or [Marmotte](/blog/la-marmotte-training-guide) — 12 weeks is appropriate for riders with a foundation of 6-8 hours per week.

### Weeks 1-4: Base

Volume: 7-9 hours/week.

Four to five rides, 80% in [Zone 2](/tools/ftp-zones). The aerobic foundation that everything else sits on. [Stephen Seiler](/guests/stephen-seiler)'s polarised training research is clear: the easy stuff has to be properly easy, or you're paying threshold prices for moderate-intensity returns.

Anchor session: long Zone 2 ride building from 2.5 hours to 3.5 hours over the block, with at least 500m of climbing in the second half. If you live in flat terrain, substitute an indoor session with simulated climbing — 2x15 minutes at 6-8% gradient, seated, at 65-70 RPM.

One session per week of tempo work: 2x20 minutes at 76-85% FTP. This builds the sustained-effort capacity the loop demands without pushing into threshold territory too early.

Track your [cadence](/tools/cadence) on climbing efforts. If you are grinding below 60 RPM on 6-7% grades, you are likely undergeared — and the Sella Ronda will expose that on pass three.

### Weeks 5-8: Build

Volume: 8-10 hours/week.

Two quality sessions per week. One threshold session: 3x10 minutes at 95-100% FTP, building to 3x12 minutes by week 8. One sustained climbing block: 3x10 minutes at 85-92% FTP on a real climb or at simulated gradient indoors.

[John Wakefield](/guests/john-wakefield) uses low-cadence torque work for climbing events. Treat 4-minute efforts at 40–60 RPM and RPE 7/10 as a coach-derived advanced option rather than a universal research prescription; use the [evidence guide](/blog/low-cadence-training-cycling-torque-intervals) before adding it. Your quads need to push through 30-50 minutes of sustained climbing repeatedly, not just once.

Long ride climbs to 4 hours with 1,000-1,500m of climbing. By week 8, you should have completed at least one ride with two sustained climbs of 20+ minutes separated by a short descent — training the rhythm of the loop.

### Weeks 9-11: Peak

Volume: 9-11 hours/week.

Sustained climbing volume increases. Long ride includes 3 climbs of 15-25 minutes, ideally back-to-back. By week 10, you should have done one 4-5 hour ride with 1,500m+ of climbing — the closest single-day analogue to the Sella Ronda.

Quality sessions tighten to event specificity: one threshold session of 2x15 minutes at 95% FTP, one sustained climbing block of 2x20 minutes at 85-90% FTP.

[Dan Lorang](/guests/dan-lorang)'s training philosophy — protect the easy days, compress the intensity into fewer sessions, make the long ride mimic the event — applies directly here. The peak block is where you rehearse the demands of four consecutive passes, not where you add volume for its own sake.

Monitor your [VAM](/tools/vam) (vertical metres per hour) on training climbs. A VAM of 800-1,000m/hr on 6-8% gradients at sub-threshold intensity confirms you are ready for the loop.

### Week 12: Taper

Volume drops 40%. Two short rides with 10-minute openers at threshold. No long efforts. No sustained climbing. The fitness is already in — the taper lets the fatigue drain while the form stays.

Travel to the Dolomites 2-3 days early if you can. Ride an easy 90-minute spin over Campolongo to feel the gradients, test your gearing under load, and let your legs adjust to the altitude. Eat well. Sleep. Hydrate.

## Pass-by-Pass Pacing Strategy

Pacing the Sella Ronda is simpler than the [Maratona](/blog/maratona-dles-dolomites-training-guide) because there is no Giau waiting to ambush you at km 80. But the same principle applies: pace on power, not on how the first climb feels. The first pass always feels easy. The fourth one never does.

For the counter-clockwise direction from Corvara:

**Passo Campolongo (1,875m) — the opener.** 5.8km at 6.0%. Steady, nowhere above 8%. Ride this at 70-75% FTP — high Zone 2. Warm-up pace. Do not chase wheels. The riders hammering Campolongo at threshold are the ones you will pass on Gardena two hours later.

**Passo Pordoi (2,239m) — the centrepiece.** 9.4km at 6.8% from Arabba. The longest climb of the day and the one where you find your rhythm. Ride the first 5km at 75-80% FTP, then settle at 80-85% for the final 4km where the gradient stiffens. Pordoi is a Giro summit for a reason — it is long, exposed, and the thin air above 2,000m makes the final switchbacks harder than the gradient suggests.

Refuel at the Pordoi summit. The views are extraordinary. Fill your bottle. Eat something solid. The descent to Canazei is fast and technical — brake smoothly, stay focused, save the sightseeing for the climb.

**Passo Sella (2,240m) — the sting.** 5.5km at 7.9% from Canazei. Short but the steepest average gradient of the day. The final 2km kick above 9% and on tired legs this is where the loop bites. Ride the first 3km at 80-85% FTP, then accept that the last 2km will feel harder than the numbers say they should. Sit down, keep the [cadence](/tools/cadence) above 60 RPM, and ride to the summit.

**Passo Gardena (2,121m) — the closer.** 10km at 4.8%. The longest approach but the gentlest gradient. By now you have three passes in the legs and 1,400m of climbing done. Ride Gardena at 75-80% FTP — steady tempo. The temptation is to push because the summit means the loop is nearly done. Resist it. The descent back into Corvara is the reward.

For detailed pacing frameworks beyond this event, the [sportive pacing guide](/blog/pacing-strategy-cycling-sportive) covers power-based strategies for multi-climb days.

## Gearing: Compact Is Not Optional

The Sella Ronda's gradients are moderate by Dolomite standards — Pordoi averages 6.8%, Sella averages 7.9%. On fresh legs those numbers are manageable in most gearing setups.

On pass-three legs with 1,200m of climbing already done and altitude thinning the air, those same gradients feel like a different road.

**Minimum recommendation:** compact chainset (50/34) with an 11-34 cassette. A 34x34 ratio gives you a 1:1 gear that lets you spin at 65-70 RPM on 8-9% gradients at sustainable wattage.

**If your derailleur fits it:** a 34x36 cassette is insurance. The extra two teeth on the back cost you nothing on the descents and buy you a survival gear on Sella's final 2km when the gradient touches 10%.

**What to avoid:** a standard crankset (53/39) with an 11-28 cassette. Your lowest gear (39x28) puts you at 70+ RPM on 7% only if you are pushing 250+ watts. On the fourth pass, with altitude and fatigue compounding, that gear demands more power than most amateurs have left.

Test your gearing before travel. Find a 7-8% local climb. Ride it at the end of a 3-hour ride, not fresh. If your easiest gear forces you below 55 RPM at sustainable power, change the cassette. Use the [gear ratio calculator](/tools/gear-ratio) to compare setups and find the right combination for your wheel size.

## The Altitude Factor

Every summit on the Sella Ronda sits above 1,875m. Three of four exceed 2,100m. The valleys between the passes sit at 1,400-1,600m. You are riding above meaningful altitude for the entire loop.

What this means physiologically:

**Reduced oxygen availability.** At 2,200m — the Pordoi and Sella summits — the air holds roughly 22% less oxygen than at sea level. Your VO2max drops proportionally. The power you can sustain for 30 minutes at sea level is not the power you can sustain at the top of Pordoi.

**Higher heart rate at equivalent power.** Expect your heart rate to run 5-10 bpm higher than sea-level values at the same wattage. If you pace on heart rate alone, you will underperform — your heart rate hits the ceiling before your legs should. Pace on power. If you do not have a power meter, pace on RPE and accept that the numbers on the heart rate monitor will look alarming.

**Dehydration accelerates.** Drier air at altitude plus UV exposure above 2,000m means you lose water faster than on a valley ride. The temperature at the summits can be 10-15 degrees Celsius cooler than the valley floors — you feel colder, drink less, and dehydrate anyway. Use the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) to estimate your needs, and add 10-15% to the result for altitude.

**What to do about it.** Arrive 2-3 days early if possible. Ride easy. Spin over Campolongo on day one. Let your body make the small adaptations it can in 48-72 hours — increased respiratory rate, slightly elevated heart rate baseline, better blood flow regulation. Full altitude acclimatisation takes weeks, but even two days at 1,400m makes the first pass feel less jarring.

If you are travelling from sea level and cannot arrive early, reduce your target power on the climbs by 5-8% from your sea-level benchmarks. Pace conservatively on the first two passes. The altitude catch-up hits hardest on the third and fourth climbs.

## Fuelling and Logistics

### On-the-Bike Nutrition

The Sella Ronda is a 3-5 hour effort, not a 7-hour Maratona. But the climbing density and altitude burn through glycogen faster than the duration suggests. Treat it like a 5-hour fuelling plan even if you expect to finish in 3.5.

**Carbohydrate target:** 80-90g per hour from the first climb. Two gels and a bar each hour, or a 90g/hour drink mix paired with one solid item every 45 minutes. Start eating on Campolongo — not after it. The altitude accelerates glycogen depletion, and by the time you feel flat it is already too late. Use the [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) to dial your numbers to body weight and expected duration.

**Summit stops.** Brief and functional. Fill a bottle, eat something solid, add a layer for the descent. The summit bars on Pordoi and Sella sell espresso, cake, and panini — the Dolomites feed you well. Limit each stop to 5-7 minutes. The legs stiffen fast at altitude once you stop pedalling.

**Caffeine.** One caffeine gel saved for the base of Sella — the third pass — is the smart play. The fatigue-masking effect of 100-200mg of caffeine landing as you hit the steepest gradient of the day is measurable. Time it so absorption peaks in the final 2km.

### Hydration

600-750ml per hour. More in the heat, less on the cold descents. Electrolyte mix in every bottle. The Dolomite UV at 2,000m is intense even when the air temperature feels cool — sunscreen at every stop and electrolyte tabs in your jersey pocket.

### What to Carry

- Two 750ml bottles (one water, one electrolyte mix)
- Four gels and two bars minimum for a 4-hour target
- One caffeine gel held back for pass three
- Gilet for descents (non-negotiable — the Pordoi descent is 15 minutes of cold wind where the [feels-like temperature](/tools/wind-chill) can drop 10-15°C below the summit reading)
- Arm warmers for the morning start
- Emergency rain cape if the forecast shows any afternoon storm risk
- Phone, card, ID
- Spare tube or repair kit

### Starting Point Logistics

Corvara is the most popular base. Hotels are cycling-friendly, bike shops are within walking distance, and the counter-clockwise loop starts with the easiest climb. Selva di Val Gardena is the second choice — slightly more access to the Gardena valley, good cafes, strong cycling infrastructure.

For broader Dolomites planning — where to base, which passes to prioritise, and when to go — the [full Dolomites cycling guide](/blog/cycling-in-the-dolomites-guide) covers the region pass by pass.

For Bike Day specifically: arrive the day before. The road closures typically run from early morning until mid-afternoon. Start early to avoid the crowds on the first climbs. The event is free — no registration, no numbers. The roads simply close to cars and the cyclists ride.

## Extending the Ride: The Sellaronda Hero

The standard Sella Ronda loop is 58km with 1,780m of climbing. If that sounds like a warm-up, there is a harder option.

The Sellaronda Hero adds Passo Giau (2,236m) and Passo Falzarego (2,105m) to the circuit, extending the route to roughly 86km with 4,200m of climbing. That puts it squarely in Maratona territory — a full day of Dolomite climbing with two of the hardest passes in the region bolted onto the standard loop.

The Giau — 9.8km at 9.3% average — is the crux. It is the hardest sustained climb in the western Dolomites, and it lands after you have already ridden the standard four passes. If you are considering the Hero, the [Maratona training guide](/blog/maratona-dles-dolomites-training-guide) covers the Giau-specific preparation in detail.

The Hero demands 3.5+ W/kg and 14-16 weeks of climbing-specific preparation. It is not the Sella Ronda with two extra passes attached — it is a fundamentally different event. Build toward the standard loop first. Ride Bike Day. Come back for the Hero the following year with the Giau in your training plan.

## Race-Day Checklist

**The evening before:**
- Lay out kit including gilet, arm warmers, and emergency rain cape
- Charge devices — bike computer, power meter, phone
- Load the route on your GPS so you do not miss a turn in the Canazei valley
- Eat well: pasta, bread, protein, 8-10g carbs per kg of body weight across the day
- Two bottles prepped in the fridge: one water, one electrolyte mix

**Morning:**
- Breakfast 2.5-3 hours before start: oats, banana, coffee, toast with jam
- Start eating on Campolongo, not after
- Arm warmers on — the valley floor at 07:00 in June is 8-12 degrees Celsius

**On the road:**
- Pace Campolongo and Pordoi on power, not on feel
- Fill a bottle at every summit stop
- Gilet on before every descent
- Caffeine gel at the base of Sella
- Enjoy Gardena — the last climb is the gentlest, the descent is the best, and the loop is nearly done

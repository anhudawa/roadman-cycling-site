Here is the thing nobody tells you about the Bealach Mor. The climbing is hard — 2,500 to 3,000 metres of it across 140km, including Britain's highest paved road with ramps that touch 20 per cent. But the climbing is not the hardest part. The hardest part is the remoteness. This is not a sportive where you puncture and wait five minutes for the broom wagon. This is a sportive where you puncture on a single-track road in Wester Ross with no mobile signal, no passing traffic, and the next village 30km away over a mountain. If you cannot fix it yourself, you are walking.

Most UK sportives are well-supported events on roads never more than a few miles from a village, a phone signal, and a Plan B. The Bealach Mor strips all of that away. Three hundred riders on some of the most isolated roads in Britain. Feed stations exist, but they are far apart. Support vehicles cover the route, but the route covers terrain where a van cannot easily reach you. And the scenery — the lochs, Torridon, the Applecross Peninsula, the wild coastline — is so staggeringly beautiful that you will forget how hard it is. Briefly.

That combination of physical difficulty, genuine remoteness, and extraordinary beauty is what makes the Bealach Mor unlike anything else in the UK calendar. It demands not just fitness, but self-sufficiency, weather resilience, and the honest understanding that out here, you are largely on your own.

## Key Takeaways

- Bealach Mor covers approximately 140km with 2,500-3,000m of climbing through some of the most remote and exposed terrain in Britain
- Bealach na Ba is Britain's highest paved road at 626m — 9km at approximately 7 per cent average with ramps to 20 per cent
- Target 2.5-3.0 W/kg for a comfortable finish, but wind on exposed sections raises the effective demand significantly
- A 12-week build with progressive long rides and specific climbing preparation is essential
- September in the Scottish Highlands delivers every weather condition in a single day
- Self-sufficiency is a genuine requirement — carry more food, more tools, and more layers than any other UK sportive
- The scenery is among the most dramatic in European cycling

## Course Profile and Key Sections

### The Opening Miles Along the Lochs

The route begins from Kinlochewe, heading through Glen Torridon with mountains rising on either side. The first 30-40km roll alongside lochs and through glens on single-track roads with passing places. The gradients are gentle — short rises of 3-5 per cent, nothing sustained — but the terrain quality sets the tone. These are Highland roads, not Surrey dual carriageways. Rough patches, loose gravel, potholes hidden by standing water. Use this section to settle into the rhythm of the day, eat early, and resist the adrenaline.

### The Approach to Bealach na Ba

The road towards the Applecross Peninsula tightens. The climbing begins in earnest — shorter ascents through moorland and along the coast, each one 2-4km at 5-8 per cent. None would trouble you in isolation. Together, with 60-80km already in the legs, they are the warm-up act. Trees disappear. The wind arrives properly. The views across to Skye open up. This is the section where the Bealach Mor stops feeling like a sportive and starts feeling like an expedition.

### Bealach na Ba — The Climb

Let me be really clear about this. Bealach na Ba is Britain's highest paved road at 626 metres, and it earns every metre. Nine kilometres at approximately 7 per cent average with ramps that hit 20 per cent. Hairpin bends carved into the mountainside. Single-track road with sheer drops on one side and rock face on the other. In clear weather, the views across the Applecross Peninsula to Raasay and Skye are among the finest in European cycling. In cloud, you see twenty metres of wet tarmac and your front wheel disappearing into grey.

The first two kilometres are honest — 5-6 per cent, wide enough for two riders side by side. Then it kicks. The ramps to 15-20 per cent arrive in bursts between the hairpins, and the road narrows to genuine single-track. In wet conditions, rock runoff creates streams across the road surface. The final two kilometres ease slightly before the summit, but by then, the damage is done.

Use the [climb time calculator](/tools/climb-time) to estimate your time and the [VAM calculator](/tools/vam) to set a sustainable climbing rate. At 7 per cent average, a 75kg rider at 2.5 W/kg takes roughly 35-40 minutes. At 3.0 W/kg, closer to 28-32 minutes. The good news: pacing to Bealach na Ba matters more than pacing on it.

### The Applecross Peninsula Descent and Coastal Road

The descent off Bealach na Ba is spectacular and terrifying in roughly equal measure. Tight hairpins, steep gradients, variable surface, and — if the weather has turned — wet roads with no run-off. Brake early. Feather, do not grab. Weight through the outside pedal. The coastal road that follows along the Applecross Peninsula is gentler — rolling terrain along the shoreline with short climbs of 2-3km — and if the wind is behind you, this is the reward section. Eat. Drink. Recover from the pass.

### The Return Through Torridon and Final Climbs

The route back through Torridon delivers several more climbs — shorter than Bealach na Ba but arriving when the legs are heaviest. Rises of 3-5km at 5-8 per cent with the Torridon mountains — Liathach, Beinn Eighe — rising directly above the road. The final 20km back towards Kinlochewe is where pacing and nutrition discipline pay off. Riders who went too hard on Bealach na Ba and stopped eating at kilometre 80 are walking here. Riders who paced and fuelled are tired but intact.

The wind caveat matters here more than on any other UK event. A 2.5 W/kg rider in still conditions covers the route comfortably. That same rider into a 30km/h headwind on the exposed coastal and moorland sections needs 15-25 per cent more power to maintain the same speed. The Highlands rarely deliver still conditions. Build wind resistance into your fitness targets.

## 12-Week Training Plan Overview

### Phase 1: Base Building (Weeks 1-4)

Volume: 6-8 hours per week rising to 8-9 hours by week four.

Four to five rides per week. Eighty per cent in Zone 2. The aerobic foundation. Build the long ride from 2.5 hours in week one to 4 hours by week four, and ride rolling terrain wherever possible. The Bealach Mor never flattens out — if your training roads are flat, add a climb loop within the long ride. Even 60m of gain repeated eight times is more specific than four hours on flat roads.

Midweek: two rides of 60-75 minutes at Zone 2, one ride of 60-90 minutes with a 20-minute tempo block at 85-90 per cent of FTP.

Off-bike core work twice per week: planks (3x45 seconds), side planks (3x30 seconds per side), dead bugs (3x10 per side), single-leg glute bridges (3x12 per side), and hip mobility work. Twenty minutes per session. The sustained climbing on Bealach na Ba demands a stable core when the gradient bites at 15-20 per cent and your cadence drops to 55 rpm. This is precisely what core work builds.

### Phase 2: Climbing Power (Weeks 5-8)

Volume: 8-10 hours per week.

Two quality sessions per week alongside continued endurance volume.

**Key session 1: Sustained climbing intervals.** Find a hill of 8-15 minutes. Ride 3x10 minutes at 88-93 per cent of FTP in week five, building to 3x15 minutes by week eight with 5 minutes recovery between efforts. The Bealach na Ba is a 28-40 minute climb depending on your power. Training sustained efforts of 10-15 minutes at sub-threshold builds the specific capacity to hold the pass together when the gradient kicks.

**Key session 2: Over-under climbing repeats.** On a 5-8 minute hill, ride 2 minutes at 95 per cent of FTP then 2 minutes at 80 per cent, repeated for the full climb length. Four to five repeats. This replicates the gradient variability on Bealach na Ba — the ramps to 15-20 per cent force surges above threshold before the road eases and you recover without stopping.

**Long ride extension:** Build to 5 hours by week eight with 1,200-1,500m of climbing.

### Phase 3: Event Readiness (Weeks 9-10)

Volume: 9-10 hours per week. Quality peaks.

**The over-distance ride (week 9 or 10 weekend):** One ride of 5-6 hours with 1,800-2,000m of climbing. Full fuelling rehearsal. Full kit rehearsal. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to map your nutrition across the ride. This is the final stress test.

**Weather-exposure ride:** At least one long ride in genuinely poor weather — rain, wind, cold. If the first time you ride four hours in those conditions is race day, you have made a serious error. Your kit, your eating, your bike handling in the wet — all of these need testing before you are 40km from anywhere on a single-track road.

**Midweek sharpener:** 4x8 minutes at 95-100 per cent of FTP with 4 minutes recovery. The only true threshold work in the plan, positioned here to top up the aerobic ceiling without the fatigue cost of repeated threshold blocks.

### Phase 4: Taper (Weeks 11-12)

**Week 11:** Volume drops to 6-7 hours. Three rides of 60-90 minutes with two efforts of 5 minutes at 90-95 per cent of FTP in each. One easy ride of 90-120 minutes. No long ride.

**Week 12 (race week):** Two rides of 45-60 minutes — Monday and Wednesday — with 3-4 openers of 30 seconds at high cadence. Thursday off. Friday a 20-minute spin if logistics allow. Saturday or Sunday: ride.

Core work continues through the taper at the same frequency but reduced intensity. Maintain the movement patterns. Do not add volume.

## Key Sessions

### Sustained Climbing Intervals

Warm up 15 minutes. Find a climb of 10-15 minutes at 5-8 per cent. Ride 3x12 minutes at 88-93 per cent of FTP, seated, at 65-75 rpm. Five minutes easy spinning between efforts. Cool down 10 minutes. The backbone session for Bealach na Ba — it teaches your body to sustain near-threshold power on a gradient for the duration that matters. If your local hills are shorter, ride continuous loops.

### The Long Hilly Ride

Your weekend anchor. Build from 3 hours to 5-6 hours across the 12 weeks with climbing progressing from 600m to 2,000m. Include a 60-90 minute block at 75-85 per cent of FTP in the middle third — this is the exact demand of the climbs after Bealach na Ba. Eat on schedule throughout. Practise carrying everything you would carry on race day.

### Wind-Resistance Riding

Choose the most exposed road you can access. Ride 60-90 minutes in genuine crosswind. Hold a stable line without gripping the bars. Relax the shoulders. Anticipate gusts. If riding with a partner, practise echelon formation. The exposed sections along the lochs and over the moorland are proper crosswind territory, and the skill of riding efficiently in wind is trainable.

## Nutrition Strategy

Here is where the Bealach Mor differs from every other UK sportive. If you bonk between Torridon and Applecross, there is no corner shop. There is no passing van. There is you, your pockets, and however much food you packed. Carry enough to fuel the entire ride without relying on feed stations. Treat the feed stations as a bonus, not the plan.

Target 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour from the first 20 minutes. Cold weather suppresses appetite — eat to schedule, not to hunger. Set a 20-minute timer on your head unit and eat regardless of how you feel.

Pre-ride: 8-10g of carbohydrate per kg of body weight in the 24-36 hours before. Substantial breakfast three hours before the start — porridge, toast, a banana, coffee.

On the bike: gels, bars, rice cakes, whatever sits well. Carry at least eight gels and two bars in jersey pockets and a top tube bag. 1,500ml of fluid capacity minimum — two large bottles plus a backup. Electrolyte mix in every bottle. A caffeine gel before Bealach na Ba sharpens the focus when you need it most. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to map your intake across the estimated ride time.

## Equipment and Gear

### Gearing

A compact chainset (50/34) with a 32-tooth rear cassette is the minimum recommendation. A 34-tooth cassette is better. The 20 per cent ramps on Bealach na Ba after 60-plus kilometres of riding are not the place to discover you do not have a low enough gear. A 34x34 lets you spin at 55-60 rpm on the steepest pitches. Muscling a 34x28 up those gradients with tired legs is a recipe for cramping and walking. Check your setup with the [gear ratio calculator](/tools/gear-ratio).

### Waterproof Kit — Essential, Not Optional

A proper waterproof jacket with sealed seams. Not a shower-proof gilet. Not a packable wind shell. A jacket that keeps rain out in a sustained downpour while riding at speed in a crosswind at 8 degrees. Pack it every time regardless of forecast. Arm warmers. Gilet. Full-finger gloves. A skull cap or buff under the helmet. Knee warmers if the forecast is below 10 degrees. This is non-negotiable.

### Spares for Self-Sufficiency

Two inner tubes. Tyre levers. A CO2 inflator and two cartridges plus a mini pump as backup. A multi-tool. Two quick links for your chain. A tyre boot. A mobile phone in a waterproof bag with an offline map downloaded. Emergency cash. An emergency foil blanket weighs nothing and could matter if you are stranded in the cold.

### Tyre Choice

Road surfaces vary from reasonable tarmac to rough Highland single-track with gravel patches, potholes, and water runoff. Run 28mm tyres minimum. 30-32mm is better if your frame clears them — wider tyres absorb rough surfaces, reduce puncture risk, and cost almost nothing in rolling resistance at sportive speeds. Tubeless with sealant is the ideal setup for this terrain. Small punctures seal themselves, and the last thing you need is a flat on Bealach na Ba with shaking hands and a 20 per cent gradient.

## Race Day Pacing

### Manage Bealach na Ba

The single biggest pacing mistake is arriving at the bottom of Bealach na Ba already spent. Every kilometre before the pass should be ridden with the pass in mind. Hold Zone 2 on the smaller climbs. Save the effort for the nine kilometres that matter most.

On the climb itself, ride at 80-85 per cent of FTP. Sit. Hold 60-70 rpm on the steep ramps. Stand briefly on the hairpins to shift muscle groups, then sit again. Let riders who attack the early ramps go — you will pass many of them in the final two kilometres when they are walking.

### Wind Management

The exposed sections along the lochs and over the moorland are where wind costs energy that the gradient alone does not explain. If you are riding into a headwind, ease back to 70 per cent of FTP and accept the slower speed. Do not fight it. If you can find a group, ride in an echelon and share the work. The maths is simple. The ego is the problem.

### Conservative Early Pacing

The first 60km should feel easy. If it does not, you are going too hard. The Bealach Mor back-loads its difficulty — the hardest climbing, the most exposed terrain, and the greatest fatigue all arrive in the second half. Riders who ride the first half as if it were the whole event are sitting at the side of the road at kilometre 110.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Underestimating the weather.** September in the Highlands is not late summer. It is early autumn. Riders who check the forecast at sea level and dress for 14 degrees discover that 14 degrees at sea level is 8 degrees on Bealach na Ba with wind chill below 5. Prepare for mountain weather.

2. **Wrong clothing.** A shower-proof gilet is not a waterproof jacket. Fingerless mitts are not cold-weather gloves. The distinction between fine and disastrous on the Bealach Mor is often one layer — the layer you left at home because the morning looked alright.

3. **Insufficient food.** This is the remoteness problem. Riders who carry enough food for a well-supported sportive — two gels and a bar — run out between feed stations and have nothing left for the final 40km. Carry enough to fuel the entire ride independently. The extra 200 grams in your pockets is the difference between riding and walking.

4. **Going too hard on Bealach na Ba.** The pass is dramatic. The temptation to push beyond your sustainable power is overwhelming. But the event does not end at the summit. There are 40-50km and several more climbs still to come, and the legs remember every watt you overspent.

5. **Poor descending in wind and wet.** The descent off Bealach na Ba in wet conditions on tired legs with cold hands is the single most dangerous section of any UK sportive. Brake early. Cover the brakes. Weight through the outside pedal. Take the hairpins wide. There is no time bonus for descending fast.

## Travel and Logistics

Here is the thing nobody considers until it is too late: getting to Wester Ross is itself a commitment. Kinlochewe is roughly 5-6 hours by car from Edinburgh, 7-8 from Manchester. There is no quick motorway run. The final 60-70 miles are single-track Highland roads — the same kind of roads you will be riding.

Arrive the day before. Book accommodation early — Kinlochewe and the Torridon area have limited options, and the Bealach Mor fills them. A B&B or small hotel in Kinlochewe, Torridon, or Shieldaig puts you within easy reach of the start.

The atmosphere of a small-field Highland event is worth the drive. Three hundred riders in Wester Ross is a different world from five thousand at a mass-participation sportive. The evening before has the feeling of a club run that got gloriously out of hand.

## Recovery Plan

The 48 hours after the Bealach Mor matter. The combination of 140km, 2,500-3,000m of climbing, and Highland conditions creates deep fatigue that a single rest day does not resolve.

**Day 1 (post-ride):** Substantial meal within 90 minutes — protein and carbohydrate. Stretch gently. Walk. Hydrate. Sleep.

**Days 2-5:** A 20-30 minute easy spin on day two if the legs allow it, or walk. Easy rides of 45-60 minutes at Zone 1 from day three onwards if you feel like riding. No intensity. No pressure. The legs will tell you when they are ready.

**Week 2:** Resume normal training volume at reduced intensity. The fitness does not disappear in a week. The fatigue, if ignored, becomes injury.

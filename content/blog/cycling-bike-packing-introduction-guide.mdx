Here is the thing nobody tells you about bikepacking. You will spend years obsessing over gram counts. Titanium bolts. Carbon bottle cages that save eleven grams over the alloy ones. You will know what your bike weighs to the nearest fifty grams, and you will feel a quiet smugness about it.

And then one Tuesday evening, you will see a photograph of someone riding a loaded bike down a gravel track at sunset with bags strapped to every tube, and something will shift. Within a week, you will be planning to add eight to twelve kilograms to the machine you spent three seasons making lighter.

It sounds absurd. It is absurd. And it is also the most fun a lot of experienced road cyclists have had on a bike in a decade.

## The Road Cyclist's Bikepacking Problem

Bikepacking is the fastest-growing segment of cycling, and it is not close. The numbers from Komoot, from Apidura's sales figures, from the sheer volume of bikepacking content flooding YouTube — all of it points in the same direction. Riders who have spent years in the road world are looking for something different. Not something better. Something different.

I have talked to hundreds of cyclists through the podcast, and the pattern repeats. Rider gets into road cycling. Rider buys a power meter. Rider chases FTP for three, four years. FTP plateaus. Rider starts to wonder what the point is. The numbers that once felt motivating now feel like a ceiling. Every ride becomes a data set. Somewhere along the way, the bike stopped being a vehicle for adventure and became a device for generating training stress.

That is the gap bikepacking fills. Not as a replacement for structured training — most bikepackers still ride with power meters, still do intervals. But as a reset. A reminder that the bicycle is a machine that lets you go places under your own power. Actual places. Places where you sleep, eat, wake up, and ride again.

For masters cyclists especially — riders in their late thirties, forties, fifties — this resonates. At some point, the question shifts from "how fast can I go?" to "where can this bike take me?" Bikepacking is the answer to that second question.

The tension is real. Everything you have learned as a road cyclist screams that weight is the enemy. And you are right. A loaded rig is slower. It climbs worse. The handling changes. But here is the counterpoint: when you are riding a quiet lane at dusk with everything you need for the night on your bike, the extra drag is completely irrelevant. Performance gives way to experience. That shift — from measuring the ride to feeling the ride — is why road cyclists fall hard for this.

## What You Already Know That Transfers

If you have spent years as a road cyclist, you are not starting from scratch. Not even close. Most of what you already know applies directly to bikepacking, and the stuff that does not is easy to learn.

**Endurance fitness.** If you can ride four or five hours on a Saturday at a steady tempo, you have the aerobic engine for a bikepacking trip. The intensity is lower — mostly zone two, zone three on climbs — but the duration is longer. A rider who can hold 200 watts for five hours on the road can hold 160 watts for seven hours loaded without specific training.

**Fuelling habits.** You already know how to eat on the bike. You know what your stomach tolerates, how many carbohydrates per hour you need, when to eat before you bonk rather than after. Bikepacking adds a second dimension — the pub stop or village shop becomes part of the infrastructure of the ride — but in-ride nutrition is identical.

**Mechanical self-sufficiency.** You can fix a puncture, adjust a derailleur, replace a chain link. These skills matter more on a bikepacking trip — no team car, no mate driving out to collect you — but you already have them.

**Pacing by feel.** Years of riding with a power meter have given you the ability to pace by feel. You know what zone two feels like in your legs. In bikepacking, where the goal is to finish the day with energy left for the next one, that instinct is gold.

**Route reading.** You can read a road. You understand how gradient, shelter, and exposure affect the ride. All of it applies — you are just adding new variables. Surface quality. Gate access. Whether that green dotted line on the map is a rideable track or a muddy stream bed.

The biggest adjustment is pace. You are moving at 15-20 km/h loaded, not 25-30 km/h clean. On rough surfaces, 12 km/h is normal. The mental shift from "I can cover 100 km in three hours" to "I will cover 80 km in a full day" takes a ride or two to calibrate. It is not a fitness problem. It is an expectations problem.

## The Three-Bag System

Here is where bikepacking gets practical. The bag system is not complicated, but it is specific, and getting it right makes the difference between a bike that handles and one that fights you every kilometre.

The standard setup uses three bags. Frame bag. Saddle bag. Handlebar roll. Between them, they carry everything you need for an overnight trip, and they do it without requiring racks, panniers, or any modification to your frame.

### The Frame Bag

This sits inside the main triangle of your frame, attached to the top tube, down tube, and seat tube with Velcro straps. Low, central, close to the bike's natural balance point. It carries your heavy, dense items: tools and spares, water bladder or extra bottles, electronics, food for the day. Anything that weighs a lot relative to its size goes here.

A full-frame bag fills the entire triangle and holds 5-8 litres. A half-frame bag leaves room for water bottles and holds 3-4 litres. For a first trip, a half-frame bag with two bottle cages is often the better choice.

Brands: Apidura (the Expedition frame pack is the benchmark), Restrap (British-made, excellent build quality), Ortlieb (waterproof by default). Budget options from Alpkit and Topeak work for a first trip.

### The Saddle Bag

A 10-15 litre drybag-style roll that hangs beneath and behind your saddle. Not the same as a small road saddle pouch — this extends back from the saddle like a tail. It carries your sleeping kit: sleeping bag, mat, bivvy bag. These items are light for their volume, which is exactly what the saddle bag is designed for.

The key is packing it tightly. A loosely packed saddle bag sways under pedalling, pulling the bike left and right. Compress the contents, roll the closure tight, strap it down until it feels solid. If it wobbles when you shake the bike, repack it. Look for 10 litres minimum for an overnight trip, 14-17 litres for multi-day.

### The Handlebar Roll

A cylindrical drybag that mounts to your handlebars via a harness or cradle. It holds clothing layers and your waterproof jacket — items you might need during the ride. Pack soft, compressible items: a down jacket, a spare base layer, a waterproof shell, a buff. Nothing rigid, nothing heavy. Heavy items here make the front end slow to turn.

The system exists for a reason. Weight distributed between three contact points. No high centre of gravity from panniers. No racks required. The bike still feels like a bike. You can still get out of the saddle on climbs.

## Weight Distribution: The Rule That Saves Your Ride

Total weight matters less than where that weight sits. Most first-timers get this wrong, and the bike punishes them for it.

The rule: heavy items low and central, light items high and towards the extremes. Dense gear — tools, food, water, electronics — goes in the frame bag. Bulky light gear — sleeping bag, mat, clothing — goes in the saddle bag and handlebar roll. Nothing goes on your back.

What happens when you ignore this. Heavy items in the saddle bag and the rear becomes a pendulum — lateral sway with every pedal stroke, loose feeling on descents. Heavy items in the handlebar roll and the front end slugs — dampened steering, poor tracking on rough surfaces. Anything meaningful in a backpack and your lower back hates you within two hours. For a seven-hour ride, it is misery.

The correct distribution:

- **Frame bag (40-50% of total gear weight):** Multi-tool, spare tube, tyre levers, chain link, pump. Battery pack. Day food. Water. Phone. Wallet.
- **Saddle bag (30-35%):** Sleeping bag or liner. Sleeping mat. Bivvy bag if camping. Spare socks and underwear.
- **Handlebar roll (15-20%):** Waterproof jacket. Insulated jacket. Spare base layer. Buff or beanie.

Pack symmetrically where possible. Asymmetric weight causes the bike to pull in one direction under braking, and on tired legs after six hours, that pull becomes exhausting to correct.

## Route Planning: A Different Game

Road cyclists plan routes by distance, elevation, and surface. Bikepacking inverts this completely. You are not looking for the fastest way between two points. You are looking for the most interesting way, constrained by three things you have probably never thought about: where to resupply, where to sleep, and where to find water.

**Resupply points.** You need to know where shops, pubs, and cafes exist along your route — and when they are open. A remote section of 40 km with no services is fine if you planned for it. It is not fine if you expected a village shop that closed at 4pm and you arrive at 5.

**Accommodation or camping spots.** If you are booking a B&B, your route is anchored to that location. If you are wild camping, you need sheltered ground and legal access (Scotland's right-to-roam is your friend; England and Wales are more complicated). Either way, plan your daily distance to reach somewhere you can sleep before dark.

**Water sources.** On a warm day, you drink 500-750ml per hour. You cannot carry enough for six hours. Know where to fill up — taps at churches or cemeteries, cafes, petrol stations. In the UK and Ireland, water is rarely a crisis. In southern Europe, it can be.

For planning tools: **Komoot** is excellent — its surface-type overlay shows gravel, trail, and tarmac sections. **Bikepacking.com** publishes curated routes with resupply notes and GPS files. **Ordnance Survey Maps** remain the gold standard for off-road navigation in Britain.

Plan for daylight on first trips — aim to finish an hour before sunset. And plan conservatively. Your average speed loaded, on mixed surfaces, will be 12-18 km/h. An 80 km day with 800m of climbing is five to seven hours of riding before stops. Overambitious distance planning is the second-most-common mistake after overpacking.

## Your First Overnight: The 80-120 km Template

Your first bikepacking trip should not be an expedition. It should be a proof of concept. One night. A known-ish route. Booked accommodation. A bailout option. The goal is to finish wanting more, not to suffer through an epic.

**Total distance: 80-120 km, split across two days.** Day one: 40-70 km to your overnight stop. Day two: 40-60 km back to the start, or onwards to a train station. Err towards a longer first day and a shorter second day — you will be slower on day two.

**Choose a route you partly know.** Ride roads or trails you have been on before, at least for the first 30 km. This removes navigation stress while you adjust to how the loaded bike handles.

**Book your accommodation.** A B&B, a hostel, a bothy with a booking system. You are learning to ride loaded, to pack, to manage your pace. You do not also need to learn how to wild camp. One new skill at a time.

**Plan one resupply stop.** A cafe, a pub, a village shop around the halfway point of day one. This is your reset point.

**Include a bailout option.** A train station within 15 km of your route. If the weather turns biblical or a mechanical defeats your toolkit, you can get home. Not weakness. Planning.

**Leave early.** Start by 8am. Early starts buy you time for wrong turns, slow sections, and the unplanned pub stop that turns a good day into a great one.

**Pack less than you think.** Whatever you think you need for one night, you need less. One change of clothes, a multi-tool with a chain breaker, and a sandwich from the shop you passed 20 km ago.

The best thing about a first overnight trip is what it teaches you about packing. You will bring things you do not use. You will forget things you wish you had. Both lessons are more valuable than any gear list on the internet.

## Sleeping Options: From Hotels to Hedges

The sleeping question is where bikepacking spans the widest spectrum of comfort, and where your first trip should lean heavily towards the comfortable end.

### Hotels and B&Bs

No extra kit required. Ride to the door, eat dinner, sleep in a bed, ride away. A night runs $60-120 depending on location. For a first trip, the simplicity is worth every penny. You are testing whether you enjoy riding loaded. You are not testing whether you enjoy sleeping on the ground. Most small B&Bs will let you bring the bike inside if you ask.

### Hostels and Bothies

One step towards independence. Hostels offer a bed, a roof, and usually a kitchen. Bothies — unlocked mountain shelters maintained by the Mountain Bothies Association — offer a roof and four walls, nothing more. Bothies are free, first-come first-served, unheated, and located in remote places. The combination of free accommodation in stunning locations makes them irresistible to experienced bikepackers. Not ideal for trip one. Excellent for trip three.

### Bivvy Camping

A bivvy bag, a sleeping mat, and a sleeping bag. No tent. Find a sheltered spot, lay out your mat, and sleep under the sky. This is where bikepacking begins to feel properly adventurous.

A good bivvy setup weighs under 1.5 kg. An Alpkit Hunka bivvy bag ($50-80), a foam or inflatable mat ($40-150), and a lightweight sleeping bag ($100-200). Total kit cost: $200-400. The limitation is weather. A bivvy in steady rain is miserable. In dry conditions, it is wonderful.

### Tents

A one-person tent (MSR Hubba NX, Nordisk Lofoten, Naturehike Cloud Up) weighing 1-1.8 kg gives you weather protection, privacy, and a door you can zip against midges. For multi-day trips, a tent makes sense. For an overnight, it is usually overkill — unless you know you will not sleep without walls around you. Sleep quality matters more than weight savings.

### The Progression

First trip: booked accommodation. Second trip: bivvy bag or bothy. Third trip onwards: choose based on the route. Some trips call for hotels. Some call for a bivvy under a clear sky with nobody for miles. The same bag system supports all of these.

## The Kit You Do Not Need (Yet)

Every first-time bikepacker overpacks. Every single one. The result is a bike that weighs 25 kg and a rider who spends the first 20 km wondering if the frame bag zip is going to survive.

Here is what you can leave at home.

**A full toolkit.** Multi-tool, spare tube, tyre levers, mini pump, chain quick link. That is the list. Not a chain whip, cassette lockring tool, spare brake pads, and bottom bracket tool. If your mechanical requires a full workshop, you are calling a taxi anyway.

**Three changes of clothes.** You need what you are wearing and one change for the evening. Merino base layers work for two days. If you are in a B&B, wash your kit in the sink overnight.

**A stove and cooking system.** A pub for dinner. A cafe for breakfast. Bars on the bike. The stove, gas canister, pot, and freeze-dried meals add 1-1.5 kg to solve a problem you do not have. Save it for remote multi-day trips.

**Excessive electronics.** A phone, a battery pack, and a front light cover navigation, communication, and safety. You do not need a GPS computer, inReach, GoPro, and Bluetooth speaker.

**Every waterproof layer you own.** One waterproof jacket. Not waterproof trousers, overshoes, and a hat. If it rains that hard, you get wet from the waist down. Waterproof trousers on a bike are sweaty, restrictive, and almost never worth the space.

The principle: pack for one night, ride, observe. What did you use? What did you not touch? One trip gives better information than a thousand gear lists.

## Why Road Cyclists Fall in Love With This

I will tell you when it happens. Not at the start, when you are fiddling with straps. Not at the first climb, when the extra weight makes itself known. Not at the halfway cafe stop.

It happens in the last hour of day one. You are 50 km in. The light is softening. Your pace has settled into something that feels less like riding and more like breathing. The road stretches ahead into landscape you have never seen from a saddle. And you realise: you are not going home tonight. The bike is taking you somewhere you earned by turning the pedals. That feeling is different from any road ride you have ever done. It is closer to the feeling you had the first time you rode a bike as a child — the understanding that this machine could take you anywhere.

For masters cyclists who have spent years chasing a number that represents fitness but not fulfilment, that feeling is powerful. It is not about watts. It is not about Strava. It is about covering ground under your own power and sleeping somewhere you would never have found in a car.

Day two amplifies it. You wake up, pack your bags, strap them to the bike, and ride. No commute. No car. You are already there. The bike is the vehicle, the wardrobe, the kitchen, the house. The simplicity of it — after years of training plans, taper weeks, and TSS targets — is addictive.

If you are curious about where other riders are finding this, the [Roadman Cycling community on Skool](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) is a good place to start. Plenty of riders have made the same transition from pure road to mixed riding, and the conversations about first trips and kit choices are worth a read.

I am not telling you to sell the race bike. I am telling you the bike has more to offer than intervals and data. One night. One bag system. One route that leads somewhere you have never been. That is enough to know whether this is for you. The bike you already own is the right bike to start with. The fitness you already have is enough. The only thing you need to add is the willingness to go slower, carry more, and see where the road leads when you stop turning around at the halfway point.

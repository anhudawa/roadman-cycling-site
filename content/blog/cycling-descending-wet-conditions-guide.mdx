Every cyclist can descend in sunshine. You clip in, you tuck, you let gravity do the work. Wet descending is a different discipline entirely. Grip and stopping distance become less predictable, the consequences of a mistake compound at speed, and the riders who stay upright are not the bravest ones — they are the ones who leave enough margin for the surface they actually have.

Here is the thing nobody tells you: wet descending is a learnable skill. It is not about nerve. It is about physics, preparation, and a handful of specific techniques that most club riders have never been taught. I spent years being nervous on wet descents before I actually sat down and worked out what was going wrong — and almost all of it was fixable.

Let me break this down.

## Why Wet Descending Is a Different Sport

Water, road film, temperature, tyre compound, tread condition and surface texture all change available grip. That is why a single coefficient, braking-distance multiplier or wet-cornering percentage is misleading. [Shimano's road-brake safety guidance](https://si.shimano.com/en/pdfs/dm/RACBR01/DM-RACBR01-03-ENG.pdf) gives the defensible instruction: wet roads make skidding easier, so reduce speed and apply the brakes early and gently.

Put it in real terms: the dry line and braking marker you used last Sunday are not evidence that the same entry speed is safe today. Ride so you can stop inside the clear road you can see. Extend the margin again for paint, polished tarmac, metal, debris, standing water, poor visibility or an unfamiliar descent.

What most people do is descend on feel and hope for the best. What actually works is recalibrating consciously, section by section, until the wet limits become as instinctive as the dry ones.

## Braking Technique on Wet Roads

Here is where it gets really interesting, because braking in the wet is not just about braking earlier. It is about braking differently.

Do not chase a fixed front-to-rear percentage. Weight transfer, gradient, load, grip and brake system all change the useful balance. Apply both brakes smoothly, avoid locking either wheel and favour the front only to the degree the surface and gradient safely allow. [British Cycling's descending guidance](https://www.britishcycling.org.uk/knowledge/training/article/izn20180117-Get-Started-Descending---Top-10-tips-0) similarly recommends both brakes, with the front only slightly favoured unless the gradient is extreme or the surface slippery.

The other critical change is modulation. Grabbing a fistful of brake lever on a wet road is how you end up looking at the sky. Progressive braking — squeezing the levers gradually, increasing pressure smoothly, then releasing smoothly — keeps the tyres at the edge of their grip window instead of overwhelming them. You are asking the tyre how much grip it has, not demanding more than it can give.

For practice, use a traffic-free, flat area at low speed. Apply both brakes early
and progressively, stop before wheel lock and keep a large run-off margin. The
goal is smooth response and repeatability, not discovering a skid threshold.

## Rim Brakes vs Disc Brakes in the Wet

Let me be really clear about this, because the difference is massive and it directly affects how you need to ride.

Disc brakes move the braking surface away from the rim, but wet stopping still depends on tyre grip, brake condition, load, speed and terrain. [SRAM's road-brake manual](https://docs.sram.com/en-US/publications/6TmfV97fHWv8kvGXVegoTy/UM%20-%20Road%20AXS%20and%20XPLR%20AXS?1x-2x-or-xplr-drivetrain-=red-xplr-axs&installation-or-maintenance-=installation&models=rd-red-1e-e1) explicitly says wet conditions take longer to stop. Test response at low speed before the descent and avoid assuming any system is unaffected by rain.

Rim brakes act on a surface exposed to water and road grime, so initial response can be delayed while the pads clear the rim. The delay is not one fixed number of wheel rotations: rim material, pad compound, contamination and water volume all matter. Start much earlier and learn the response of your exact setup away from traffic.

With rim brakes, light early contact can help clear water before stronger braking
is required. Do not assume this restores dry performance, and do not drag the
brakes continuously; heat, wear, rim material and contamination still matter.

## Tyre Pressure Adjustments

Do not automatically drop 5 to 10 PSI. In one controlled three-tyre test, [lower test pressure increased measured wet grip](https://www.bicyclerollingresistance.com/specials/wet-grip-pressure-test), but the experiment used one wheel load, rim, surface and speed. It did not establish a safe universal reduction for every road-bike system.

Begin with the [Roadman tyre-pressure calculator](/tools/tyre-pressure), using complete system weight, measured mounted width and the surface. Then check the tyre and rim documentation: compatibility and the higher minimum or lower maximum override every calculator. If you want to compare a wet setting, change only 1-2 PSI at a time on a familiar, low-risk route. Stop lowering if the tyre squirms, bottoms, burps or feels unsupported.

Pressure is only one input. Tyre compound and condition, road contamination, entry speed, braking smoothness and line choice all affect control. Read the full [cycling tyre-pressure guide](/blog/cycling-tyre-pressure-guide) before changing a hookless or unfamiliar system.

## Line Selection: Where You Ride Changes Everything

On a public road, the safe line stays inside your lane and preserves sight distance. In the wet, choose it early enough to avoid last-second swerves around hazards.

**Avoid painted road markings.** White lines, lane markings, pedestrian crossings and cycle-lane paint can be especially slippery when wet. If you must cross one, do it upright and in a straight line without abrupt braking or steering.

**Avoid manhole covers and metal grates.** They can be much more slippery than surrounding tarmac when wet. Plan your line around them. If you cannot avoid one, cross it upright and straight without abrupt braking or turning.

**Scan for contamination.** Oil, diesel, leaves, grit and rubber residue can create a greasy film. Look for discolouration or an iridescent sheen, but do not make a sudden line change or move into an unsafe traffic position to avoid it.

**Read the water.** Running water reveals camber and drainage, while standing water can hide potholes and debris. Avoid it where you can without swerving into traffic, slow before you reach it and keep the bike upright with smooth inputs.

## Reading Road Surfaces

Not all tarmac is equal. Texture, polish, repairs, drainage and contamination
change grip, and visual appearance cannot give a precise coefficient. Treat shiny,
patched or poorly drained surfaces cautiously and preserve room to change the plan.

Camber changes catch riders out because the road angle shifts mid-corner. A corner that cambers inward (helping you) might transition to a negative camber section (working against you) halfway through. In the dry, you barely notice. In the wet, that change can break traction without warning. Read the road ten to fifteen metres ahead, not two metres ahead.

## Rain After a Dry Spell

Rain after a dry spell can lift oil, rubber particles and dust into a slick surface film. Treat that transition cautiously, but do not rely on a 20-minute countdown.

During dry weather, contamination accumulates on the surface and can mix with new rain. It does not wash away on a universal schedule: rain rate, drainage, traffic and the type of spill all matter, and a diesel patch can remain hazardous long after steady rain. Reduce speed, avoid abrupt inputs and keep the larger safety margin whenever the road looks wet, dirty or iridescent.

## Cornering in the Wet

Cornering is where most wet-weather crashes happen, and they happen for one specific reason: the rider asked for more grip than the tyre had available.

Here is how to corner in the wet without finding the limit the hard way.

**Choose a smooth line inside your lane.** A gentler arc can reduce lean demand, but never cross the centre line, compromise sight distance or move into traffic to create it. Enter slower so you have options.

**Reduce lean demand.** Keep the inside pedal up, weight the outside foot and avoid sudden steering corrections. Entry speed and line choice determine how much lean the corner requires.

**Weight through the outside pedal.** Outside foot at six o'clock, pressing hard. This drives the tyre into the road at the contact patch, maximising grip and lowering your centre of gravity.

**Reduce braking as lean increases.** Braking and cornering share the available
grip. Do most braking while upright, release smoothly as you turn and avoid abrupt
lever inputs. Never choose to run wide into traffic or a barrier to avoid braking.

**Smooth inputs only.** No sudden steering corrections, no sharp braking, no aggressive pedal strokes. Think of riding on glass. That mental model produces the right behaviour.

## Body Position

Where you sit on the bike changes what happens when things go wrong.

**Hands in the drops.** More leverage on the brakes and a lower centre of gravity. Both matter in the wet.

**Weight back.** Shift towards the rear of the saddle. This loads the rear tyre, improving grip, and reduces the load on the front tyre during braking.

**Elbows bent.** Your arms are shock absorbers. Locked elbows transmit every road imperfection to the front wheel, which can break traction on a rough wet surface. Bent elbows absorb those impacts.

**Relaxed grip.** Counterintuitive when you are nervous, but a death grip on the bars makes everything worse. Tight forearms transmit tension to the steering, making your inputs jerky. Consciously relax your hands. If you notice yourself gripping harder, shake out one hand at a time on the straights.

## Speed Management

The principle is simple but most riders get it backwards. You manage speed on the straights so you do not have to manage it in the corners.

On a long descent, use the straight sections to scrub speed with gentle, progressive braking. Get down to a speed that you are confident you can carry through the next corner without touching the brakes. Then release the brakes before you tip into the turn and roll through on a clean, unbraked line.

This is the opposite of what nervous descenders do. Nervous descenders carry too much speed into corners, panic-brake mid-corner, and crash. The fix is not more bravery — it is better planning. Slower in, smoother through, safer out.

On a long sustained descent, manage heat as specified by the brake, pad and wheel
manufacturers. Use early controlled applications and safe stopping places rather
than relying on one alternating or constant-drag technique for every system. Stop
if braking changes, overheats or becomes unreliable.

## Building Confidence

Confidence in wet descending comes from one place: time spent descending in the wet. There is no shortcut. But there is a smart sequence.

Start on a quiet, flat or gently sloping practice area rather than using a live descent as a grip test. At low speed, learn how early both brakes need to be applied and how smoothly the bike changes direction. Transfer one skill at a time to familiar descents while leaving a generous margin; never practise by deliberately finding the skid limit on a public road.

Coached practice can help, but do not copy another rider's speed or line without
your own sight distance and safety margin. Give yourself permission to be slow;
a time gap is always expendable.

## Kit That Makes a Difference

A few specific items that improve your safety and comfort on wet descents.

**Clear or yellow-tinted glasses.** Dark lenses reduce visibility in already low-light conditions. Clear lenses protect your eyes from spray without dimming your vision. Yellow tints improve contrast, making road surface details easier to read. I find the eye protection worth it — a piece of grit in your eye at 50 kph on a wet descent is a problem you do not need.

**Mudguards for group rides.** Not for you — for the person behind you. Rear spray without mudguards blinds the following rider. Fit a clip-on rear guard at minimum. Basic etiquette.

**Shoe covers.** Wet cleats on wet pedals reduce the security of the connection between you and the bike. Neoprene covers keep your cleats drier and maintain that positive engagement.

**A cap under your helmet.** Keeps rain out of your eyes, channels water away from your face. A small thing that makes a significant difference.

## The Mental Approach

Here is the part most guides skip, and it might be the most important section in this entire article.

Accept that you will be slower. Not might be. Will be. There is no universal percentage, so ignore the Strava comparison and choose a speed that preserves stopping distance and line options on the road in front of you.

Once you accept the speed reduction, everything else follows. You stop trying to be fast and start trying to be smooth: smooth braking, smooth steering and smooth weight transfers, with enough margin to respond to the next hazard.

Focus on process, not outcome. Your attention should be on the next 50 metres of road, the next corner entry, the next braking zone. Not on the average speed readout. Not on the gap to the rider ahead. When your attention narrows to the immediate task, the anxiety drops because your brain is solving problems instead of imagining catastrophes.

Progress is not linear. Record which conditions and skills felt controlled, then
repeat only when the practice area and weather remain suitable.

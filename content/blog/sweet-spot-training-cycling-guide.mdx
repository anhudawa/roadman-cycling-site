Sweet spot training is a **cycling coaching convention commonly set at 88–94% of functional threshold power (FTP)**. It is used to prescribe sustained work below or around the lower edge of threshold while accumulating more controlled time than many riders can manage at FTP.

That definition is useful. The mythology built around it is not.

Sweet spot is not a separately discovered physiological zone, it does not deliver a proven “90% of threshold's benefit”, and no fixed number of sessions guarantees an FTP increase. The band is a starting prescription. The rider's test method, training history, power-duration profile and response determine whether the session is appropriate.

## What percentage of FTP is sweet spot?

[TrainingPeaks describes sweet spot as 88–94% FTP](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/how-to-get-started-training-with-power/). In the same power framework, tempo is listed as 76–90% and threshold as 91–105%. Sweet spot therefore overlaps two named levels rather than occupying a separate level of its own.

For a 250-watt FTP, 88–94% displays as 220–235 watts. That arithmetic is exact; the physiology is not.

FTP depends on how it was estimated. A 20-minute test, sustained effort, ramp-test estimate and modelled value can produce different anchors. A [scoping review of the FTP20 test](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34304689/) found it can be reliable, while comparisons with physiological markers and other power concepts showed large limits of agreement. The authors also noted that the evidence was limited and concentrated in trained men.

Keep the test method attached to the number. Ninety per cent of an inflated FTP can become threshold work; 90% of an underestimated FTP can become tempo. A narrow percentage band cannot repair a poor anchor.

## What sweet spot is—and is not

Sweet spot describes an external power target. It does not directly measure lactate, oxygen uptake, muscle-fibre recruitment, carbohydrate use or recovery cost.

Those internal responses change continuously with intensity and vary between cyclists. They are also affected by duration, prior work, heat, fuelling, sleep, training status and the accuracy of the FTP value. Crossing from 90 to 91% FTP does not flip a biological switch.

This distinction matters because four claims are often repeated as if they were settled facts:

1. **“Sweet spot produces 90% of threshold's adaptation.”** No direct body of trials establishes that universal ratio.
2. **“It creates much less fatigue.”** A lower target can reduce the cost of a comparable effort, but fatigue depends on total work, session design and the rider—not the label alone.
3. **“It is the best training for anyone with limited time.”** That is a plausible coaching use, not a universal research finding.
4. **“Two or three sessions per week is optimal.”** The literature does not provide one frequency for every cyclist.

A useful intensity does not need exaggerated certainty. Sweet spot earns its place when it solves a clear training problem and the rider can absorb the dose.

## Does sweet spot training work?

Structured cycling training works; the narrower question is whether one distribution or one exact band is consistently best.

A [2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of trained cyclists](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39788807/) included 41 studies, 81 training groups and 797 participants. Training improved VO2max and time-trial performance, but the review found no significant difference between polarised and non-polarised categories. Longer interventions were associated with larger improvements; adding volume beyond a necessary amount was not associated with further gains in that analysis.

An [individual-participant network meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/) likewise found no statistically significant overall differences between polarised and other training-intensity distributions. Its subgroup result suggested competitive and recreational athletes may respond differently. An earlier [randomised-trial meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863593/) favoured polarised over threshold-heavy training for time-trial performance, but included only four studies and rated their methodological quality low.

These reviews do not prove sweet spot ineffective. They show why “sweet spot always wins” and “sweet spot is junk” are both stronger than the evidence. Research usually tests complete programmes, not an isolated 88–94% band, and the category called threshold or non-polarised is not identical to a modern sweet-spot session.

The honest conclusion: sweet spot can be a productive part of training, but its effect belongs to the whole programme.

## How to do a sweet spot cycling session

Start with the purpose, not the famous workout.

If the goal is controlled sub-threshold work, choose an interval length that lets the rider keep power, breathing and technique stable across the complete set. For a cyclist new to structured work, that might mean three repetitions of eight to ten minutes near the lower end of the band. A rider with recent evidence of longer sustained work may choose longer repetitions.

These are examples, not minimum effective doses:

| Starting point  | Example main set                                    | Use it when                                                             | Stop or adjust when                                      |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Familiarisation | 3 × 8 min at 88–90%, easy recovery                  | The rider is new to the band or returning to structure                  | Power fades early or RPE rises disproportionately        |
| Accumulation    | 3 × 12 min at a controlled target                   | The shorter set is repeatable and the next normal session is unaffected | The final interval no longer resembles the first         |
| Longer blocks   | 2 × 20 min at a rider-specific sub-threshold target | Recent training supports sustained, even work                           | The target becomes a threshold test rather than training |

Recovery between repetitions should be long enough to restore control for the next planned effort. There is no prize for making recovery artificially short. The session is successful when it delivers its intended work, not when the rider survives the most punishing version.

### Progress time before power

Hold the chosen target steady and extend total controlled time in small steps. When the rider completes the set with even pacing and returns to normal training as expected, add time to one repetition or add a short repetition. Do not simultaneously increase target, interval length, frequency and weekly volume.

Progress is not mandatory every week. Repeat the same session when conditions differ or when the previous response was uncertain. Reduce it after illness, a hard race, travel, poor sleep or an unexplained performance change.

The [interval builder](/tools/interval-builder) can turn the intended work into a session. The [FTP zones guide](/blog/ftp-training-zones-cycling-complete-guide) explains why the test and zone model must stay attached to the prescription.

## How many sweet spot sessions per week?

There is no universal answer.

A [systematic review of periodisation in trained road cyclists](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36640771/) found only seven eligible studies and no evidence favouring one periodisation model over eight to twelve weeks. That is not a foundation for claiming that every rider needs two sessions, that three is a ceiling, or that one cannot work.

Count the whole week before adding sweet spot:

- races and hard group rides;
- threshold or VO2max intervals;
- demanding strength training or another sport;
- recent training volume and experience;
- sleep, illness, heat, travel and life stress; and
- the event's actual demands.

For a rider without another demanding cycling day, one controlled sweet-spot session is a conservative place to start. A second may fit when the first remains repeatable, normal endurance riding stays normal and recovery indicators remain stable. That is a coaching progression, not a universal prescription.

Persistent chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, palpitations or symptoms that concern the rider are not training-plan problems to solve by lowering FTP. Stop and seek appropriate medical assessment.

## Sweet spot vs Zone 2, threshold and polarised training

These terms do different jobs:

- **Zone 2** is a lower-intensity range commonly used to accumulate volume at a lower cost. It is not interchangeable with sweet spot. See [sweet spot vs Zone 2](/compare/sweet-spot-vs-zone-2).
- **Threshold training** targets sustained work nearer FTP. It is usually more specific when the goal requires riding close to threshold, but the exact cost depends on session design and the athlete. See [sweet spot vs threshold](/compare/sweet-spot-vs-threshold).
- **Polarised training** describes how training is distributed across low and high intensity, not one workout. Its comparisons with other distributions are more nuanced than “80/20 always wins.” See [polarised vs sweet spot](/blog/polarised-vs-sweet-spot-training).
- **VO2max intervals** target a different job above threshold. They are not automatically required in the same week as sweet spot. See the [VO2max interval guide](/blog/cycling-vo2max-intervals).

Choose from the rider's current limiter and event demands. A cyclist preparing for repeated short climbs needs a different mix from a rider preparing for one sustained ascent. A time-crunched schedule changes what is feasible, but it does not make one intensity biologically mandatory.

## Sweet spot for time-crunched cyclists

Sweet spot is attractive when a cyclist has enough time for a focused interval session but not enough for large training volume. It can create a clear, measurable quality day without requiring the rider to turn every short ride into hard work.

The mistake is replacing all low-intensity riding with sweet spot because each hour feels more “productive.” That changes both the stimulus and recovery cost of the week. It may also leave the rider too tired to execute truly high-intensity or event-specific work.

Protect contrast in the programme: quality days should have a defined purpose; other riding should be as easy as its purpose requires. If a commute, club ride and weekend event already supply moderate-to-hard work, an extra sweet-spot day may solve no problem at all.

## Sweet spot training for masters cyclists

“Over 40” is a search category, not a recovery prescription.

A [review of masters-athlete recovery](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25880787/) concluded that research was limited, suggested a possible age effect and warned that reduced physical activity is difficult to separate from ageing. A small study of trained cyclists and triathletes found [similar performance and recovery responses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35050974/) between its younger and older groups after short HIIT protocols. Neither source validates a fixed 48- or 72-hour rule for sweet spot.

Masters riders should still individualise the decision, with particular attention to:

- recent comparable training rather than age alone;
- the combined cost of cycling, strength and life stress;
- sleep, soreness, motivation and normal power trends;
- health conditions and medication that affect exercise response; and
- whether the planned event rewards sustained sub-threshold work.

If the next demanding session repeatedly underperforms, separate the sessions further, reduce work duration or remove one hard day. If the rider recovers well, do not manufacture a problem from a birthday.

## Indoor vs outdoor sweet spot

Both can work.

Indoors offers uninterrupted power and easy control of recovery periods. Strong airflow, adequate fluids and an FTP that reflects indoor conditions matter because heat can raise internal strain. ERG mode can help hold a target, but resistance mode may better reveal natural pacing and lets the rider reduce power without fighting the trainer.

Outdoors can better match event position, cadence and terrain. Use a route without junctions, traffic conflicts or technical descents during the work. Do not stare at the head unit at the expense of bike control.

The better environment is the one that safely delivers the intended session with interpretable data.

## The Roadman decision rule

Use sweet spot when sustained sub-threshold work solves a specific problem. Start from a current FTP, choose a repeatable dose and progress time before power. Judge the workout by the whole set and the next normal training day—not by one heroic average.

Change the approach when the rider cannot recover, when the target event demands another quality, or when repeatable performance has stopped progressing. No training intensity deserves permanent ownership of the week.

Sweet spot is a useful tool. The trusted version is narrower and more honest than the marketing: **88–94% FTP is a convention, the percentage is not the athlete, and the best dose is the one the rider can execute, absorb and connect to the goal.**

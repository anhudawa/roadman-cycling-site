Here's the thing nobody tells you about the RBC GranFondo Whistler. It is not the total climbing that breaks people. Eighteen hundred metres of elevation across 122 kilometres — that is less than an Alpine sportive and well within the range of any well-prepared amateur. What breaks people is that the climbing never stops. There is no recovery valley. There is no long descent where you coast, eat, drink, and let your legs come back to life. You leave Stanley Park at sea level, and you ride uphill — mostly gently, sometimes not — for the next four to six hours until you arrive in Whistler Village at roughly 650 metres. The Sea-to-Sky Highway is one sustained effort dressed up as a gran fondo. And the riders who understand that ride it completely differently from the riders who don't.

Five to seven thousand riders line up every September, making it one of the largest gran fondos in North America. Fully closed roads on Highway 99. A route that follows the British Columbia coastline before turning inland through some of the most spectacular scenery you will ride anywhere — ocean, coastal rainforest, granite walls, and the Coast Mountains filling the horizon. But it is also a 4-6 hour day on gradients from flat to 7 per cent, in weather that could be anything from late-summer sunshine to cold Pacific Northwest rain, with no meaningful downhill until you are done.

The good news: this is entirely fixable. The fitness requirement is straightforward, the pacing strategy is simple (though not easy to execute), and the training is more about discipline than talent. Let me break this down.

## Key Takeaways

- The 122km route from Vancouver to Whistler gains approximately 1,800m of elevation — but the profile is one long, sustained climb, not discrete efforts with valleys, making pacing the single most important skill for this event.
- Target 2.5-3.0 W/kg for a comfortable finish — your ability to hold 65-75 per cent of FTP for 4-5 hours on a gradual uphill is the defining fitness requirement, not peak climbing power.
- A 12-week preparation block building to 10-12 hours per week is appropriate — the climbing is sustained but moderate in gradient, so the preparation centres on endurance and pacing discipline.
- The first 30km out of Vancouver is the trap — it feels flat, the group is together, the adrenaline is high, and riders who average 32-35 km/h here arrive at the sustained climb with 15-20 per cent less energy than riders who sat in at 28-30 km/h.
- Pacific Northwest weather can deliver anything from 25-degree sunshine to 8-degree rain — September in British Columbia is genuinely unpredictable, and you need clothing options for both extremes.
- Nutrition across 4-6 hours of sustained uphill riding requires 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour — the constant low-grade effort burns glycogen steadily and a bonk on the final 30km with no downhill to coast on is a very long walk.
- The Forte route at 55km is a legitimate option — it covers the most scenic section and finishes with a genuine sense of accomplishment, particularly for riders in their first gran fondo season.

## Course Profile and Key Sections

The Sea-to-Sky Highway is not a series of climbs. It is one long, gradual ramp from sea level to 650 metres. Understanding each section is the difference between pacing well and arriving in Whistler empty.

### Stanley Park Start and the Vancouver Waterfront

The first 10-15km roll through West Vancouver and along the coast. Flat to gently undulating. Five to seven thousand riders. Adrenaline everywhere. The pelotons will be flying at 32-38 km/h, and you will feel strong because you are fresh and surrounded by the energy of a mass start.

This is where the race is lost, not won. The energy you spend here — on the front, chasing gaps, riding above your target — will not be available in four hours when the road tilts upward and stays there. Sit in. Draft. Eat early. Save everything.

### The Coast to Horseshoe Bay and the Squamish Transition

The route follows the coast north through West Vancouver and Horseshoe Bay — the Pacific Ocean on one side, coastal mountains on the other. Still relatively flat, still fast in the bunch. Headwinds are possible along this stretch, and the riders who sat in the group will be grateful while the riders who were on the front will be doing maths they do not like.

Between Horseshoe Bay and Squamish, the road begins to tilt. The gradient is modest — perhaps 2-3 per cent average — but this is the first sustained uphill effort of the day. The groups fragment here. Riders who went too hard in the first 30km start drifting backward. The riders who paced correctly start moving through the field without changing their effort.

Squamish sits at roughly the halfway point. You should feel genuinely comfortable arriving here — not fresh, but comfortable. If you feel tired in Squamish, the second half will be hard.

### The Sustained Climb from Squamish to Whistler — The Crux

Let me be really clear about this: the stretch from Squamish to Whistler is the event. Everything before it is prologue.

Approximately 60 kilometres of gradual but relentless uphill. The average gradient is 3-5 per cent, which sounds gentle, but 60 kilometres of 3-5 per cent with no flat or downhill relief is a completely different physiological demand from rolling terrain with the same average gradient. Your legs never get a chance to spin freely. You never coast. There is no recovery. The effort is constant, moderate, and cumulative — and the riders who blow up here almost always blow up because of what they did in the first half, not because the climbing is beyond them.

The gradient kicks up in places — sections of 5-7 per cent that feel significantly harder after three or four hours of sustained effort. Not long, but frequent enough to catch tired riders.

Here's where it gets really interesting: the psychological challenge of this section is underestimated by almost everyone. In a sportive with distinct climbs, you can see the top and you know the descent is coming. On the Sea-to-Sky Highway, the road just keeps going up. There is no summit on the horizon. The riders who handle it best are the riders who stop looking ahead and focus on the metre they are riding now.

### Arrival in Whistler Village

The final kilometres flatten slightly as you approach Whistler Village. This is where you feel the accumulation of the day — your legs have been working continuously for 4-6 hours and the sudden reduction in gradient can actually feel strange. Enjoy it. You have earned it.

Let me be really clear: the W/kg number that matters for this event is not your 20-minute test result on fresh legs. It is what you can hold for 4-5 hours on a gradual uphill. For most riders, that is 65-75 per cent of their tested FTP. If your FTP is 3.0 W/kg, your sustainable output for this event is closer to 2.0-2.3 W/kg. ## 12-Week Training Plan Overview

Twelve weeks is the right timeframe for this event, assuming you are currently riding 6-8 hours per week and can handle a 3-hour ride. If you are starting from less than that, add 3-4 weeks of base riding before week one.

### Phase 1: Aerobic Foundation (Weeks 1-3)

**Volume:** 7-9 hours per week. Four to five rides. Eighty per cent in Zone 2.

Build the aerobic engine that sustains you for 4-6 hours on the Sea-to-Sky Highway. Steady riding below 75 per cent of FTP — properly easy, conversational pace. One long ride of 3-3.5 hours on the weekend. Two midweek rides of 60-90 minutes. One optional recovery spin.

Include 20-30 minutes of core work three times per week: planks (front and side, 3 x 45 seconds), dead bugs (3 x 10 each side), and single-leg glute bridges (3 x 12 each side). Four to six hours of sustained climbing loads the core and lower back in a way that flat riding does not. Build the support structure now.

Begin gut training: practise eating 40-60g of carbohydrate per hour during your long ride. Your gut is trainable — start lower and build toward 60-80g per hour over the twelve weeks.

### Phase 2: Sustained Climbing (Weeks 4-7)

**Volume:** 9-11 hours per week. Four to five rides. Introduce sustained tempo work.

This is where the training becomes specific to the Sea-to-Sky Highway. Two quality sessions per week: one sustained tempo or sweet-spot effort on gradient (or simulated gradient on a turbo), one set of longer threshold intervals. The long ride extends to 4-4.5 hours and should include at least 60-90 minutes of sustained uphill riding if your terrain allows it.

The key adaptation here is the ability to hold moderate power on a gradient for extended periods without spiking. This is not about peak power. It is about consistency.

Core work continues. Add bird-dogs (3 x 10 each side) and increase plank holds to 60 seconds.

### Phase 3: Event Simulation (Weeks 8-10)

**Volume:** 10-12 hours per week. This is peak training load.

One long ride of 5-6 hours that simulates the event profile — sustained climbing at 65-75 per cent of FTP for the majority of the ride. If you can find a 2-3 hour sustained climb in your area, ride it. If not, use a turbo trainer at 3-4 per cent gradient for 60-90 minutes within a longer ride.

One midweek session targets sustained effort at FTP or slightly below for 2 x 20-25 minutes. One session practises the pacing discipline described below.

Nutrition strategy should be fully rehearsed by now: 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour, every hour, from the start. The [Fuel Planner](/tools/fuel-planner) will give you exact targets.

### Phase 4: Taper (Weeks 11-12)

**Volume drops to 60-70 per cent of peak.** Intensity stays. Duration drops.

The fitness is banked. Now you absorb it. Ride four times per week, keep two quality sessions at normal intensity but cut duration by 30-40 per cent. The long ride drops to 2.5-3 hours. Recovery rides fill the gaps.

Sleep eight hours a night. Eat well. Arrive at Stanley Park feeling restless, not tired. If you feel like you should be doing more, the taper is working.

## Key Sessions

These are the four sessions that matter most for the GranFondo Whistler.

### The Sustained Climb Simulation

Find a gradient of 3-5 per cent and ride it for 45-60 minutes at 70-80 per cent of FTP. No sustained climb nearby? Set a turbo trainer to 3-4 per cent gradient for the same duration. The effort should feel moderate and sustainable — not easy, but nowhere near the point where you start bargaining with yourself. Build from 30 minutes in week 4 to 60 minutes by week 9. This is the most event-specific session you can do.

### The Long Endurance Ride

Five to six hours with as much climbing as your terrain allows. Pace the first two hours at 60-65 per cent of FTP — deliberately easy. Ride the final two to three hours at 65-75 per cent of FTP. This mirrors the event: conservative start, sustained effort in the second half.

Practise your full nutrition plan on every long ride. Same food, same timing, same quantities you will use on event day.

### The Pacing Discipline Session

This one is simple but harder than it sounds. Go out for a 90-minute ride. For the first 60 minutes, ride at 10 per cent below your target event power — deliberately, consciously, uncomfortably easy. Then ride the final 30 minutes at your target event power. The purpose is to practise the discipline of going slower than you want to in the first half. If you can do this in training, you can do it when five thousand riders are pushing the pace out of Stanley Park.

### The Tempo Block

Two blocks of 20-25 minutes at 85-90 per cent of FTP with 5 minutes of recovery between them. This builds the sustained power that carries you through the sections of 5-7 per cent gradient between Squamish and Whistler. Not a race effort — a controlled, steady push.

## Nutrition Strategy

A 75kg rider finishing the full route in 5 hours at an average of 200 watts will burn approximately 3,600 calories. Even with aggressive on-bike fuelling, you are running a significant energy deficit by the finish. The riders who bonk in the final 30km to Whistler are almost always the riders who under-fuelled in the first three hours.

Target 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour from the start — not from when the climbing begins, from the start. That means eating in the first 30 minutes when you feel great and think you do not need it. Practically, one gel or half an energy bar every 20-25 minutes, plus 500-750ml of fluid per hour with electrolytes. Use the [Fuel Planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to set your exact per-hour targets based on bodyweight and ride intensity.

Here's where it gets really interesting: the sustained uphill nature of this event means you never coast. On a rolling sportive, you eat and drink on the descents. On the Sea-to-Sky Highway, there are no descents. You have to practise eating while riding at moderate effort on a gradient — it is a specific skill. Carry more than you think you need. The feed stations are adequate but the queues can be long. Self-sufficiency is the safest approach.

## Equipment and Gear

**Gearing.** A compact crankset (50/34) with an 11-28 or 11-30 cassette is sufficient for most riders. The gradients are moderate — 3-5 per cent with occasional 5-7 per cent — so you do not need mountain gearing. If you are under 2.5 W/kg, an 11-32 cassette provides a useful safety net for the steeper sections after four hours of riding. Check the [FTP Zone Calculator](/tools/ftp-zones) to confirm what power you can sustain and match your gearing to the gradient.

**Clothing.** This is where the Pacific Northwest demands respect. Pack for both extremes:
- **Warm and dry forecast:** Arm warmers in a pocket. A lightweight gilet. You may not need them, but the temperature drops noticeably as you climb toward Whistler.
- **Cool or wet forecast:** A packable rain jacket is non-negotiable. Arm warmers. A merino base layer under your jersey. Overshoes if rain is expected. Getting wet at kilometre 80 with 40km of climbing ahead and no shelter is miserable.

Whatever the forecast says on Thursday, check it again on Saturday night. September in British Columbia changes its mind.

**Tyres.** The Sea-to-Sky Highway is well-surfaced tarmac. Run 25-28mm tyres at sensible pressures — around 80-90 PSI for a 75kg rider on 25mm, lower for wider tyres. Take extra caution on painted road markings in wet conditions.

## Race Day Pacing

Let me break this down into three phases, because getting this right is worth more than any training session.

**Phase 1: Vancouver to Horseshoe Bay (first 30km).** Sit in the bunch. Draft. Do not lead. Do not chase. If your target event power is 200 watts, ride this section at 170-180 watts. Riders will pass you. It will feel too easy. That is correct. They are spending 15-20 per cent more energy than you, and they will pay for it between Squamish and Whistler.

**Phase 2: Horseshoe Bay to Squamish (km 30-60).** Settle into your rhythm. The road tilts upward. The groups thin out. Find riders going your pace and work with them. Target 65-70 per cent of FTP. Eat and drink on schedule. If you feel good arriving in Squamish, you are on plan.

**Phase 3: Squamish to Whistler (km 60-122).** This is the event. Sixty kilometres of sustained uphill. Target 65-75 per cent of FTP and hold it steady. Do not surge on the steeper sections — ride them at the same perceived effort, even if your speed drops. Keep the power number steady and let the speed be whatever it is.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Going too hard in the first 30km.** The single most common mistake. The flat coastal section out of Vancouver flatters your legs — you feel strong because you are fresh and the road is easy. Then it starts climbing and never stops. Everything you spent in the first hour is gone.

2. **Treating it like a hilly sportive.** The Sea-to-Sky Highway is not a series of climbs with valleys. It is one sustained effort. Riders who plan their effort around "this climb, then recover" find that the recovery never arrives. Plan for continuous moderate output.

3. **Under-fuelling in the first half.** You do not feel hungry when you are fresh and riding at moderate effort. You will feel hungry at kilometre 90, and by then it is too late. Sixty to eighty grams of carbohydrate per hour from kilometre one. No exceptions.

4. **Ignoring the weather.** September in British Columbia is not September in southern Europe. Cold rain can arrive mid-ride. Riders without a rain jacket who get caught at kilometre 70 face 50km of climbing in the wet with dropping body temperature. Carry layers.

5. **Skipping the Forte because of ego.** The Forte at 55km covers the most dramatic section of the Sea-to-Sky Highway. If this is your first gran fondo season, or your longest ride is under four hours, the Forte is the smart choice. Come back for the full route next year with a year of riding in your legs.

## Travel and Logistics

**Getting there.** Vancouver International Airport (YVR) is a major hub with direct flights from across North America. The city is easy to get around by transit. Most airlines charge CAD$50-100 each way for a bike bag.

**Race morning.** The start is in Stanley Park — central Vancouver, well served by transit and hotels. Arrive 60-90 minutes before your start wave. Gear bags are transported to Whistler by the organisers so you can collect warm, dry clothes at the finish.

**Entry.** Approximately CAD$175-250 depending on registration timing. The event sells out, so do not wait.

**Accommodation.** Whistler Village books quickly for the event weekend. Book early if you want to stay at the finish line, or stay in Vancouver and take the return shuttle.

## Recovery Plan

You have just ridden for 4-6 hours on a sustained uphill with no real recovery sections. Your legs will be more depleted than they feel at the finish, because the constant moderate effort drains glycogen without the acute muscle damage of steep, hard climbs.

**Immediately after:** Eat a proper meal with carbohydrate and protein within 30 minutes. Whistler Village has restaurants everywhere — take advantage. Aim for 1-1.5g of carbohydrate per kilogram of bodyweight and 30-40g of protein.

**Days one to four:** Walk, stretch, easy spin if you have access to a bike. The sustained nature of this event means the fatigue is more systemic than muscular — you may feel generally tired rather than specifically sore. Light spinning or complete rest, your call.

**Day five onward:** Resume easy riding. Full training resumes after 7-10 days.

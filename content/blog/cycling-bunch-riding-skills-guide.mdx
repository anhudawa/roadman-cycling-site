## The Fear Is Real. The Fear Is Also Useful.

You've been riding for months. Maybe years. You can hold 200 watts for two hours solo. You've climbed every hill in the county. You own the bike, the kit, the shoes, the sunglasses. And yet the thought of riding in a group of twenty riders at 35kph with wheels inches from your own makes your stomach tighten.

That fear is not weakness. It is the correct response to an objectively dangerous situation.

Riding in close proximity to other humans on two-wheeled machines with no crumple zones, no seatbelts, and no ABS is objectively risky. Your brain knows this. The flinch response when a wheel appears beside yours, the tension in your shoulders when the bunch tightens on a descent, the urge to brake when the rider ahead stands up — these are your nervous system doing exactly what it was designed to do.

The problem is not the fear. The problem is that untrained fear makes you rigid. Rigid riders are unpredictable riders. Unpredictable riders crash.

So the goal is not to kill the fear. The goal is to convert it from panic into awareness. From a flinch into a read. From braking into anticipating. That conversion is a skill, and like every skill in cycling, it can be trained.

## What Happens When You Follow a Wheel

Before we talk about confidence, we need to talk about physics. Because understanding what's actually happening in the air between you and the rider ahead changes how you think about distance.

Bert Blocken's wind tunnel research at Eindhoven University of Technology quantified what every bunch rider already felt: sitting behind another rider saves energy. Dramatically. At a following distance of about one metre, you save roughly 27% of the energy you'd spend riding alone at the same speed. Close that gap to half a metre and the savings climb towards 35-40%.

But here's the part most people miss. Those savings change your braking equation.

At 35kph with a following distance of 50cm, you have approximately 0.5 seconds before contact if the rider ahead brakes hard. Half a second. That's less time than it takes to say the word "brake." Your reaction time alone — the gap between seeing the deceleration and your hands actually squeezing the levers — is about 0.2-0.3 seconds in ideal conditions. That leaves you 0.2-0.3 seconds of actual braking. At 35kph, that's barely enough.

This is why bunch riders don't brake the way solo riders brake. In a bunch, you modulate. You feather. You scrub speed with air resistance by sitting up slightly. You use the gap like a buffer, letting it compress and expand naturally. Hard braking in a bunch is a last resort, not a first response.

Understanding this changes your relationship with the wheel in front. You're not ignoring risk by riding close. You're managing it differently. You're replacing reaction with anticipation.

## Overlapping Wheels: The One Rule That Prevents Most Crashes

If you learn nothing else from this post, learn this: never overlap wheels.

Overlapping happens when your front wheel moves alongside the rear wheel of the rider ahead. Your front axle sits level with or ahead of their rear axle. From above, the wheels overlap like two coins placed side by side.

It looks harmless. It is the single most common cause of crashes in bunch riding.

Here's why. When your wheel is directly behind the rider ahead, their movements don't affect your steering. They can drift left or right by a few centimetres and nothing happens to you. But the moment your front wheel sits beside their rear wheel, any lateral movement from them pushes your front wheel sideways. Your bars turn. You have zero time to correct. You're on the ground before you've processed what happened.

The rider ahead doesn't even know they've taken you out. A slight drift to avoid a crack in the road surface. A natural sway as they stand on a climb. A centimetre of movement that wouldn't register if you were behind them. But beside them, that centimetre redirects your front wheel and puts you into the tarmac.

Three rules to prevent it:

**Stay square behind the wheel.** Your front wheel should point at the hub of the rider ahead. Not to their left. Not to their right. Dead centre.

**Watch your line in crosswinds.** When the wind comes from the side, the natural draft position shifts diagonally. You'll instinctively move to the leeward side of the rider ahead, which is correct for shelter but dangerous for overlap. Offset to the sheltered side, but keep your front wheel behind their rear wheel. Diagonal shelter, not parallel positioning.

**Moving past, move past decisively.** If you need to pass someone — they're slowing, you're moving up — don't linger beside them. Commit. Accelerate smoothly past until your rear wheel is clear of their front wheel. The danger zone is the transition, so spend as little time in it as possible.

## Reading the Bunch

Nervous riders fixate on the wheel directly in front. It's instinct. The closest threat gets the most attention. But it's the wrong strategy, because that wheel only tells you what's happening right now. By the time the rider ahead brakes, it's already too late for you to respond smoothly.

Experienced bunch riders look through the group, not at it.

### Where to Look

Your primary focus should be three or four riders ahead. Not their wheels — their upper bodies. Shoulders, heads, torsos. These tell you what's coming before the wheels react.

A rider about to brake sits up slightly. Their shoulders rise. Their elbows bend inward. You'll see this ripple through the riders ahead of you like a wave, and that wave gives you time. Two seconds of warning instead of half a second. That's the difference between feathering your brakes and grabbing them.

Use your peripheral vision for the wheel directly ahead. You don't need to stare at it. You need to register changes in gap distance. Your peripheral system is better at detecting motion changes than your central vision anyway. Let it do its job.

Every few seconds, flick your eyes to the road surface 20-30 metres ahead. The group will react to potholes, drain covers, gravel patches, and parked cars. If you can see these hazards before the bunch reaches them, you can position yourself early rather than swerving late.

### Reading Body Language

Riders broadcast their intentions constantly. Most of them don't realise they're doing it.

A rider who's about to accelerate shifts their weight forward. Their hands move to the drops or the hoods tighten. Their cadence drops slightly as they prepare to shift gear and push. You'll see this a full second before the pace changes.

A rider who's struggling does the opposite. Shoulders creep upward towards the ears. The bike starts to sway side to side as they recruit upper body muscles to maintain power. Their gap to the rider ahead opens and closes unevenly — they surge to close it, then drift back, then surge again. This accordion pattern is a warning sign. That rider is about to crack, and when they do, they'll either sit up suddenly or drift sideways. Be ready to move around them.

A rider who's about to stand on the pedals shifts their grip wider on the hoods. Their weight moves back slightly before the explosive forward motion of standing. If you're on their wheel, this matters — they'll decelerate briefly as they transition from seated to standing, even if they're about to accelerate overall. That momentary slowdown catches riders out. Expect it.

### Reading the Road

The bunch doesn't ride in a vacuum. The road shapes its behaviour.

Approaching a roundabout, the bunch compresses. Riders tighten up, gaps close, and the pace drops. Position yourself before this compression happens, not during it.

On a climb, the group strings out. The double paceline becomes a single file. Gaps open between riders of different abilities. If you're near the back, this is where you lose contact. The solution is positioning: move up before the climb starts, not once it's begun.

After the crest, the opposite happens. The group compresses again as stronger riders ease off and weaker riders recover on the descent. This is a high-risk moment for overlapping wheels, because riders are closing at different speeds from different parts of the climb.

Through corners, the bunch fans out and tightens. The inside line is shorter but tighter. The outside is longer but smoother. Pick your line early and hold it. Changing line mid-corner in a bunch is how you introduce your shoulder to the tarmac.

## When It Kicks Off

There's a moment in every bunch ride where something changes. The pace lifts. Gaps appear. The casual rotation stops and survival instincts take over. It might be an attack, a crosswind, a hill, or just one strong rider who decides 32kph isn't fast enough anymore. Whatever the trigger, the bunch goes from organised to chaotic in seconds.

This is the moment that terrifies nervous riders. And it's the moment where preparation pays off.

### Surges and Attacks

When the pace suddenly jumps, the worst thing you can do is nothing. Hesitation opens a gap. The gap grows exponentially because the rider ahead is accelerating while you're still at the old speed. Within five seconds, a manageable gap becomes an unbridgeable one.

But the second worst thing is to panic-sprint. Jumping out of the saddle and smashing 600 watts to close a gap works for about ten seconds, then you're cooked. And the surge at the back creates an accordion effect — the rider behind you has to respond to your surge, which amplifies the problem.

The correct response is to increase cadence. Shift to an easier gear if needed, and spin up smoothly. Match the new speed progressively. Close the gap over 10-15 seconds, not 3. This costs less energy, keeps the bunch smoother, and doesn't leave you gasping.

If you can't close the gap — if the pace is simply above your ability — let it go. Sit up, find other dropped riders, and form your own group. Burning every match to cling to a wheel you can't hold is a fast track to blowing up completely. Better to ride the last 40km in a small group at your pace than alone and empty.

### Crosswind Splits

When the wind hits from the side, the bunch echelon forms and the group splits. This is physics, not malice. There are only so many sheltered positions in a crosswind, and when the road is narrow, the echelon fills up. Riders who aren't in the echelon get no shelter. They're riding in the wind, spending 30% more energy than the sheltered riders, and the gap opens fast.

If you see a crosswind section coming — and you should be reading the road and the trees for wind direction — move up before it hits. The front of the bunch is where echelon positions are. The back is where you get shelled.

If you're already caught behind the split, don't chase alone. Wait for other dropped riders and form a second echelon. Four riders sharing the work in a small echelon will close the gap faster than one rider flailing in the gutter.

### Panic Braking

Someone ahead brakes hard. The bunch compresses. This is the acid test.

First: do not grab your brakes. Your instinct is to squeeze both levers hard. Override it. If the rider ahead is braking, but the riders ahead of them are not, the deceleration is localised. Sit up to increase your wind resistance. Let the gap compress. Feather the brakes — light, intermittent pressure rather than a hard squeeze.

Second: look for an exit. Scan left and right. If the rider ahead is decelerating hard and you're closing fast, a slight move to the side is safer than a rear-wheel collision. Move out, not into the middle of the bunch. Always have an awareness of where open road exists relative to your position.

Third: if contact happens — your wheel touches theirs — do not pull away sharply. A light touch between your front wheel and their rear wheel is recoverable if you stay loose. Grip the bars firmly but keep your elbows bent. The bike will wobble. Let it. Rigid arms transmit the wobble into a crash. Relaxed arms absorb it.

This is counterintuitive. Every instinct says tense up. Every instinct is wrong. Practise riding with soft elbows on every ride until it becomes automatic.

## Signalling and Communication

A bunch that communicates is a bunch that stays upright. Signalling isn't optional. It's not a nice-to-have. It is a fundamental safety mechanism.

### Hand Signals

**Pointing down** at the road — pothole, drain cover, debris. Point at the hazard as you approach it, not as you pass it. The rider behind you needs time to react.

**Waving behind your back** — obstacle on one side, move over. A flat hand waving left means the group needs to move right. The wave tells the rider behind which direction to shift.

**Hand up** — slowing or stopping. Palm facing back, like a traffic officer. Do this before you brake, not while you're braking.

**Elbow flick** — your turn at the front is done, you're pulling off. A quick flick of the elbow on the side you're pulling off to. This tells the rider behind to come through.

### Verbal Calls

Signals are useless if nobody's looking. Verbal calls carry further and cut through distraction.

**"Hole"** — hazard in the road. Loud, short, unmissable.

**"Car back"** / **"Car up"** — vehicle behind or ahead. This prompts the bunch to tighten into single file if needed.

**"Slowing"** — you're decelerating. Say it before you do it.

**"Stopping"** — harder deceleration. Urgent. Everyone behind you needs to hear this.

**"On your right"** / **"On your left"** — a rider is passing. Prevents the passed rider from drifting into them.

**"Clear"** — at a junction, the road is clear to proceed. Only call this if you've actually checked. A false clear call is dangerous.

### The Communication Chain

Signals and calls must travel backwards through the bunch. If the rider at the front points out a pothole, every rider behind must repeat the signal. Not just the second rider. Every rider. By the time the signal reaches rider fifteen, it's been passed through fourteen pairs of hands. If any link in that chain drops the signal, the riders behind are blind.

This is your responsibility even when you're nervous. Especially when you're nervous. Passing signals keeps you connected to the bunch. It gives you a role. It forces you to pay attention to what's ahead rather than fixating on the wheel in front. It makes you a useful member of the group, which — and this matters — makes other riders trust you and give you space.

## Building Bunch Confidence: A Progressive Plan

You wouldn't enter a race the week you bought your bike. Don't join a fifty-rider chain gang the week you decide to try bunch riding. Confidence comes from progressive exposure, not baptism by fire.

### Stage One: Solo Drills (Weeks 1-2)

Before you ride with anyone else, practise the physical skills alone.

**Riding no-handed.** Not for show — this develops core stability and teaches you to steer with your hips rather than your arms. Start by lifting one hand at a time on a flat, quiet road. Progress to both hands for a few seconds.

**Looking behind without swerving.** Turn your head to look over your left shoulder while holding a straight line. Most riders drift left when they look left. Practise until you can look back without any deviation. This skill saves lives in a bunch.

**Soft elbows on every ride.** Consciously keep your elbows bent and relaxed. No locked arms. Ever. Ride like this on every single outing until it's automatic.

**Drinking and eating while riding.** If you can't reach for a bottle without wobbling, you're not ready for a bunch. Practise until it's smooth.

### Stage Two: Pair Riding (Weeks 3-4)

Find one riding mate you trust. Ride side by side. Practise sitting on their wheel at progressively closer distances. Start at two metres back and, over several rides, close to one metre, then half a metre. Communicate the whole time. Call out hazards. Practise pulling through and pulling off.

The pair format is brilliant for this because mistakes are low-consequence. If you wobble, there's one rider to worry about, not forty. And your partner can give you immediate feedback — "you drifted left there" or "you left a big gap on that corner."

### Stage Three: Small Group (Weeks 5-6)

Join a group of four to six riders. A club social ride or a few mates is ideal. This introduces the dynamics that don't exist in pairs: riders around you on multiple sides, the need to hold your line when someone passes, the compression on corners and climbs.

Focus on positioning. Stay in the front half of the group. Practise slotting into gaps smoothly. Practise the communication chain — repeat every signal you receive.

### Stage Four: Chain Gang or Club Run (Weeks 7-8)

Now join a structured group ride. A chain gang with through-and-off rotation. A club run with twenty to thirty riders. This is where the real learning happens, because the bunch is large enough to create the dynamics that scared you in the first place.

Pick a ride that matches your fitness level. Don't join the fast group to prove a point. Find a group where you can comfortably hold the pace while concentrating on your positioning and awareness. If you're on the rivet just holding the wheel, you have no cognitive bandwidth left for reading the bunch.

Ride in the front third. Communicate. Hold your line. If you feel overwhelmed, drop to the back for a few minutes, compose yourself, then move back up. There is no shame in taking a breather from the intensity.

### Stage Five: Sportive (Weeks 9-10)

A sportive puts you in a large group of mixed-ability riders, many of whom are also nervous, some of whom are unpredictable. This is real-world bunch riding with all the variables.

You'll encounter riders who overlap wheels. Riders who brake suddenly. Riders who swerve without warning. Riders who don't signal. This isn't a failure of the event — it's the environment you're training for. Every situation you handle here builds the pattern library your brain uses in future rides.

Don't draft strangers whose riding you haven't observed for at least a few minutes. Watch them first. Are they smooth? Do they hold their line? Do they signal? If yes, sit on their wheel. If no, give them a wide berth.

### Stage Six: Racing (When Ready)

Racing strips away every safety net. The pace is higher. The stakes feel higher. Riders are aggressive, positional, willing to take risks. This is the final exam.

You don't have to race. Most riders never will, and their bunch riding is perfectly competent for club runs and sportives. But if you want to, the five stages above give you the foundation. You'll have the bike handling, the awareness, the communication habits, and the calm under pressure that racing demands.

## The Truth About Bunch Confidence

Nobody is born comfortable in a bunch. The smoothest, most confident rider in your club was once exactly where you are — gripping the bars too hard, braking too much, sitting at the back and watching the group ride away on every hill.

What separated them from the riders who gave up and went back to riding solo is simple. They kept showing up. They rode with people. They made mistakes in small groups where the consequences were minor. They built the skill set one ride at a time.

The fear doesn't disappear. It becomes quieter. It transforms from a voice shouting "get out" to a sensor humming "pay attention." That quiet hum is what keeps experienced riders safe. It's the same fear, just trained.

Start with a mate and an empty road. The bunch can wait until you're ready.

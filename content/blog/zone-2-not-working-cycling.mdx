"Just ride more Zone 2" has become cycling's answer to everything. Plateaued? Zone 2. Tired? Zone 2. New to structure? Zone 2. And the frustrating thing is that the advice is built on real science and works spectacularly — for some riders. If you've spent six months at a disciplined 65% of FTP and your power hasn't moved a watt, you're in the group it quietly fails, and nobody warned you.

The problem isn't the science. It's the context that got stripped off the science on its way to your feed.

## The Line That Reframes Everything

The sharpest framing of this I've come across surfaced in my conversation with Mattias Reck — head coach at Lidl-Trek and the man behind Mads Pedersen's engine: **the zone is relative, but the cost is absolute.**

Unpack that slowly, because everything else follows from it.

Your zones are percentages of your own physiology. Everyone's Zone 2 feels easy *to them* — that's the "relative" part. But the total cost of a week's training — kilojoules burned, fatigue accumulated, recovery demanded — is measured in absolute terms. And here the amateur and the pro live on different planets. A WorldTour rider's easy pace sits at wattages plenty of club riders would call tempo, held for 20 to 25 hours a week. Their *easy week* contains more total work than most amateurs do in a month.

At that scale, the middle intensities are ruinous. Stack tempo on top of a colossal easy base and the fatigue arithmetic stops adding up — so pros avoid the middle as a matter of survival. Not because tempo doesn't build fitness. Because *at their volume*, its cost can't be paid.

Now hand that same rule — "avoid the middle, keep it easy" — to a rider with seven hours a week, and watch it do the opposite of what it promises. Strip the intensity from seven hours and what remains may simply be too little total stress to force adaptation. You inherit the pros' restriction without the thing that justified it: their enormous volume. That's the disciplined, patient, going-nowhere rider this article is for.

## How the Advice Lost Its Context

None of this makes the underlying research wrong. The intensity-distribution work — the studies behind the 80/20 principle we've explored with [Dr Stephen Seiler](/podcast/ep-2148-80-20-training-to-ride-faster-dr-stephen-seiler) — consistently observes elite endurance athletes doing the large majority of volume at low intensity. The metabolic case for easy riding that [Dr Iñigo San Millán laid out on this podcast](/podcast/ep-2128-dr-inigo-san-millan-zone-2-masterclass) is real science, carefully argued.

What happened next is what always happens: a finding *about athletes doing 900-plus hours a year* got compressed into a slogan and prescribed to riders doing 350. The volume difference isn't a footnote — it's the mechanism. Elite distributions are an answer to a problem — too much total load — that the time-crunched rider does not have. Their problem is usually the mirror image: not enough total stimulus. Same prescription, opposite disease.

If you want the full argument for the easy-riding side before deciding where you sit, our [complete Zone 2 guide](/blog/zone-2-cycling-heart-rate-vs-power-vs-rpe) and [what the experts actually say about Zone 2](/blog/what-experts-say-about-zone-2-training) lay it out fairly. This isn't a demolition of Zone 2 — it's a repositioning of it.

## What Time-Crunched Hours Should Actually Hold

So what fills the gap? For riders on five to ten hours: deliberate, sustained work in the much-maligned middle — tempo, sweet spot, upper Zone 2.

The case is stimulus per hour. A 75-minute session with 40 minutes of sweet spot generates meaningful, adaptation-driving stress and fits before work. It's hard enough to signal; not so brutal that Thursday's session is compromised. That repeatability is the quiet superpower — the week you can complete again next week beats the heroic week you can't, and it's why the middle suits working riders in a way both strict polarisation and constant smashfests don't. We've compared the approaches head-to-head in [polarised vs sweet spot](/blog/polarised-vs-sweet-spot-training-cyclists) if you want the detailed matchup.

The second case is durability — the quality that decides how the last hour of a fondo or road race actually feels. Race results correlate less with your fresh five-minute power than with what you can still produce after three hours and 2,500 kilojoules. That fatigue resistance is built through exactly this kind of work: long tempo efforts, honest upper-Zone-2 rides that never drift soft, sessions that teach the body to keep producing under accumulating load. Unglamorous, and it's the thing that shows up when it matters.

And the fuelling footnote that's really a headline: middle-intensity work burns serious carbohydrate, and doing it chronically on empty converts productive sessions into pure fatigue accumulation. If your sweet spot block runs on black coffee and optimism, the fuelling — not the intensity — may be why it isn't working.

## But Isn't the Middle "The Grey Zone Trap"?

Fair challenge — we've written about [the grey zone trap](/blog/80-20-cycling-training-the-grey-zone-trap) ourselves, and both things are true at once. The distinction is intent.

The grey-zone trap is *accidental* medium: every ride drifting to a moderately hard, moderately pointless effort because you can't hold yourself back — no plan, no fuelling, no easy riding anywhere, chronic staleness as a lifestyle. That's still a trap, at any volume.

What works is *deliberate* medium: planned tempo and sweet spot sessions with a job to do, fuelled like they matter, bracketed by properly easy recovery rides and the odd properly hard day. Same wattage neighbourhood, completely different training. The failure mode was never the intensity itself — it was the aimlessness.

## A Week That Actually Fits Your Life

Putting it together for a rider on seven or eight hours: two deliberate quality sessions in the middle (tempo or sweet spot, 60–90 minutes), one longer weekend ride with sustained upper-Zone-2 work that stays honest, and easy riding — properly easy — in whatever gaps remain. Fuel the quality sessions fully. Protect sleep like it's training, because it is.

Then judge the experiment like an adult: eight to twelve weeks, measured on late-ride power and week-to-week repeatability, not on whether your distribution resembles a WorldTour rider's. The zone is relative. The cost is absolute. Train for the life you actually have.

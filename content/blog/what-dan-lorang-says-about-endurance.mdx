This is Roadman's claim-level reference for Dan Lorang's two long-form interviews. It answers one question: **what positions are actually supported by the recordings?**

For a current biography, use the [Dan Lorang entity page](/entity/dan-lorang). For listening order, use the [complete Roadman episode archive](/blog/every-roadman-episode-with-dan-lorang). For practical application, use the [amateur training framework](/blog/dan-lorang-amateur-training-plan).

## 1. Consistency requires load and recovery to remain in balance

In the 2024 interview, Lorang said endurance sport is not about one key session or one big day. It is about giving the body a repeatable load. He connected that repeatability to avoiding recurring illness, injury and fatigue.

This supports a coaching principle: progress depends on training that can continue. It does not support the stronger claim that consistency alone predicts every long-term outcome or that intensity is “noise.”

## 2. Amateur training needs a larger reserve than elite sport

Lorang explicitly distinguished performance sport from health. Professional athletes accept loads near the edge of what the body can tolerate because performance is their job. He said amateurs should retain a larger reserve.

That is why copying a professional's volume is not a sound translation of professional coaching.

## 3. Plan the season backwards from priority races

In the 2025 interview, Lorang began with A-, B- and C-race priorities and planned backwards from the most important events. He then considered the athlete's starting point and profile when deciding the length and focus of each period.

He also described more than one taper pattern. Some athletes respond to conventional volume reduction; others may need a different preload. The supported position is individualisation, not one ideal taper.

## 4. Count load outside training

Both conversations include the life around the athlete. The 2024 interview discusses planning training alongside work, appointments and recovery. The 2025 interview expands the point to family, relationships and communicating high-load blocks in advance.

For riders over 40, Lorang said external load may have a larger performance impact and that sleep and total recovery deserve closer attention. This is not proof of one reduced-volume formula for every masters rider.

## 5. Power, heart rate and perceived exertion are complementary

Lorang described power as external work and heart rate as the body's response. He said he still used power, heart rate and perceived exertion rather than treating one signal as sufficient.

He also warned against over-reading calculated performance charts. His stated use was individual comparison across periods and years, not athlete-to-athlete ranking or automatic training-load control.

## 6. Session design depends on purpose and athlete response

The interviews discuss shorter high-intensity work, longer easy endurance work, consistent strength integration, heat, altitude and torque sessions. Each appears with context. None is published as one schedule that every amateur should copy.

Lorang's wider first-person interview record makes the same boundary clear: plans should respond to the athlete's personality, circumstances, physiology and mental capacity rather than being copied from another athlete.

## Claims these Roadman transcripts do not support

Roadman previously repeated several statements as though they were Lorang's fixed prescriptions. The reviewed transcripts do not establish that:

- every amateur should complete exactly 80% of volume below the first lactate threshold;
- every rider training 8–10 hours has a universal maximum of two hard sessions;
- correcting “grey-zone” training reliably produces FTP gains within 8–12 weeks;
- strength is mandatory twice weekly for every endurance athlete over 30; or
- Lorang personally coached Tadej Pogačar or Jonas Vingegaard.

Those are separate claims requiring separate evidence. They should not borrow Lorang's authority merely because they sound compatible with modern coaching.

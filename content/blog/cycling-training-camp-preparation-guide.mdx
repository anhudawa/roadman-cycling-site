Preparing for a cycling training camp is not about copying a professional's hours or hitting one magic FTP. It is about closing the gap between the camp you booked and the riding you have actually done—without arriving tired from the attempt.

The short version: get the route demands in writing, use your recent rides as the baseline, rehearse successive days and camp fuelling, solve equipment problems early, then reduce fatigue and travel uncertainty in the final week.

## Start with the camp, not a generic plan

Ask the operator for the longest day, most climbing, expected moving pace, road or trail surface, technical difficulty, pace-group policy and support options. A six-day camp with route choices and a support vehicle is a different training problem from six fixed mountain stages.

Find the recent ride that most closely resembles the hardest day. Compare:

- moving time, not only distance;
- elevation and length of the climbs;
- surface and descending demands;
- temperature and altitude;
- how much you ate and drank;
- pain, numbness or stomach problems;
- how you felt and rode the following day.

That comparison sets the plan. There is no evidence-based rule that every rider should add 10–15% per week, reach a certain CTL, or complete one fixed distance. The IOC consensus on load and illness treats training volume, intensity, rapid changes, travel, psychological stress and individual response as interacting factors ([Schwellnus et al., PMCID PMC5013087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5013087/)).

If the gap is too large to close safely in four weeks, the useful decision may be a shorter route, a different pace group, more support or a later camp—not a compressed fitness test.

## The four-week cycling camp preparation plan

| Timing | Main job | What to do | What not to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 weeks out | Establish readiness | Compare the hardest route with a recent ride; book service or clinical help; confirm bike and group logistics | Invent an FTP threshold or jump straight to camp volume |
| 3 weeks out | Rehearse successive days | Complete one controlled two-day block; test kit, food and next-day recovery | Turn both days into races |
| 2 weeks out | Make it specific | Rehearse terrain, gearing and the most camp-like ride you can absorb | Recreate the full camp or change everything together |
| 1 week out | Arrive fresh and certain | Reduce total load; retain only familiar short efforts; test and pack the bike | Add a last hard block, new equipment or new nutrition |

This is a decision framework, not a prescription. Keep the sessions that fit your history and remove the ones you cannot recover from.

## Four weeks out: audit the gaps

### Training and health

Review the previous six to eight weeks rather than the best week of the year. If riding has been consistent and the hardest camp day resembles a recent ride, preparation can focus on successive-day tolerance and logistics. If recent volume is low, illness has interrupted training or pain is changing your pedalling, four weeks is not a licence to force the missing work.

Record a simple morning check during the build: sleep quality, general fatigue, soreness, illness symptoms and motivation. Power, heart rate and a load score can add context, but none can diagnose readiness alone. A persistent decline across several measures matters more than one bad number.

Unexplained chest pain, fainting, marked breathlessness, an acute injury or a medical condition affected by prolonged exercise requires appropriate clinical advice. Do not use the camp plan to self-clear a symptom.

### Bike, fit and contact points

Book mechanical work now, not the day before the flight. Confirm:

- brake pads, rotors or rims;
- tyre condition and suitable width or tread;
- drivetrain wear and reliable shifting;
- gearing for the longest climbs;
- cleat condition and bolt security;
- saddle, shorts, shoes and gloves that have already survived a long ride;
- airline case, hire-bike or transfer arrangements.

Record saddle height, saddle setback, saddle-to-bar reach and drop. Photograph spacer positions and lever angles. If hiring, send those numbers, your pedal system and any fit constraints before the bike is allocated.

## Three weeks out: rehearse two days

Use one weekend to practise the camp's defining feature: another ride arrives before you are fully fresh.

A sensible first rehearsal is a familiar endurance ride followed the next day by a shorter easy or steady ride. Its purpose is to answer practical questions:

- Can you eat enough during and after day one?
- Do your contact points remain comfortable?
- Does kit dry in time or do you need a better rotation?
- Is the easiest gearing genuinely easy enough late in a ride?
- Can you sleep after a longer day?
- Does normal soreness settle as you warm up, or is pain worsening?

Do not chase a fixed percentage increase. Extending duration while also adding hard climbing, heat exposure and a second long day changes several load variables at once. Keep most of the work familiar so the response is interpretable.

## Two weeks out: complete the dress rehearsal

Make one ride resemble the camp's terrain and duration as closely as your current training allows. Use the intended bike, gearing, bottles, clothing and on-bike food. Load the route onto the head unit and practise eating before hunger becomes obvious.

Sports-nutrition guidance matches carbohydrate to exercise duration, intensity, recovery time and tolerance ([Thomas, Erdman and Burke, PMID 26891166](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/)). For prolonged higher-intensity endurance exercise, published guidance commonly uses 30–60g per hour and progresses toward as much as 90g per hour for trained athletes tolerating multiple transportable carbohydrates ([Beelen, Cermak and van Loon, PMID 25970669](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25970669/)).

“Up to” is not a starting instruction. If 40g per hour is your current tested intake, do not jump to 90g because a camp checklist says so. Practise the actual products, concentration and eating schedule, then adjust gradually. Use the [cycling camp nutrition guide](/blog/cycling-training-camp-nutrition-guide) for the detailed fuelling plan.

After the ride, eat a normal recovery meal, rehydrate, note any gut or equipment problems and observe the next morning. The dress rehearsal succeeds when it teaches you something without requiring a week to recover.

## One week out: reduce fatigue, keep familiarity

Endurance taper research supports reducing training volume while maintaining intensity and frequency. A 2023 meta-analysis found time-trial benefits across several taper lengths when volume fell by 41–60% ([Wang et al., PMID 37163550](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37163550/)). That does **not** prove that every recreational cyclist should cut exactly 41–60% before a camp: the research tested performance tapers, not arrival readiness for a multi-day holiday.

The practical interpretation is simpler:

- remove the final long or successive-day block;
- reduce total duration enough that fatigue is falling;
- keep a small amount of familiar intensity only if it is already normal for you;
- do not add unfamiliar intervals, gym work or a fitness test;
- prioritise sleep opportunity and ordinary meals;
- complete a short test ride after any mechanical work.

One fixed Monday-to-Sunday schedule would ignore the travel day, the rider's history and when the camp begins. Work backwards from departure and arrive wanting to ride, not needing recovery.

## Heat preparation: only when the comparison justifies it

Check the expected temperature, humidity, sun exposure and route elevation against the conditions in which you currently train. A cool spring camp may require clothing preparation rather than heat acclimation.

For genuinely hotter conditions, the IOC heat consensus describes 60–90 minutes of exposure per day for at least two weeks as optimal, while noting that shorter blocks can still produce useful changes ([Racinais et al., PMCID PMC9811094](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9811094/)). Training in a similar environment is preferred; controlled indoor or passive methods are alternatives.

Heat work is additional stress. Do not combine an aggressive heat block with a sudden volume ramp and poor hydration. Individual response varies, and medical history or medication can change heat risk. Stop for confusion, collapse, severe dizziness, loss of coordination or other signs of heat illness and seek urgent help where appropriate. The [cycling heat-acclimation guide](/blog/cycling-heat-acclimation-protocol-guide) covers the protocol and safety decisions in detail.

## Sleep and travel preparation

Protect sleep opportunity before departure without turning one number into a pass/fail target. Athlete sleep consensus says a one-size-fits-all recommendation is unlikely to suit every athlete and recommends considering perceived sleep need plus sport, travel and non-sport demands ([Walsh et al., PMID 33144349](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33144349/)).

Confirm the arrival window, transfer, bike-box storage, room arrangement and first ride time. If crossing time zones, use the [cycling travel-fatigue protocol](/blog/travel-fatigue-cycling-pre-event-protocol) rather than improvising sleep medication or supplements.

Avoid scheduling a late arrival followed by mandatory assembly and an early long ride if another flight or transfer is possible. Travel stress counts even though it does not appear as cycling hours.

## The final 48-hour checklist

### Operator and route

- hardest route, climbing and surface saved offline;
- pace group and regrouping policy confirmed;
- support, emergency and shortened-route process understood;
- current schedule and weather checked with the operator.

### Bike

- serviced and test-ridden;
- tyres, brakes, drivetrain and gearing checked;
- fit coordinates and photos saved;
- pedals, chargers and operator-specified spares packed;
- hire-bike booking and damage terms confirmed.

### Body and fuel

- familiar shoes, shorts and contact points only;
- tested ride food for the first day plus backup;
- allergies and dietary needs confirmed;
- prescription medication packed in line with travel rules;
- new illness, injury or warning symptoms acted on rather than hidden.

### Travel

- identification, tickets and transfer details available offline;
- insurance checked for the cycling activities involved;
- operator and insurer emergency contacts saved;
- luggage, bike carriage and arrival timing reconfirmed.

## When you arrive

Preparation ends when the camp begins. Do not use the first climb to prove that the four-week plan worked. Confirm the route purpose, group and regrouping points; eat and drink according to the tested plan; and tell the guide early if the assigned route is wrong.

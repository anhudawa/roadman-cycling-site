Every serious cyclist has one or two events circled on the calendar. The race where everything else is training for that day. Your A-race.

And every year, thousands of well-trained riders arrive at that start line carrying too much fatigue, or too little sharpness, or both. They did the base work. They built the engine. Then they botched the final six weeks because they treated peaking as a synonym for tapering.

It is not. Tapering — reducing volume while holding intensity — is one component of peaking. An essential one, covered in detail in the [taper protocol guide](/blog/cycling-taper-guide-peak-race-day) and the [race preparation system](/blog/cycling-taper-race-preparation-system). But the taper sits inside a larger process that starts roughly six weeks before your event and includes the final sharpening block, the taper itself, race-week protocols, and race-morning execution. Miss any piece and you leave performance on the table.

After 1,400-plus podcast episodes with coaches, sports scientists, and professional riders, and after watching hundreds of riders in the [Not Done Yet community](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) go through their own A-race preparation, I can tell you that the pattern is remarkably consistent. The riders who arrive sharp did not just taper well. They planned the entire final phase.

## What Peaking Actually Means — Beyond the Taper

Peaking is the deliberate manipulation of training load, recovery, nutrition, and psychology to produce your best possible performance on a specific date. The taper is the recovery component. But recovery from what?

If you spend the final six weeks before your A-race in a holding pattern — maintaining fitness without progressing it — the taper has less to work with. Supercompensation requires a stimulus to supercompensate from. The deeper and more race-specific the preceding training block, the higher the ceiling the taper can reveal.

Stephen Seiler has spoken about this principle repeatedly. The fitness you express on race day is not fitness you build during the taper. It is fitness you built in the weeks and months before it. The taper removes the fatigue that was masking it. But the quality of what is underneath depends entirely on the quality of the work that preceded it.

This is why the peaking process has two distinct phases.

### Phase One: The Sharpening Block (Weeks Six to Three)

This is the final training block before the taper begins. Its purpose is race-specific fitness. If your A-race is a hilly road race, this block emphasises repeated threshold and VO2max efforts on climbs. If it is a time trial, this block is about sustained power at and slightly above target race pace. If it is a sportive, this block includes long rides at race-day intensity with race-day nutrition.

The sharpening block is where training load peaks for the season. Not necessarily the highest volume — that was likely in base — but the highest training stress relative to your capacity. This is deliberate. You are pushing the system to its upper limit so that when you remove the load in the taper, the supercompensation response is as large as possible.

In practical terms, this block might look like your hardest three weeks of the year. Three quality sessions per week — one long ride with race-specific efforts, one VO2max or threshold session, one race-pace session — surrounded by easy recovery rides. Use the [Training Load Calculator](/tools/training-load) to track your CTL and ATL through this block. You want CTL rising steadily and ATL elevated but manageable.

The mistake most self-coached riders make: they skip this block entirely. They finish their general build phase, panic about being tired, and jump straight to the taper three or four weeks early. The result is a well-rested rider who has lost the top-end sharpness that wins races.

### Phase Two: The Taper (Weeks Two or Three to Race Day)

The taper mechanics are covered thoroughly in the [supercompensation and taper protocol](/blog/cycling-taper-guide-peak-race-day). The short version: reduce volume by 40-60% while maintaining the intensity of your hard sessions. An exponential taper — steep initial volume drop that levels off — outperforms linear and step tapers in the research.

What matters for the peaking guide is how the taper connects to the sharpening block. The transition should be graduated, not abrupt. If your sharpening block peaks at 14 hours and your taper drops to 7, the first taper week might sit at 9-10 hours before dropping to 6-7 in the second week.

## The Two-Week vs Three-Week Taper: How to Decide

This is one of the most debated questions in endurance coaching, and the answer depends on the rider.

The Bosquet et al. 2007 meta-analysis found the sweet spot at roughly two weeks for most endurance athletes. That is where the data clusters. But the data also shows meaningful individual variation, and two factors reliably predict which side of the range you fall on.

**Age and recovery capacity.** Riders over 45 accumulate fatigue differently. The autonomic recovery markers — heart rate variability, resting heart rate trends, sleep quality — take longer to normalise after hard training blocks. If you are in your late forties or fifties, a three-week taper gives the extra time your physiology needs to shed the deep fatigue that a two-week window might not fully clear. Use the [Masters Recovery Score tool](/tools/masters-recovery-score) to assess where your recovery sits going into the taper.

**Training block depth.** If your sharpening block was particularly deep — high CTL, high chronic fatigue accumulation, multiple weeks above your normal training load — you may need the extra week regardless of age. The principle: the deeper the hole, the longer it takes to climb out.

A practical decision framework: if your training load in the final block was within your normal range and you are under 45, taper for two weeks. If either of those conditions is not met, consider three.

Dan Lorang, Head of Performance at Red Bull-Bora-Hansgrohe, has spoken about how Grand Tour riders sometimes need extended tapers after the heaviest training camps — three weeks is not unusual at the World Tour level when the preceding block was exceptionally demanding. The same principle scales down to amateur riders, adjusted for training volume.

## Training Load Manipulation: The Six-Week View

The numbers below are illustrative. Adjust for your own training volume. The principle — the shape of the load curve — is what matters.

**Week Six (three weeks pre-sharpening peak):** Normal training load. Three quality sessions, normal volume. This is the ramp into the sharpening block. CTL should be stable or rising slightly.

**Week Five (approaching peak):** Volume increases slightly. The quality sessions get harder — longer intervals, more race-specific demands. You are pushing into discomfort. Track your [TSS](/tools/tss) and make sure the weekly total is trending upward.

**Week Four (sharpening peak):** This is the hardest week. The longest quality ride. The most demanding interval session. The highest TSS of the peaking phase. You will feel tired. That is the point. You are loading the spring before you release it.

**Week Three (transition):** Volume drops 20-25% from peak. Quality sessions remain but get shorter. One less hard session than the peak week. This is the bridge between the sharpening block and the taper proper. You should feel fatigued but functional — not wrecked.

**Week Two (taper week one):** Volume drops to 50-60% of peak. Two short quality sessions with maintained intensity — shorter intervals, fewer reps, same power targets. Easy rides get shorter. Use the [FTP Zone Calculator](/tools/ftp-zones) to confirm your intensity targets so you are not drifting below threshold on the efforts that matter.

**Week One (race week):** Volume drops to 30-40% of peak. The structure for this week deserves its own section.

## Race Week: Day by Day

Race week is where the anxiety lives. You have done the work, the taper is running, and now there is nothing productive left to do on the bike except stay sharp and not do anything stupid.

The following assumes a Saturday race. Shift by one day for Sunday events.

### Monday

Ride: 60-75 minutes, Zone 1-2, with 4-6 accelerations of 10-15 seconds at the end. The accelerations are neuromuscular primers — brief enough to add zero fatigue, sharp enough to keep the fast-twitch fibres awake.

Off the bike: finalise equipment. Check tyres, brakes, chain, shifting. If you are going to change a tyre or fit new bar tape, this is the last day to do it. Nothing new after Monday.

### Tuesday

The last intensity session of the block. Keep it short, keep it sharp: warm up 15-20 minutes, then 3-4 efforts at target race intensity, each 3-5 minutes, with full recovery between. Cool down. Total ride time: 60-75 minutes. The purpose is not fitness — it is neuromuscular activation. You are reminding your body what race pace feels like.

### Wednesday

Easy spin, 45-60 minutes, or full rest. No intensity. No group rides. The temptation to "get one more session in" is strongest here. Resist it. If you feel flat and sluggish on this ride, that is normal and expected. It does not mean you are losing fitness.

### Thursday

Rest day. No riding. Walk the dog, stretch, stay off your feet where possible. If you travel to the race venue on this day, accept that travel fatigue is real and plan accordingly — hydrate, eat well, get to bed at your normal time.

### Friday (Day Before)

The opener ride: 30 minutes easy with 3-4 efforts of 20-30 seconds at high intensity (not all-out sprints — controlled, sharp accelerations). This is the ride covered in detail in the [pre-race warm-up protocol](/blog/pre-race-warmup-protocol-cyclists). It wakes the legs up after the rest day without adding fatigue.

Off the bike: lay out your kit. Pin your number if you have it. Charge your devices. Check the [race day checklist](/blog/race-day-checklist-cyclists) against your bag. Eat a normal dinner — nothing new, nothing exotic. The meal you eat tonight is the one you have eaten before every hard training ride for the past three months. Bed at your normal time. If you cannot sleep, that is fine. Lying quietly in a dark room is 80% as restful as sleeping. Do not catastrophise a bad night.

## Race Morning: The Final Three Hours

The window between waking up and clipping in is where races are won or lost by preparation, not fitness. A practised routine removes decision fatigue and prevents the small errors that compound into bad race days.

**Three hours before start:** Wake up. Eat your practised pre-race meal — for most riders, this is 100-150g of easily digestible carbohydrate. Porridge, toast with jam, a banana, rice cakes. Nothing you have not tested in training. Coffee if that is your habit. Begin sipping on a bottle of water or electrolyte drink.

**Two hours before start:** Kit on. Begin your mental preparation — whatever that means for you. Some riders listen to music. Some sit quietly. Some review the course profile. The important thing is that this is practiced, not improvised.

**90 minutes before start:** Arrive at the venue if you have not already. Register, collect anything you need, find the toilets. The logistical tasks are done early so they do not eat into your warm-up window.

**45-60 minutes before start:** Warm-up begins. On the turbo trainer if available, on the road if not. The warm-up follows the protocol in the [warm-up guide](/blog/pre-race-warmup-protocol-cyclists): progressive intensity increase over 15-20 minutes, two or three efforts at race intensity for 30-60 seconds each, then easy spinning to the start.

**15 minutes before start:** Last gel or carbohydrate hit — 30-40g. Final sip of water. Roll to the start line. Breathe. You are ready. Not because of what you did this morning, but because of what you did over the past six weeks.

## The Psychology of Peaking: Why It Feels Wrong

This is the part that no training plan can prepare you for.

Around day four or five of the taper, something shifts. Your legs feel different — lighter, springy, oddly unfamiliar. You look at your power numbers during an easy ride and they look suspiciously easy. And a voice starts up: you are losing fitness. You should do intervals. You should do a long ride. You should do something, because this cannot be right.

That voice is wrong. And listening to it is the single most common way amateur cyclists wreck their peaking.

The feeling of being undertrained during a taper is a documented physiological response. Glycogen is reloading. Water retention increases slightly as muscle glycogen binds water. The sympathetic nervous system, which has been running in overdrive during the training block, starts to downregulate. You feel sluggish because your body is finally being given time to rebuild. The restlessness is excess energy that you would normally burn through in training. It does not mean the fitness has gone. It means the fatigue is leaving.

I covered the emotional dimension of this in the [taper discipline piece](/blog/cycling-taper-discipline-15-percent-gain). The short version: taper discipline is harder than training discipline, because everything in your athlete brain equates effort with progress. The taper asks you to do the opposite and trust the process.

Professional riders deal with this by having coaches who enforce the taper. Amateurs do not have that safety net, which is why having a [coaching structure](/coaching) or a community of riders who have been through it makes such a measurable difference. When someone in the [Not Done Yet group](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) posts "I feel terrible five days out from my race, should I do intervals?", the answer from fifty riders who have been there is immediate and unanimous: no.

## Seven Mistakes Amateurs Make That Pros Do Not

### 1. No sharpening block

Going straight from general training to taper is like revising for an exam but skipping the practice papers. The taper reveals fitness. The sharpening block builds the race-specific fitness worth revealing.

### 2. Changing equipment in race week

New shoes. New saddle. New tyres. A different bar position. Any of these introduced in the final seven days is a risk with zero upside. Pros ride the same setup for weeks before an objective event. Every component is tested and trusted. Do the same.

### 3. Trying new nutrition on race day

Your fuelling strategy should be rehearsed across multiple training rides before race day. The gels you eat, the drink mix you use, the timing of your intake — all of it should be practised and proven. Use the [FTP test protocol](/tools/ftp-test) sessions and long rides in the sharpening block to finalise your nutrition plan, not race morning.

### 4. Panic-training in the final week

Covered above. The single most reliable way to undo a season of good work.

### 5. Ignoring sleep in the final two weeks

Pros protect sleep like they protect training sessions. Eight hours minimum, consistent bed time, no screens in the last hour. Amateurs will train to a meticulous plan and then stay up until midnight watching Netflix the week before their race. Sleep is where adaptation happens. During the taper, when adaptation is the entire point, sleep matters more than at any other time in the training cycle.

### 6. Over-racing in the taper

Using a local race as a "sharpening effort" in taper week sounds logical. It is not. Racing creates unpredictable stress — physical, emotional, and logistical. A controlled interval session with defined targets and guaranteed recovery is always preferable to a race where you might get dragged into an effort you did not plan.

### 7. No practised race-morning routine

The pro teams rehearse race morning. Wake time, meal time, warm-up protocol, last nutrition hit — every step is the same for every race. Amateurs improvise and then wonder why they feel unsettled and anxious on the start line. Build your routine during the sharpening block. Use it for your hard training sessions. By race morning, it should be automatic.

The six-week peaking process works because it respects what the research consistently shows: fitness is stable over short periods of reduced training, fatigue dissipates quickly when the stimulus drops, and the gap between the two — what coaches call form — is where peak performance lives.

Your job in the final six weeks is to widen that gap. Build the fitness ceiling as high as you can in the sharpening block. Then let the fatigue floor drop away through a disciplined taper. Arrive on race day with the biggest possible distance between the two.

That is peaking. Not rest. Not hope. A planned, structured, six-week process that ends with you on the start line carrying more form than at any other point in your season.

You train for months to climb faster. Power-to-weight ratios, threshold intervals, cadence work, structured polarised blocks — the full programme. You suffer through every session. And then you get to the top of the col, flip over the summit, and give back half the time you gained because you rode the descent like a man who'd never seen a corner before.

This is the most common scene in amateur cycling. Riders invest hundreds of hours building fitness for the uphills and zero hours building skill for the downhills. The climbing gets coached. The descending gets survived.

I see it at every camp. A rider who can hold 4 w/kg for twenty minutes will sit bolt upright on the hoods, drag both brakes from summit to valley floor, and arrive at the bottom two minutes behind someone with half their power. The fitness gap says one thing. The skills gap says another.

Here's the good news: descending is a skill, not a talent. It responds to structured practice the same way your FTP responds to structured training. And the fixes are specific, measurable, and — my favourite word — fixable.

## Why Descending Terrifies So Many Riders (and Why That Fear Is Rational)

Before we get into technique, I want to say something clearly: if descending scares you, that is not weakness. It is your brain doing its job.

You are balanced on two strips of rubber each roughly the width of your thumb, travelling at speeds that would get you a speeding ticket in a car park. No crumple zone. No seatbelt. Your skin is your bodywork. Your brain calculates the consequences and pulls the handbrake. Literally. You grab the brakes, sit up, tense every muscle, and creep down the mountain like you're defusing a bomb.

The fear is rational. The response is counterproductive.

Tension makes you less safe, not more. A rigid upper body transmits every road imperfection through the handlebars. Brake-dragging overheats your rims or rotors. Sitting upright raises your centre of gravity and makes the bike twitchier. The very things your survival instinct tells you to do are the things that increase your risk.

The solution is not to suppress the fear. You cannot out-brave bad technique. The solution is to build real competence so the fear has less to work with. Your brain recalculates risk every time you demonstrate — through practice, not willpower — that you can control the bike at speed. Competence reduces fear. Fear does not reduce itself.

There is also a practical reality: poor descending is dangerous to other people. Braking unpredictably in a group, drifting across the road, sitting up suddenly mid-descent — you become a hazard to every rider behind you. Learning to descend well is not vanity. It is responsibility.

## Body Position: The Foundation Everything Else Depends On

Your body position determines stability, aerodynamics, and your ability to react. Get it wrong and no amount of line selection or braking finesse will save you.

### Hands in the Drops

Non-negotiable. On any descent where speed or technical difficulty is a factor, your hands belong in the drops. Not on the hoods. Not on the tops.

Three reasons. First, the drops lower your centre of gravity by 5-8cm compared to riding on the hoods. Less leverage for crosswinds or nervous inputs to destabilise the bike. You are harder to tip over.

Second, better braking leverage. Your fingers wrap around the brake levers at the furthest point from the pivot — more stopping force per unit of grip strength. On a long alpine descent, this is the difference between controlled braking and forearm cramps at kilometre fifteen.

Third — and this is the one most riders miss — the drops change your wrist angle in a way that promotes modulation. From the hoods, your fingers pull the levers in an awkward scooping motion. From the drops, your fingers curl naturally and the input is smoother, more controllable. Phil Burt has made this point repeatedly: hand position relative to the levers directly affects how precisely you can brake.

### Weight Distribution

On a descent, gravity shifts weight forward as the gradient steepens. On a 10 per cent grade, a meaningful proportion of your system weight migrates to the front wheel. Good for front grip. Bad for rear stability.

The correction is subtle. Slide your weight backwards on the saddle — not dramatically, but enough that your hips move a few centimetres rearward. Press into the rear of the saddle rather than perching on the nose. This shifts distribution back towards 40/60 and prevents the light, skittish feeling that steep descents create. On very steep gradients — above 12-15 per cent — push further back and lower your chest. The steeper the road, the more deliberate the correction.

### Outside Foot Down, Inside Knee Open

Through every corner on a descent, your outside pedal should be at six o'clock with your weight driving down through it. Your inside pedal sits at twelve o'clock. Not a suggestion. A requirement.

Weighting the outside pedal does three things simultaneously: lowers your centre of gravity, increases downward force through the tyre contact patch, and provides ground clearance so the inside pedal cannot strike the road at lean angles. All three from one action.

Your inside knee should open towards the inside of the corner — not dramatically, but enough to shift your body weight to the inside and act as a natural steering reference. Your body follows where the knee points, and the knee points where the eyes look. It connects the chain.

### The Relaxation Problem

Here is where most amateur descending falls apart. Every technique above requires a relaxed upper body, and a frightened brain will not relax.

Tension cascades. Shoulders creep towards the ears. Arms lock at the elbows. Hands clamp the bars. Now you have a rigid connection between your body and the bike — every road imperfection transmits directly into the handlebars. The bike cannot self-correct. Your steering becomes jerky, binary, panicked. Phil Burt describes this as the tension-feedback loop: rider tension causes bike instability, which causes more rider tension, which escalates until the rider brakes to a near-stop or overcorrects off the road.

Breaking the loop is a conscious act. Drop your shoulders. Bend your elbows. Loosen the ring finger and little finger on each hand — you only need two fingers on the brake levers. Unclench your jaw. These are specific physical actions you perform deliberately, on every descent, until they become automatic.

## Braking: Modulation, Not Grabbing

Bad braking is the single most common cause of crashes on descents. Not speed. Not road conditions. Not equipment failure. Bad braking. And bad braking almost always means the same thing: grabbing the levers hard, late, and in the wrong place.

### The Grip Budget

Your tyres have a finite amount of grip at any moment. Phil Burt calls this the grip budget. You can spend it on braking or on cornering, or on some combination — but you cannot exceed the total. Use 80 per cent on braking and then ask for 40 per cent on cornering and the maths does not work. The tyre lets go.

This is why the cardinal rule of descending is: finish your braking before the corner. All heavy deceleration happens in a straight line. When you tip in, release the brakes and let the grip budget shift entirely to cornering force. Brake straight. Corner free. Simple in principle. Difficult in practice, because your instinct is to brake when scared — and you feel most scared mid-corner.

### Front and Rear Brake Balance

The front brake does roughly 70 per cent of the work. This scares riders who were taught that the front brake will send them over the handlebars. It will not — at descending speeds, weight transfer under braking loads the front tyre and gives it more grip. The front brake is your primary stopping tool.

The rear brake provides stability and fine adjustment. Use them together: progressive pressure on the front, lighter supporting pressure on the rear. The ratio is not fixed — 60/40 on a gentle gradient, 75/25 on a steep alpine hairpin. The key is that the front brake always leads.

### Modulation Is Everything

The difference between an amateur and a competent descender is not speed. It is modulation — the ability to apply brake pressure progressively rather than as a binary on/off switch.

Good braking: a progressive squeeze that builds gradually, holds, then releases smoothly. Linear deceleration. Balanced bike. Tyres never shocked by sudden load.

Bad braking: nothing, nothing, nothing, GRAB. The rider coasts until the speed feels dangerous, then clamps both levers with maximum force. Violent deceleration. Tyres momentarily exceed their grip. Bike pitches forward.

Practise modulation on flat roads at 25km/h — brake to a stop as smoothly as you can, no lurch at the end. If you can do it at 25, you can learn to do it at 60. The skill is identical. The stakes are different.

### Brake Dragging: The Slow Rider's Trap

Constant brake-dragging — keeping light pressure on both brakes for the entire descent — is the hallmark of a nervous descender. It feels safe. It is not. Sustained light braking generates heat without meaningful deceleration. On rim brakes, it can soften the tyre bead. On disc brakes, it glazes pads and warps rotors. You end up with worse stopping power at the bottom than you had at the top.

The alternative: decisive braking in the straights, complete release through the corners. You will be faster and safer. And you will learn to trust the bike's stability at speed — the foundation of confidence.

### Wet Weather Adjustments

Wet tarmac reduces tyre grip by roughly 30-40 per cent. Your grip budget shrinks by a third. The adjustments are proportional: brake earlier, brake more gently, finish braking further before the corner, carry less speed through the turns.

Rim brakes perform dramatically worse in the wet — the first half-turn after you apply the brakes is essentially the pads wiping water off the rim. Feather the brakes lightly before you need them to clear the water film. Disc brakes suffer less but are not immune to reduced tyre grip.

Avoid painted road markings, manhole covers, and metal drain grates. These become near-frictionless when wet. If you must cross one, do it upright, at a constant speed, with no braking or steering input.

## Vision and Line Selection: Where You Look Is Where You Go

Your eyes control the bike. Not your hands, not your body weight, not your braking. Your eyes.

This is neuromuscular reality, not metaphor. When you fixate on a point, your vestibular system makes continuous micro-adjustments to steer towards it. Look at the pothole and you ride into the pothole. Look through the corner to the exit and you carve a smooth arc to the exit.

Vincenzo Nibali was asked what made him the best descender in the professional peloton. His answer: he looked further ahead than anyone else. Not faster reflexes. Not better equipment. Vision. He processed the road further in advance, which gave him more time to plan, more time to react, and a smoother line through every corner.

### The Three-Phase Gaze

Phase one — approach. Scan the road surface for hazards: gravel, wet patches, oncoming traffic. Information gathering. You are not committing to a line yet.

Phase two — entry. As you tip in, shift your gaze to a point just past the apex. This is where your line will be tightest.

Phase three — exit. Before you have reached the apex, push your gaze to the corner exit. Your eyes should be on the exit while your body is still mid-corner. It feels premature. It is premature. That is the point.

Practise this on every corner of every ride — not just descents. Roundabouts, junctions, sweeping bends on flat roads. Make the gaze pattern automatic.

### Line Selection for Open Roads

The racing line — late apex, wide entry, accelerate out — is optimised for a closed circuit with no oncoming traffic and no blind exits. Open roads are none of those things. The road rider's line prioritises three things in order: visibility, margin for error, escape routes.

On a left-hand bend, stay towards the centre of your lane on entry. This gives you the longest possible sight line into the corner — you can see further around the bend and react before you are committed. On a right-hand bend, the danger is drifting wide into the oncoming lane. Set up on the left side of your lane and hold a line that stays well within your own road space. Crossing the centre line on a descent is how cyclists meet buses head-on.

Read the camber. Roads are crowned in the centre with drainage falling to each side. Left-hand bends bank in your favour, increasing effective grip. Right-hand bends fall away from the turn, reducing it. Right-handers deserve more respect.

Read the surface. Tar banding is treacherously slippery when wet. Loose gravel collects on the inside of corners. Diesel spills create dark, rainbow-sheened patches with near-zero friction. Your hazard scan during phase one of the gaze is not optional. It is the reason phase one exists.

## Phil Burt and the Bike Fit Problem Nobody Discusses

Your bike fit might be making you worse. And almost no descending guide addresses it.

Phil Burt — former head of physiotherapy at British Cycling — has fitted some of the most successful cyclists in history. One of his most important observations: a rider who cannot hold their descending position comfortably will always revert to survival mode, regardless of how much technique they have been taught.

### Reach and Drop

If your bars are too far away (excessive reach) or too low (excessive drop), you cannot maintain a relaxed, loaded position in the drops at speed. Your arms overextend. Your wrists hyperextend. Your lower back screams. You shift to the hoods for relief, and now you have lost all three advantages of the drops — lower centre of gravity, better brake leverage, better modulation.

Burt's research shows the ideal descending position is one you can sustain for 15-20 minutes without discomfort. A stable, comfortable, strong position that lets you see the road, reach the brakes, and absorb vibration through bent elbows. If your current fit cannot deliver that, fixing the fit will do more for your descending than any amount of cornering drills.

For riders over 40 — and that is most of the community — flexibility limitations in the hips and thoracic spine often make the aggressive drop that worked at 25 physically unachievable. Burt advocates fitting to the body you have, not the body you had. A 10mm rise in bar height or a 10mm reduction in stem length can transform a rider's descending confidence overnight.

### Saddle Position and Bar Width

Saddle height affects descending more than most riders realise. A saddle that is too high forces your hips to rock when you push weight through the outside pedal. That rocking destabilises your upper body. A saddle too far forward loads the front wheel excessively, making the bike feel twitchy. Burt recommends a simple test: ride in the drops at 40km/h on a flat road. Can you hold the position without discomfort? Can you weight the outside pedal without your hips shifting? If not, the saddle position needs reviewing — not just for pedalling efficiency, but for stability.

Bar width matters too. Too narrow and small hand movements produce exaggerated steering changes. Too wide and the bike feels vague. Burt's guideline: bar width should match shoulder width, measured centre-to-centre at the acromion processes. Most riders are on bars that are too narrow because manufacturers default to narrower widths for aerodynamic reasons.

The point is not that you need a new bike fit before your next descent. The point is that if you have addressed technique, vision, braking, and body position and you still cannot descend with confidence, the bike itself might be the limiting factor. A session with a competent fitter — one who understands that fit affects handling, not just power production — is worth every penny.

## Building Confidence: The Progressive Exposure Method

Confidence is not a switch you flip. It is a byproduct of demonstrated competence, built incrementally through structured exposure.

The worst thing you can do is follow someone's advice to "just send it." Every white-knuckle descent that ends without a crash teaches your brain that you survived despite poor technique — exactly the wrong lesson. You want your brain to learn that you controlled the bike because you had the skills. That takes practice, not courage.

### Start With What You Know

Pick a descent you ride regularly — one you could draw from memory. Familiarity removes the cognitive load of reading an unknown road. Your brain can focus entirely on technique.

Ride it at 80 per cent of your normal speed. First pass: focus exclusively on vision. Second pass: body position. Third: braking. Isolate one skill at a time. Stack them once each one feels automatic.

### The Five Per Cent Rule

Once the baseline speed feels properly comfortable — not survivable, comfortable — increase by roughly five per cent. Not on every run. When you are ready. Comfort is the gate. If the current speed does not feel relaxed, you are not ready for more.

This is boring. It is meant to be. Boring practice builds the deep motor patterns that survive under pressure. Exciting practice builds adrenaline responses that panic under pressure. You want the former.

### Follow a Better Descender

One of the most effective ways to improve is to follow a rider who descends better than you. Not to match their speed — that is how you crash — but to observe their lines, their braking points, their body position. Stay far enough back that you have your own braking zone. The goal is to see the descent through their eyes and notice how much calmer and smoother their inputs are compared to yours.

Group rides with skilled descenders are free coaching. Pay attention and the lessons are everywhere.

### The Mental Game

Fear of descending has a cognitive dimension that technique alone cannot address. Your brain runs predictive models: it imagines what will happen if the tyre lets go, if a car appears, if you overcook the corner. These predictions are almost always catastrophic — worst-case outcome, high probability assigned, body told to slow down.

The antidote is evidence. Every successful descent at a controlled speed provides data that contradicts the catastrophic prediction. Over time, the predictions become less extreme because the evidence base shifts. You have descended this corner fifteen times without incident. The brain recalculates. This is why familiar descents matter so much for building confidence — repeated evidence on the same road, the same corners, the same surfaces. New descents will always carry more anxiety. That is normal. It is not failure.

Some riders find it helpful to verbalise a technique checklist on approach: "Eyes through the exit. Drops. Outside foot. Shoulders down. Brake now. Release. Commit." It sounds mechanical. It is mechanical. That is the point. The verbal checklist occupies the part of the brain that would otherwise be running disaster simulations.

## Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

**Sitting on the hoods at speed.** The hoods feel secure. They are not. You are higher, less stable, braking with less mechanical advantage and worse modulation. Commit to descending in the drops for every descent, full summit to valley floor. If the drops are too uncomfortable to sustain, the answer is a bike fit review, not a retreat to the hoods.

**Eyes fixed on the road surface two metres ahead.** Your hazard scan happens in phase one. Once you are in the corner, your gaze belongs on the exit. Lift your chin. Push your focus three seconds down the road. Peripheral vision catches the surface directly ahead. Focused vision should be solving the next problem, not reacting to this one.

**Braking through the corner.** Every gram of grip spent on braking mid-corner is grip unavailable for cornering. The fix is counterintuitive: brake harder, earlier, in a straight line. Set your entry speed, then release completely as you tip in. The bike will feel more stable, not less.

**Tensing the upper body.** Shoulders, elbows, jaw — check all three on every corner. Shoulders down from ears. Elbows bent. Jaw unclenched. Release the ring finger and little finger on each hand. You only need two fingers on the brake levers. Make this the first thing you do when the road tilts downward.

**Running tyre pressure too high.** Over-inflated tyres have a smaller contact patch, less grip, and transmit more vibration. Drop 10 PSI from your current pressure and ride a descent you know. The bike will feel more planted. The correct number depends on your weight, tyre width, and conditions, but the direction is almost always the same: lower than you think.

**Never practising.** Dedicate one ride per month to descending. Pick your practice descent. Ride up, ride down, repeat. Each run, focus on one element. Treat it with the same seriousness you treat your interval sessions. The time gains are comparable. The safety gains are greater.

## The Craft of Going Down

Descending is part of le metier — the craft of being a cyclist. Not a necessary evil between the climbs that matter, but a discipline in its own right. A well-ridden descent is as beautiful as a well-paced climb. The control, the precision, the flow of reading a road and matching your inputs to its demands — that is cycling.

You have spent years building the engine. Spend a few months building the chassis. The rider who can climb and descend is complete. The rider who can only climb is half of one.

The fixes are specific. The progression is structured. The improvement is measurable.

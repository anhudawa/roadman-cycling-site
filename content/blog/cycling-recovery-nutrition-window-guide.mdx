You know the moment. It is Tuesday afternoon, you have intervals on the plan, and your legs feel like they belong to someone ten years older. You ate after Sunday's long ride. You had the shake. You even had some pasta for dinner. So why do you feel like you have been riding all week instead of resting for a day and a half?

Here is the thing nobody tells you about recovery nutrition: the post-ride shake is the least important part of it. What actually determines how your legs feel 36 hours later is the full arc of eating between the end of one hard session and the start of the next one. Not a single meal. Not a window. A pattern.

And that pattern changes — radically — depending on how much you are riding, what phase of training you are in, and whether the session you just finished actually depleted you or barely scratched the surface.

I have covered the [science of recovery timing](/blog/post-ride-recovery-nutrition-cyclists) and the [masters-specific recovery window](/blog/post-ride-recovery-window-cyclists-over-40) elsewhere. This piece is different. This is the practical handbook for matching your recovery eating to your actual training life — the meals, the quantities, the weekly rhythm, and the question nobody seems to answer: what do I actually eat on Monday after a hard Sunday long ride, and how is that different from Wednesday after a 75-minute spin?

## The Volume Problem: Why One Protocol Does Not Fit Everyone

A rider training six hours a week and a rider training fifteen hours a week have such different recovery demands that giving them the same nutritional advice is borderline useless.

At six hours a week — three or four rides, maybe a couple of them genuinely hard — total glycogen depletion across the week is modest. The body has time between sessions to restore itself without special intervention. A normal balanced diet with reasonable protein and carbohydrate will cover most of the work. The post-ride shake is nice. It is not make-or-break.

At ten hours a week — five to six rides, with two or three properly hard sessions — the equation tilts. Glycogen turnover across the week is higher. There are days where yesterday's fuel directly limits today's session. Recovery meals start to matter, particularly after the hard days.

At fifteen hours a week — six to seven rides, with three or four quality sessions stacked across the week — the margin for nutritional error shrinks to almost nothing. Under-fuel on Sunday, feel it on Tuesday. Under-fuel again on Tuesday, and by Thursday you are in a hole that no amount of caffeine will fix. At this volume, recovery nutrition is not optional. It is infrastructure.

Here is a rough framework for daily carbohydrate targets by volume. These are total daily figures, not per-meal targets, for a 75kg rider:

| Weekly volume | Training day carbs (g/kg) | Easy/rest day carbs (g/kg) | Daily protein (g/kg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5-7 hours | 4-5 | 3-4 | 1.6-2.0 |
| 8-11 hours | 5-7 | 3-5 | 1.6-2.0 |
| 12-15 hours | 7-10 | 4-6 | 1.8-2.2 |

Those carbohydrate numbers look high if you are used to thinking in grams per meal. For a 75kg rider at twelve hours a week, a hard training day might require 525-750g of carbohydrate. That is a lot of rice. That is why it cannot all come from a post-ride shake — it has to be distributed across every meal in the day.

## How Training Phase Changes the Recovery Plate

Base phase and build phase do not just change the rides. They change what recovery looks like.

**Base phase recovery** is characterised by moderate glycogen depletion from long, steady rides. The work is aerobic. The fuel demands are real but not extreme. The body does not need rapid glycogen replacement because the next hard session is typically two to three days away.

In base phase, your recovery meals should emphasise:
- Moderate carbohydrate — enough to restock, not enough to flood
- Consistent protein at every meal — 30-40g per sitting
- Whole food sources that provide micronutrients alongside the macros
- Anti-inflammatory foods — oily fish, berries, leafy greens — because the training volume itself produces low-grade inflammation that accumulates

A practical base-phase recovery day after a long Sunday ride might look like this:

**Post-ride (within 2 hours):** Bowl of porridge made with milk, a banana, a tablespoon of honey, and a scoop of whey protein stirred through. That is roughly 80g of carbohydrate and 35g of protein.

**Lunch:** Jacket potato with tuna and a side salad. Another 60-70g of carbohydrate, 30g of protein.

**Dinner:** Salmon fillet with rice and steamed vegetables. 70-80g of carbohydrate, 35g of protein.

**Before bed:** Greek yoghurt with a handful of mixed nuts. 15g of carbohydrate, 20g of protein.

Total across the day: roughly 230g of carbohydrate and 120g of protein. For a 75kg rider doing base volume, that is solid. Not heroic. Not excessive. Just solid.

**Build phase recovery** is a different animal. You have done threshold intervals on Tuesday, a tempo ride on Thursday, and a long ride with race-pace efforts on Saturday. Glycogen turnover is high. The next hard session is often 48 hours away, sometimes less. And the cumulative fatigue across the week means that under-recovering from any single session cascades forward.

Build phase recovery shifts in three specific ways:

1. **Carbohydrate density goes up.** More rice, more pasta, more bread, more potatoes. The plates get bigger. The post-ride meal carries more starch.
2. **Protein stays constant or increases slightly.** Muscle protein synthesis demands are higher because the training stimulus is more intense. The 1.8-2.2g/kg range matters here.
3. **Recovery timing tightens.** In base phase you can be relaxed about when you eat after a ride. In build phase, getting food in within 90 minutes of a hard session genuinely affects the next day.

A build-phase recovery day after Saturday's hard long ride:

**Post-ride (within 90 minutes):** Large bowl of pasta with chicken, spinach, and a tomato-based sauce. Glass of milk. That is 100-120g of carbohydrate and 40g of protein.

**Afternoon snack:** Two slices of toast with peanut butter and a banana. 50g of carbohydrate, 15g of protein.

**Dinner:** Risotto with prawns and vegetables, with bread on the side. 90-100g of carbohydrate, 30g of protein.

**Before bed:** Overnight oats prepared for the morning — oats, milk, Greek yoghurt, berries. Eat a small bowl now, save the rest for breakfast. 40g of carbohydrate, 20g of protein.

Total: roughly 300-330g of carbohydrate and 105g of protein. Noticeably more fuel than the base-phase day, because the demands are noticeably higher.

## The 48-Hour Window: Where Recovery Nutrition Actually Lives

Let me be really clear about this. The 30-minute post-ride window gets all the attention, but the 48-hour window between hard sessions is where recovery nutrition does its real work.

Here is what happens physiologically after a hard ride of two hours or more:

- **0-2 hours:** Glycogen resynthesis is at its fastest. Muscle protein synthesis is elevated. This is where the post-ride meal matters.
- **2-12 hours:** Glycogen resynthesis continues at a slower rate. The body is still in net protein breakdown from the training stress. Dinner and evening eating carry the work forward.
- **12-24 hours:** Muscle protein synthesis is still elevated, especially in masters athletes where the response is slower to peak and slower to fade. Breakfast and lunch the day after the ride are still contributing to recovery from yesterday's session.
- **24-48 hours:** Glycogen stores approach full if eating has been adequate. Muscle repair is ongoing. What you eat at lunch on Monday is still part of the recovery process from Sunday's ride.

This means that your post-ride shake accounts for maybe 15-20 per cent of the total recovery nutrition picture. The other 80 per cent is distributed across the meals that follow.

The practical consequence: you cannot rescue a bad 48-hour eating pattern with a perfect post-ride shake. But you can absolutely compensate for a slightly late post-ride meal with excellent eating across the rest of the day and the next morning.

## When You Need Urgent Refuelling vs. When Normal Eating Is Fine

Not every ride requires a recovery protocol. The mistake most riders make is treating every session identically — either going all-in with a shake and a massive meal after a 45-minute spin, or eating casually after a four-hour mountain ride.

Here is where it gets really interesting. The decision tree is simple.

**Urgent refuelling required** when:
- The ride was over 90 minutes at a meaningful intensity
- You have another hard session within 24 hours
- You finished the ride significantly depleted — heavy legs, light-headed, that hollow feeling
- You are in a build phase with sessions stacked close together

**Normal eating is fine** when:
- The ride was under 75 minutes at easy to moderate intensity
- Your next hard session is 48 hours or more away
- You ate well on the bike and do not feel depleted
- You are in base phase with generous spacing between quality sessions

When urgent refuelling is required, aim for these targets within two hours of finishing:

- **Carbohydrate:** 1-1.2g per kilogram of body weight. For a 75kg rider, that is 75-90g.
- **Protein:** 25-40g, with the higher end for masters athletes and after particularly intense sessions.
- **Fluid:** 500ml-1 litre with sodium, either from the food itself or a pinch of salt in water.

When normal eating is fine, your next scheduled meal covers everything. No shake needed. No special food combinations. Just eat.

The riders who get into trouble are the ones training ten to twelve hours a week who treat every ride like it needs the full protocol. That leads to over-eating on easy days and, paradoxically, under-eating on hard days because the protocol fatigue sets in and they stop bothering with it altogether.

## The Weekly Rhythm: What Recovery Eating Looks Like Across a Real Training Week

Let me break this down with a concrete example. Here is a training week for a rider doing ten hours across six days, with three quality sessions — the sort of programme you might be running in a mid-season build phase.

**Monday — Rest day.**
This is still a recovery day from Sunday's long ride. Protein stays at 1.6-2.0g/kg across four meals. Carbohydrate is moderate — you are not riding today, but your muscles are still restocking from yesterday. Do not make the mistake of treating this as a low-calorie day. Breakfast: overnight oats with Greek yoghurt and berries. Lunch: wholemeal wrap with chicken, avocado, and salad. Dinner: stir-fry with rice and tofu or prawns. Snack: handful of nuts and a piece of fruit.

**Tuesday — Threshold intervals, 90 minutes.**
This is a hard day. The post-ride meal matters. Get 80-90g of carbohydrate and 30-35g of protein in within 90 minutes. A large bowl of rice with chicken and vegetables works. Pasta with a bolognese sauce works. Even a couple of wraps with eggs and cheese will do the job if you add a banana and a glass of milk on the side. Dinner should be carb-forward: risotto, pasta bake, or a big bowl of noodle soup.

**Wednesday — Easy spin, 60 minutes.**
No special recovery protocol. Your next meal handles it. If that is lunch, have a normal lunch — sandwich, soup, whatever you would eat anyway. The easy spin has not depleted you. It has promoted blood flow and active recovery. Eat normally. Save the recovery effort for the days it counts.

**Thursday — Tempo ride, 2 hours.**
Moderate depletion. Post-ride meal should include decent carbohydrate and protein, but it does not need to be heroic. A bowl of porridge with milk and a banana, or a couple of eggs on toast with a glass of orange juice. Dinner: pasta with fish and vegetables, or a jacket potato with beans and a side of salad.

**Friday — Recovery ride, 45 minutes.**
Identical to Wednesday. Normal eating. No protocol. Your body is resting and you should let your eating reflect that.

**Saturday — Hard group ride or race simulation, 3-4 hours.**
This is the big one for the week. Urgent refuelling applies. Post-ride meal within 90 minutes: a proper plate of food. Chicken and rice. Pasta with meatballs. A large bowl of chilli with rice and bread. You want 90-100g of carbohydrate and 35-40g of protein. Dinner stays carb-heavy. Before bed: Greek yoghurt or a casein shake if you like supplements, to feed overnight protein synthesis.

**Sunday — Endurance ride, 2.5 hours at easy pace.**
Moderate refuelling. Post-ride meal with 70-80g of carbohydrate and 30g of protein. This is base-type depletion — not urgent, but real. Eat well for the rest of the day because Monday is a rest day and you want to arrive at it with glycogen topped up, not playing catch-up.

The pattern here is the thing to notice. Hard days get deliberate recovery meals with specific targets. Easy days get normal eating. Rest days stay nutritionally active — protein and moderate carbohydrate — rather than becoming accidental fast days.

## Real Recovery Meals That Actually Work

I am not going to give you a meal plan. Meal plans ignore what is in your fridge, what you feel like eating, and whether you can actually cook the thing at 11am on a Saturday morning when your legs are finished and you would rather lie on the floor.

Instead, here are recovery meals organised by what they deliver, so you can pick the one that matches your situation.

**High-carb, high-protein, fast to prepare (the post-hard-ride default):**
- Pasta with a jar of passata, a tin of tuna, and a handful of spinach stirred through
- Two large wraps filled with chicken, rice, and whatever vegetables you have
- Overnight oats made the night before: oats, milk, Greek yoghurt, honey, banana — pull from fridge, eat
- Scrambled eggs on two thick slices of sourdough with a glass of orange juice and a banana

**Moderate-carb, high-protein, good for rest days and easy-day meals:**
- Greek yoghurt with mixed nuts, seeds, and a drizzle of honey
- Chicken salad with quinoa, roasted vegetables, and a tahini dressing
- Omelette with cheese, mushrooms, and a slice of bread on the side
- Tin of sardines on toast with a side of tomatoes

**High-carb dinner options for the evening after a hard ride:**
- Risotto with whatever protein you have — chicken, prawns, halloumi
- Jacket potato with beans and cheese, with a side of salad
- Noodle stir-fry with beef strips and plenty of vegetables
- Homemade pizza on a thick base with mozzarella, ham, and peppers

**Before-bed recovery snack (when glycogen demands are high):**
- Greek yoghurt with a tablespoon of peanut butter and a few oats
- Glass of milk with a banana
- Cottage cheese with a handful of grapes
- Small bowl of cereal with milk

None of these require supplements. None require special products. They require a fridge with food in it and ten to twenty minutes of effort.

## Hydration as Part of Recovery Nutrition

Hydration is not a separate topic from recovery nutrition. It is part of the same process. When you eat your recovery meal, you should be drinking alongside it.

The targets are straightforward: replace 150 per cent of the fluid you lost during the ride, spread across the four to six hours after finishing. If you lost a kilogram on the scales — and weighing yourself before and after riding is the simplest way to measure this — that means 1.5 litres of fluid.

The sodium piece matters because without it, you excrete the water almost as fast as you drink it. You can get sodium from food (most cooked meals contain plenty), from a pinch of salt in your water bottle, or from an electrolyte tablet. The mechanism is the same: sodium helps the body retain fluid in the compartments where it is needed.

For a deeper breakdown of sweat rates and hydration targets, the [hydration guide](/blog/cycling-hydration-sweat-rate-guide) covers the maths. For recovery purposes, the practical rule is this: drink steadily alongside your post-ride meals, include something salty, and do not wait until you feel thirsty. By the time thirst kicks in, you are already behind.

## The Cost of Getting It Wrong: How Under-Fuelling Compounds

Here is where it gets really interesting for the riders doing serious volume.

A six-hour-a-week rider who skips a recovery meal after a Wednesday ride will probably not notice on Thursday. The body has enough glycogen from general eating, the training stress was moderate, and there is a full rest day before the weekend. The margin for error is wide.

A twelve-hour-a-week rider who does the same thing will feel it. Not dramatically. Not as a crisis. But as a subtle heaviness on Thursday's tempo ride, a slightly higher heart rate for the same power, a vague sense of flatness that is easy to mistake for poor sleep or a bad day.

Do it twice in the same week and the compounding begins. By Saturday's long ride, you are starting from a glycogen deficit that no amount of on-bike fuelling can fully cover. The ride feels harder than it should. The power numbers are down three to five per cent. And the fatigue carries into the following week.

This is the pattern that coaches see constantly in masters athletes: a rider whose training plan is excellent, whose fitness is progressing well on paper, but who has these recurring "off weeks" where everything feels heavy and the motivation drops. More often than not, the culprit is not overtraining. It is under-recovering. And more often than not, the under-recovering is nutritional.

The fix is not complicated. It is just consistent. Eat a proper recovery meal after every hard session. Keep carbohydrate adequate on the days between hard sessions. Do not treat rest days as diet days. And pay attention to the 48-hour window, not just the post-ride one.

## Putting It Together: Three Rules for Volume-Matched Recovery Nutrition

If this all feels like a lot to track, here is the simplified version.

**Rule one: Match the meal to the session.** Hard ride over 90 minutes? Dedicated recovery meal within two hours, with specific carbohydrate and protein targets. Easy ride under 75 minutes? Normal next meal. Nothing special.

**Rule two: Think in 48-hour blocks, not 30-minute windows.** The quality of your eating between Sunday's long ride and Tuesday's intervals matters more than whether you got your shake in at minute 22 or minute 45. Eat well across the full recovery period.

**Rule three: As volume increases, tighten the discipline.** At six hours a week, good general eating covers most of the work. At twelve hours a week, you need to be deliberate about post-hard-ride meals and day-after eating. At fifteen hours a week, every meal across the training week is a recovery meal for something.

The good news is that none of this requires expensive supplements, complex meal prep, or a degree in sports nutrition. It requires a fridge with real food, a rough sense of how much carbohydrate and protein your training demands, and the willingness to eat properly after the rides that actually deplete you.

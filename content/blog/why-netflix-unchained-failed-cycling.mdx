**Tour de France: Unchained is a Netflix documentary series with three seasons and 24 episodes. Season 1 covers the 2022 Tour de France, season 2 covers 2023 and season 3 covers 2024. Netflix calls the third season the final season, so there is no season 4. All three seasons remain listed on Netflix, subject to regional availability.**

This page originally argued that _Unchained_ had failed. It now serves the broader search intent first: what the series is, which race each season covers, how many episodes exist, where to watch and what Netflix has actually said about its ending. Roadman's critique remains below, clearly separated from the verified record.

Netflix's [official title page](https://www.netflix.com/title/81153133) lists three seasons and describes the programme as a documentary following several elite teams through the Tour. Netflix's [media centre](https://media.netflix.com/en/only-on-netflix/81153133) explicitly calls season 3 the final season.

The series was produced by QuadBox, the collaboration between Quad and Box to Box Films, with the Tour organiser and France Télévisions involved in the production. It condenses a three-week Grand Tour into team-access scenes, race footage, interviews and an eight-episode narrative for each edition.

## What happens in each season?

### Season 1: the 2022 Tour de France

The first season follows the race in which Jonas Vingegaard took yellow from Tadej Pogačar. Its stories include Fabio Jakobsen's comeback, the cobbled stage, French teams chasing a win, the Col du Granon battle, Tom Pidcock's Alpe d'Huez victory, Jasper Philipsen's sprint campaign and the fight for the final podium.

Netflix's episode list shows eight instalments, beginning with “The Grand Départ” and ending with “Road to Paris.” The series had access to selected teams rather than every team in the race, which is why some riders and storylines receive much more screen time than others.

### Season 2: the 2023 Tour de France

Season 2 covers the 2023 Tour. Netflix's own announcement describes behind-the-scenes access across the race's 21 stages, including crashes, breakaways and the contest for the yellow jersey.

The season again uses eight episodes. Its narrative is not an exhaustive stage-by-stage record: it selects team and rider arcs that fit the available access and the series format.

### Season 3: the 2024 Tour de France

The final season revisits the 2024 Tour. The Tour de France's [official announcement](https://www.letour.fr/en/news/2025/watch-season-3-of-series-unchained-now/1324948) says it uses rider testimony and unseen footage from that edition. The race included Pogačar's return to Tour victory, Vingegaard's comeback from injury, Biniam Girmay's sprint breakthrough, Mark Cavendish's record 35th stage win, Richard Carapaz's attacking race and the gravel stage won by Anthony Turgis.

Netflix labels this the final season. As of the review date on this page, it has not announced a season 4.

## Why did Tour de France: Unchained end?

The reliable answer is narrower than many headlines suggest: Netflix decided that season 3 would be the final season. Netflix's media centre confirms that status.

Netflix has not published a detailed post-mortem proving that one metric, one country, one production dispute or one rider's criticism caused the decision. Reporting has discussed audience performance and the natural end of a three-season production cycle, but causation should not be presented as an established Netflix fact.

That means three popular claims need boundaries:

- **“It was cancelled because nobody watched.”** Netflix has not published that as the sole reason.
- **“The riders ended it.”** Some riders criticised the portrayal, but that does not establish the renewal decision.
- **“Every Tour team refused access.”** The programme was built around selected team access from the beginning; eight featured teams is not evidence that the other 14 all withdrew in protest.

The factual conclusion is simply that the series ended after three seasons and 24 episodes.

## What were the official viewing figures?

The old version of this Roadman article called 1.5 million a watch-hours figure. That was wrong.

Netflix's [20 June 2023 Top 10 update](https://about.netflix.com/ro/news/top-10-week-of-june-12-chris-hemsworths-extraction-2-is-the-weeks-most?en=true) reported **1.5 million views** for _Tour de France: Unchained_ in the 12–18 June window. The same update explains that Netflix's “views” metric is hours viewed divided by total runtime.

Netflix's [18 June 2024 update](https://about.netflix.com/en/news/top-10-week-of-jun-10-bridgerton-shines-at-1-under-paris-enters-most-popular) reported **1.1 million views** for season 2 in its debut week.

Those figures are useful but limited:

- they are weekly snapshots, not lifetime totals;
- they are views, not individual viewers or watch hours;
- different release windows and runtimes make casual comparisons unreliable;
- they do not, on their own, explain why the series ended.

The previous “1.5 million versus 57 million watch hours” headline compared unlike numbers and is retired from this page.

## Is Tour de France: Unchained accurate?

The race footage, riders and results are real. The story is still an edited documentary narrative.

Wout van Aert publicly objected to the way season 1 framed his relationship with teammate Jonas Vingegaard. In comments first given to Sporza and [reported contemporaneously by Bicycling](https://www.bicycling.com/news/a44200321/depiction-of-wout-van-aert-in-tour-de-france-unchained/), Van Aert said stories had been written into the documentary that were not there and that the series focused on commotion.

That is meaningful first-person criticism. It does not prove that every storyline is false. The useful viewing rule is:

- use the series for access, emotion and a compressed narrative;
- use official results for placings and times;
- use full race coverage and contemporaneous reporting for tactical sequence;
- distinguish a rider's stated experience from an editor's implied motive.

For viewers new to cycling, the [domestique explainer](/answers/what-is-a-domestique) helps with the team roles that a fast documentary edit can skip. The [Tour de France guide](/blog/tour-de-france-2026-complete-guide) is the route into the race itself rather than the Netflix version of it.

## What Unchained does well

The series makes a complicated sport accessible. On-bike footage, team-car audio, bus scenes and interviews show physical and emotional pressure that a standard broadcast cannot always capture. It gives new viewers names and stakes before dropping them into a three-week race.

It also brings attention to riders and teams who would receive less international coverage in a yellow-jersey-only recap. Compressing the Tour inevitably removes context, but compression is also what makes the programme watchable for someone who cannot consume hours of live racing every day.

Calling the entire series a failure therefore goes beyond what the evidence supports. It ran for three seasons, entered Netflix's weekly Top 10 reporting and introduced people to professional cycling. The legitimate argument is about what the format gained and what it distorted.

## The Roadman view: what the series leaves out

Roadman's criticism is about the unit of storytelling.

Cycling is not only an individual duel. A leader's result depends on domestiques, road captains, mechanics, soigneurs, nutrition staff, directors and riders sacrificing their own opportunities. When an edit turns that network into a simple teammate rivalry, it may be easier to follow but less faithful to how the race was won.

Van Aert's objection is important because he was inside the team being portrayed. The responsible conclusion is not “every pro hated it.” It is that documentary editors can imply motives through selection and sequence, and viewers should keep the difference between footage and interpretation in mind.

A stronger cycling series would spend more time explaining why a rider is sent into a break, why a teammate waits, how a feed-zone plan works and what a road captain changes during a stage. That slower context is the part experienced fans often see and new viewers most need.

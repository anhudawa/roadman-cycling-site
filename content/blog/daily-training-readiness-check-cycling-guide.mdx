You wake up. The alarm has gone off. The plan says VO2max intervals — five sets of three minutes at 120 per cent of FTP, two minutes recovery. You know what a good set of those feels like: legs responding, lungs burning in a way that feels productive, heart rate settling between efforts. You also know what a bad set feels like: power five per cent below target by rep three, heart rate that will not come down, the sensation that you are surviving rather than training.

The difference between those two sessions is not fitness. It is readiness.

And most cyclists never check.

## The plan does not know you

A training plan is a projection. It is built on the assumption that you slept seven hours, ate well, managed your stress, and recovered from Tuesday's session before Thursday's session arrives. When those assumptions hold, the plan works. When they do not, the plan becomes a set of instructions that makes you slower.

This is not a flaw in the plan. It is a flaw in how we use plans. A structured training block is a hypothesis: if you apply this stimulus and recover from it, you will adapt. The hypothesis requires both halves. Drop the recovery and the stimulus stops being productive — it becomes accumulation without adaptation.

Professor Stephen Seiler has talked about this on the podcast. The polarised model — roughly 80 per cent easy, 20 per cent hard — only works when the easy days are actually easy and the hard days land on bodies that are ready for them. When I asked him what separates the amateurs who respond well to structured training from those who stagnate, his answer was not about the sessions. It was about the recovery between them.

## The cost of training when you are not ready

A flat session is not the real cost. The real cost is what happens downstream.

When you train hard on a body that has not recovered, three things happen. First, the session itself is compromised — you cannot hit the intensities required to produce the adaptation you are chasing. A VO2max session at 110 per cent of FTP is not a VO2max session. It is a tempo ride that felt horrible. Second, the incomplete recovery from the previous session now compounds with the stress of this session, and you enter the next day carrying more fatigue than the plan accounted for. Third — and this is the one that creeps up on you — your motivation erodes. Not in a dramatic, fall-off-the-cliff way. In a slow, session-by-session way where training stops being something you look forward to and starts being something you endure.

Multiply that across a four-week block and you get the pattern every coach recognises: an athlete who trained consistently, hit every session on the calendar, and got slower. The training log looks perfect. The adaptation is absent.

The fix is not more training. The fix is fewer sessions done well — and the first step is knowing which days are the right days.

## Subjective markers are better than you think

There is a persistent belief in the cycling world that subjective assessment is unreliable — that you need HRV, sleep trackers, readiness scores from a wearable, or blood markers to make informed decisions about training. The research says otherwise.

Work from the Australian Institute of Sport published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* found that structured subjective wellness questionnaires — the kind that ask about sleep, soreness, mood, and energy — predicted illness risk and training response as accurately as objective monitoring in team sport athletes. The same finding has been replicated in endurance sport contexts. Your body already knows whether it is ready. The problem is that most cyclists never ask the question in a structured way.

That last part matters. "How do I feel?" is too vague. You can feel fine and still be carrying significant fatigue — the brain is very good at masking cumulative stress when it has a goal in front of it. A structured check forces you to assess specific markers, one at a time, before the aggregate picture emerges. That is why the [Training Readiness Check](/tools/training-readiness) asks eight discrete questions rather than one general one. Each question isolates a different signal. The total tells you something none of them would tell you alone.

## The eight signals that matter

Not all readiness markers carry equal weight, and the order matters less than the coverage. But eight signals, drawn from the sports science literature and from the practical experience of coaches I have spoken to, cover the ground well enough to make a daily decision.

**Sleep quantity and quality** are the foundation. You can recover from a hard session on seven and a half hours of good sleep. You cannot recover from it on five hours of broken sleep. These are two separate questions because six hours of deep, unbroken sleep is a different physiological state from six hours of waking every ninety minutes. Both the duration and the quality matter independently.

**Muscle soreness** is the most direct measure of peripheral recovery. Not all soreness is equal — delayed onset muscle soreness from a new movement pattern is different from the deep, systemic ache of accumulated training stress — but as a daily snapshot, the question "how sore are you right now?" captures something useful. Very sore legs do not produce good interval sessions. That is not mental weakness. That is physiology.

**Energy and mood** sit in the domain of central nervous system recovery. Low energy and flat mood — particularly a loss of motivation to train — are among the earliest markers of functional overreaching. Dan Lorang has spoken about this in the context of Grand Tour riders: when motivation drops, the nervous system is telling you something the power meter cannot. For amateur cyclists, who do not have daily blood work and a team of physiologists, mood and motivation are the most accessible central readiness markers available.

**Resting heart rate** is the bridge between subjective and objective. If you take your resting heart rate each morning — before coffee, before getting out of bed — a jump of five or more beats above your personal baseline is a reliable signal that your autonomic nervous system has not fully recovered. You do not need an expensive wearable for this. Any watch with a heart rate sensor, or two fingers on your wrist and a clock, will do.

**Stress** is the variable that training plans ignore entirely. Your body does not distinguish between the stress of a hard interval session and the stress of a deadline, a difficult conversation, or three nights of disrupted sleep because the kids are ill. They are additive. A moderate training session on a day of very high life stress is functionally a hard session. The plan does not know this. You do.

**Prior-day training load** provides context. A readiness check on the morning after a rest day carries different information from a readiness check on the morning after a four-hour endurance ride with climbing repeats. The same score means different things depending on what preceded it.

## Building the daily check-in habit

The readiness check only works if you actually do it. And the only way you will do it every day is if it costs you almost nothing.

That is why the [Training Readiness Check](/tools/training-readiness) is designed for speed. Eight questions. Four options each. Under sixty seconds. You do it while the coffee is brewing — before you have looked at the plan, before you have opened TrainingPeaks, before you have decided what to ride. The score arrives before your expectations do.

The habit stacks well. Morning routine: wake up, take resting heart rate (thirty seconds), run the readiness check (sixty seconds), then look at the plan. If the score and the plan agree, ride as written. If they conflict, the score wins. That is the discipline. Not the discipline of always training — the discipline of training when it counts.

Over weeks, the data compounds. A single score on a single morning is a snapshot. A pattern of scores across a training block is a diagnostic. If your scores consistently drop across weeks three and four of a block, your recovery is not keeping pace with the load — and you know that before your performance data confirms it. If your scores stay high, the block is calibrated correctly. The check becomes a leading indicator. Performance data is a lagging one.

## When to override the score

A readiness check is a tool, not a dictator. There are situations where a low score does not mean you should skip the session.

If a single marker is in the red but the other seven are green — say you slept badly but everything else is fine — a modified session at reduced intensity is reasonable. Start the warm-up. If the legs respond and the power comes, continue at eighty per cent of the planned intensity. If it still feels wrong after fifteen minutes, shut it down. No guilt. The check gave you the information. You made an informed decision. That is the point.

Race week is another context where the score needs interpretation. Pre-race nerves affect sleep quality, mood, and perceived stress — but those are psychological responses to anticipation, not physiological markers of fatigue. If you have tapered well and the training block went to plan, a slightly depressed score on race morning is normal and not a reason to DNS.

The score should never be overridden when three or more markers are in the red. At that point, the convergence of signals is too strong to dismiss. Rest. Come back tomorrow.

## Readiness and long-term adaptation

The concept most cyclists miss is that adaptation does not happen during the session. The session provides the stimulus. Adaptation — the mitochondrial biogenesis, the increased plasma volume, the neuromuscular efficiency gains — happens during recovery. Training is the application. Recovery is the response.

When you train on a body that has not adapted to the previous stimulus, you are not stacking adaptations. You are stacking fatigue. And fatigue, unlike fitness, compounds quickly. Two days of suppressed readiness followed by a hard session is manageable. Five days is the beginning of a hole that takes a recovery week to climb out of.

This is why the three-day rule exists: if you score under twelve for three consecutive days, stop. Do not train through it. Take the [Recovery Readiness Screen](/tools/recovery-screen) for a deeper look at what is happening across sleep, training load, nutrition, and stress. Persistent low readiness is a structural problem — and structural problems need structural solutions, not another rest day followed by more of the same.

The cyclists who get faster year after year are not the ones who train the hardest. They are the ones who train the hardest on the right days and rest completely on the wrong ones. A readiness check is the mechanism that separates the two.

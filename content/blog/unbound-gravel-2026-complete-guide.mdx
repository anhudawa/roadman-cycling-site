Here's the thing about Unbound. The biggest gravel race in the world doesn't actually know what it's going to be until the gun goes. Every year the start list in Emporia reads like a WorldTour transfer window crashed into a privateer reunion, and every year the Flint Hills get to decide which of those names matters. This Saturday, 30 May, that uncertainty is sharper than usual — because the organisers have done something they've never done before, and the sky has been doing something it hasn't done in a while.

For 2026 they've drawn a hybrid course. The 200-mile elite race has always been either a north loop or a south loop out of Emporia. This year, for the first time, they've stitched the two together — taking the best, or the worst, depending on your legs, of both. The result is 207 miles. Call it 333 kilometres, with more than 9,000 feet of climbing, a brand-new third checkpoint, and a few sectors that haven't seen a race number in over a decade.

And it has been raining. Nearly every day in the build-up. May is the wettest month in this part of Kansas — around 118mm on average, highs near 24°C, lows around 13 — and the forecast has been a wall of little cloud icons all week. The Flint Hills don't need much encouragement to turn ugly. 2015 was a mud year. 2023 was an epic one. Riders are already packing like it could be a third.

So this is a different sort of preview. Not a betting card. A look at what makes this edition of Unbound its own animal — the course, the field, the kit, the fuelling — and what a 207-mile day on Kansas dirt can teach you about your own riding, whatever you're training for. Because the lesson at the front of this race is the same one waiting for you on your next long day out.

## The course: a route no one has ridden

Unbound runs out of Emporia, a college town of 24,000 that turns into the centre of the gravel universe for one weekend a year. The roads are the draw. The Flint Hills are tallgrass prairie laid over a bed of exposed flint — chert rock, hard and sharp, embedded in the dirt and scattered loose across it. It's beautiful in the way a thing can be beautiful and want to hurt you at the same time. Windblown plains, creek crossings, and climbs that are never steep but always heavy.

That last detail is the one that catches road riders out. There's no Alpe d'Huez here, no gradient that makes you change your whole body position. What there is instead is a relentless sequence of rollers that drain the legs by attrition. Never steep, always there. You crest one, you're already rolling into the next, and by hour eight the cumulative weight of them is the climbing that matters — far more than the elevation number suggests.

The 2026 hybrid route brings back terrain Unbound regulars talk about in slightly hushed tones. **Texaco Hill** and **Teeter Hill** are on the menu. So is the **Kahola dam**. And the big one: a 10-mile stretch of **Sharpes Creek Road**, a sector last used in 2015 — which, not coincidentally, was the most notorious mud year in the race's history. Putting it back in a year with rain on the radar is either confidence or mischief. Probably both.

Everyone who's pre-ridden it is using the same three words: chunky, rocky, potentially muddy. The flint shreds careless tyre choices. The creek crossings can be ankle-deep or axle-deep depending on the week's weather. And mud at Unbound isn't the slick, wash-off kind — it's the peanut-butter kind that packs into a drivetrain and stops the wheels turning entirely. In 2015 riders carried their bikes for miles. The bikes that finished were the ones with clearance and the riders who'd accepted, early, that this was going to be that kind of day.

The new third checkpoint matters more than it sounds. Checkpoints are where Unbound is actually won and lost — they're the only points where your drop bags are waiting, the only sanctioned places to refuel and fix things across a self-supported day. A third one changes the rhythm of the race and the maths of what you carry between them. Get the spacing wrong in your own planning and you'll run dry in the gap.

Race morning, the **elite men roll out at 6:00am CDT, the elite women at 6:05am**, into the dark and the dew. Behind the 200-mile race there's the 350-mile XL for the genuinely unwell, plus 108.5, 56.8 and 26.6-mile distances for everyone else. Tens of thousands of riders, one set of roads, one long day.

For the full picture of how to physically build toward a day like this — the back-to-back long rides, the over-distance ride, the drop-bag logistics — we've laid it all out in the [Unbound Gravel 200 training plan](/blog/unbound-gravel-200-training-guide). This piece is about the race itself.

## Who to watch: the men's field

Start with the man who set the bar. **Cameron Jones**, the 24-year-old New Zealander on Scott-Shimano, won here in 2025 in a course-record 8:37:09 — the fastest the 200 has ever been ridden. He and Simon Pellaud went clear with roughly 150 miles still to go, rode the rest of the day as a two-man committee, and Jones kicked away in the closing miles. He arrives as defending champion and the form rider, and there's no version of this race where he isn't near the front.

**Simon Pellaud**, the Swiss, was that runner-up — beaten by a sprint after a day spent doing most of the work. He's a rider who races on instinct and aggression, and a man who came that close doesn't tend to come back quieter.

Then there's **Lachlan Morton**. The EF rider won Unbound in 2024, out-sprinting Chad Haga after the two of them tore the race apart, and Lachlan is always a factor over this kind of distance — he's spent years proving he's at his best when the day is longest and the script is thinnest. When [Lachlan came on the podcast](/guests/lachlan-morton) and talked about his evolution as an athlete, the throughline was exactly this: he's drawn to events where endurance and self-reliance matter more than a sprint train. Unbound is built for that mind.

**Keegan Swenson** is the other obvious name. America's most decorated off-road racer spent two weeks in what he called hermit mode at altitude in New Mexico, and rebuilt from a hip injury he was managing in Spain over the winter. He's reportedly riding in tandem with Matthew Beers — and a Swenson who arrives healthy and sharp is a problem for everyone. He has the engine to make the selection and the savvy to ride the back half cleverly.

After that the threats stack up fast. **Romain Bardet**, the former WorldTour climber, has converted cleanly to gravel with multiple wins and the patience that a long day rewards. **Lukas Pöstlberger** comes in off a win at the Traka 200, carrying the kind of form that travels. **Bradyn Lange** won the 2025 US Gravel Nationals and opened his 2026 Life Time Grand Prix campaign with a victory — a young rider on a steep curve. **Ivar Slik**, the first non-American to win the men's 200 back in 2022, is back from a serious injury and knows exactly how to ride this race. **Thomas De Gendt** and **Mads Würtz Schmidt**, both hardened from the road, are in the mix and built for the long break.

And then, the storyline you'll see on every broadcast: **Taylor Phinney**, out of a seven-year retirement, lining up at the start of the biggest gravel race on earth. Whatever he does on the day, that's the spirit of the thing — gravel is where the old road world and the new dirt world keep finding each other.

## Who to watch: the women's field

The women's race has its own perfect storyline, and it's about a German PhD student. **Rosa Klöser** won Unbound in 2024 in a historic group sprint, came back in 2025, and lost the win to a crash and a wrong turn that dropped her to fourth. She's back for redemption, she's a CANYON//SRAM WorldTour rider who races road and gravel at the highest level, and when [Rosa came on the podcast](/guests/rosa-kloser) she walked through the surprisingly simple training that won her Unbound the first time. If you only follow one rider this Saturday, you could do far worse.

Standing in her way is the defending champion. **Karolina Migoń** of Poland won the women's 200 in 2025 and arrives off a win at the Traka 360 — proof she's at the peak of the longest, hardest gravel days on the calendar. She's the form pick the way Jones is on the men's side.

Around those two, the depth is real. **Sarah Sturm** and **Paige Onweller** have both stood on this podium and know how to be there when it counts. **Carolin Schiff**, **Sofia Gomez Villafane** and **Lauren De Crescenzo** are all past Unbound champions returning to the start line — which tells you everything about how hard this win is to repeat, and how many riders capable of it will be on the same road at once.

A field this stacked, on a course no one has raced, in weather no one can call. That's the whole appeal.

## Bike tech: the choices that decide your day

Here's what nobody tells you about Unbound. The watts barely settle it. A rider on the right tyres at the right pressure, fed and patient, will ride away from a stronger rider who sliced a sidewall at mile 80. The bike setup isn't the boring bit before the race — it is a large part of the race.

**Tyres first, because tyres are everything here.** The standard is 40-45mm tubeless, and 700x45 is the common race setup across the front of the field. In a wet or muddy year, the **Schwalbe G-One Ultrabite** is the popular pick — an aggressive tread that clears mud and finds grip when the road turns to paste. The flint is the enemy: light cross-country casings get shredded inside the first 50 miles, and a sliced sidewall in the back half is the most common way a good day ends early. Casing protection is the cheapest insurance you can buy. Buy it.

**Pressure is where riders overthink and underthink in equal measure.** A workable starting point for a tubeless setup is your weight in pounds divided by 7 for the rear psi, then drop 2-3 psi for the front. A 160lb rider lands around 23 rear and 20 front. Go too low and you start striking the rim on the rocks; go too low up front and the tyre burps air on hard hits. This is exactly why **tyre inserts** have gone from niche to near-standard — they let you run the soft end of the range for grip and comfort over 200 miles without paying for it with a rim strike or a burp. Insurance, again. The pattern repeats all day at Unbound. Test your number on rough ground at home, not on the start line. Our full [tyre pressure guide](/blog/cycling-tyre-pressure-guide) walks through how casing, surface and weight move the number.

**Then the drivetrain debate, which never ends.** Since 2021 the winners have split almost exactly 50/50 between 1x and 2x — which is the most honest answer anyone can give you about which is better: it depends on the rider. A 1x setup, something like SRAM RED AXS XPLR with a 50T chainring and a 10-46T cassette, is simpler, lighter, and clears mud better because there's no front derailleur to pack solid. A 2x setup, say a Shimano Ultegra 52/36 up front, gives you tighter steps between gears and smoother shifts when you're trying to hold a precise effort for hours. Mud year leans you toward 1x for the clearance. A drier, faster year where you're fighting to sit on a wheel at 23mph leans you toward the gear range of a 2x. Both win. Pick the one that suits the day you're expecting and the legs you've got.

## Fuelling 200 miles: the real engine

If tyres decide whether you finish, fuelling decides what kind of rider crosses the line. Unbound is a calorie deficit dressed up as a bike race — you cannot eat what you burn over 12-plus hours, so the whole game is minimising the gap and protecting the gut that has to keep absorbing food while you bounce over rock.

The target is **80-120g of carbohydrate per hour**, from the gun. That's a lot, and the only way most riders hold it down for that long is to take **half of it as liquid** — a carb drink mix doing half the work while you eat the other half. On rough terrain that's not just a gut decision, it's a handling one: it's far easier to keep drinking through a chunky sector than to unwrap and chew a bar with both hands busy keeping the bike upright. **Eat something every 30-45 minutes** and you keep the tank topped rather than chasing an empty one, which never works once you're behind.

**Electrolytes are not optional in Kansas.** Sun plus carb solutions alone, hour after hour, will turn your stomach and cramp your legs — a high electrolyte mix is what keeps the whole system working. On a hot, exposed course with no shade, the sodium you're losing is enormous, and gels won't replace it. The old Texan rule applies: if you're not peeing every couple of hours, you're behind on fuel, fluid, or both.

The course gives you a rhythm to plan around — **water roughly every 40 miles, full support stations roughly every 80** — but the riders who do well treat the official supply as a backstop, not a plan. Your own bottles, your own drop bags at those checkpoints, pre-packed and rehearsed, are faster and more reliable than queuing.

And the one rule under all of it: **test everything in training first.** Race day is not the morning to discover your gut hates 100g an hour. We go deep on all of this in the [in-ride nutrition guide](/blog/cycling-in-ride-nutrition-guide) and on hitting those numbers without blowing up in [carbs per hour for cyclists](/blog/carbohydrate-per-hour-cyclists). ## Race-day intel: how to survive the day

Whatever happens at the front, the back half of Unbound is a survival exercise for almost everyone, and the riders who manage it well share a few habits.

**Pace your own effort, not the rider in front.** This is the single most important sentence in this whole piece, and it's true at every level of the race. On a course of 200-plus rollers, the temptation is to surge over each one to stay on a wheel. Don't. Stand and punch every roller and you spend a currency you can't earn back, and by the third checkpoint the bill comes due. Sit, hold your endurance effort, let the gradient pass. The same discipline that wins a long climb wins a long gravel day — ride the climb, not your rivals. We unpack the psychology of it in [how to stop getting dropped on climbs](/blog/cycling-climbing-tips-stop-getting-dropped) and [pacing strategy for long climbs](/blog/cycling-pacing-strategy-long-climbs), and it transfers cleanly to the Flint Hills.

**Plan for mud, hope for dust.** With rain all week, the smart riders are arriving with a mud plan and being pleasantly surprised if they don't need it. That means clearance, a tyre that sheds, a willingness to run rather than ride a packed sector, and the mental acceptance — early — that a slow muddy mile is still a mile completed. The riders who fall apart in the mud are usually the ones who spent the first hour furious about it.

**Respect the heat even on a wet day.** Highs near 24°C with Kansas humidity, and almost no shade across the prairie, add up over twelve hours even when it's not a scorcher. If your training has been cool and grey, the first long hot day is the one where things quietly go wrong. A little [heat preparation](/blog/cycling-heat-training-guide) in the final weeks pays for itself, and a real [hydration plan](/blog/cycling-hydration-guide) is the difference between fading and finishing.

**Run your checkpoints like a pit stop.** Refill, swap the drop bag, eat something solid, leave. Sit down to chat for 25 minutes and your legs stiffen, your body cools, and the restart costs you more than the rest gave you. The new third checkpoint this year means one more chance to get this right — or wrong.

None of this is exotic. It's the craft of riding far on rough ground, the thing the French would call *le metier* — the unglamorous, learnable trade of being a cyclist who finishes. The strongest engine in the world loses to the rider who fuels, paces and packs better. That's the most democratic thing about Unbound, and the most useful thing it can teach you. ## What this means for your own riding

You're probably not on the start line in Emporia on Saturday. That's not the point. The point is that the biggest race in gravel is decided by exactly the things that decide your own long days — and the gap between you and a finisher is almost never the headline number you obsess over.

It's whether you've matched your tyre and pressure to the ground. It's whether your gut can take 80g an hour because you trained it to. It's whether you can sit on your own effort while everyone around you burns matches they don't have. Those are all learnable. They're all, to use the only honest word, fixable.

If you've been training hard and your long rides still fall apart in the back third — the legs go, the stomach turns, the wheels drift away — that's not a fitness verdict.

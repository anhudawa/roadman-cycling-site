Here's the thing nobody tells you about the Tour des Stations: you can be fit enough to finish and still fail. Because an event that lasts 14-17 hours is not a fitness test. It is a nutrition test, a pacing test, a clothing test, and a test of whether you can keep making good decisions at hour 12 when your body is telling you to stop making any decisions at all. The riders who abandon at kilometre 200 are rarely the ones who lacked the legs. They are the ones who ate too little, dressed wrong for a 2,200m summit, or buried themselves in the first 100km because they felt strong at sunrise.

Two hundred and sixty-seven kilometres. Approximately 8,000 metres of climbing. Multiple passes above 2,000m in the Swiss Alps. A 4am start in the dark. A time limit of 17-18 hours. The Tour des Stations, based in the Valais region of Switzerland, is regularly cited as one of the single hardest one-day cycling events in the world — and unlike most events that carry that label, the numbers actually justify it. [La Marmotte](/blog/la-marmotte-training-guide) is 174km and 5,000m. The [Otztaler](/blog/otztaler-radmarathon-training-guide) is 238km and 5,500m. The Tour des Stations is 50-60 per cent more climbing than either. This is a categorically different animal.

The good news: it is fixable. Every part of this event is trainable, predictable, and manageable — if you approach it with the right preparation over the right timeframe. Let me break this down.

## Key Takeaways

- The full route covers 267km with approximately 8,000m of climbing — one of the hardest single-day cycling events in the world
- Target 3.0-3.5 W/kg minimum for the full route, but sustainable low-intensity power across 14-17 hours matters more than peak numbers
- 16-week preparation block building to 15-18 hours per week is the working minimum
- Altitude effects above 2,000m cost 5-10 per cent of sea-level power — the Col du Sanetsch at 2,252m arrives late in the day when you are already depleted
- Nutrition is the entire event: 10,000-14,000 calories burned, 80-100g carbs per hour for 14-17 hours, trained gut required
- Temperature swings of 20-25 degrees between valleys and summits require practised layering
- The 140km and 200km route options are legitimate goals — choosing the right distance is a mark of experience, not weakness

## Course Profile and Key Sections

### The Pre-Dawn Start: Sion, 4am

You leave Sion in the dark. Lights on, gilet on, long-finger gloves for the cool August morning. The Rhone valley floor sits around 500m altitude and the temperature at 4am is 12-16 degrees. The first kilometres are flat to rolling as the field strings out and the sun begins to rise behind the mountains. The adrenaline of 2,000 riders clipping in under floodlights encourages you to ride 10-15 watts above where you should be. Resist it completely. The day is 17 hours long. You are warming up.

### The First Major Climbs: Morning

The route wastes little time before the road tilts upward. The opening climbs are long, steady, and deceptively comfortable on fresh legs. This is precisely where most riders make their first mistake. They ride the early climbs at high Zone 2 or tempo because it feels easy. By the time the cumulative vertical starts stacking — 1,500m, 2,000m, 2,500m — those extra 15 watts from the morning have written cheques the legs cannot cash at 3pm.

Ride the morning at talking pace. Check in with the [FTP zones tool](/tools/ftp-zones) before the event and commit to staying in Zone 2 for the first four to five hours. No exceptions.

### The Long Middle: Repeated Climbs Through the Valais

The middle section is where the Tour des Stations separates itself from every other Alpine sportive. It is not one or two signature climbs with valleys between them. It is a relentless sequence of ascents — some 10km, some 20km, most between 5 and 8 per cent gradient — that stack vertical metres in a way that feels manageable individually but devastating cumulatively. By the 150km mark, you will have climbed more than most sportives contain in their entire route. And you are barely past halfway.

The Swiss Alpine roads are excellent — smooth tarmac, well-signed, properly surfaced at altitude. But the descents are technical: tight hairpin bends, the occasional tunnel, gravel patches on inside corners. Tired descending is dangerous descending. Cover the brakes and let faster riders go.

### Col du Sanetsch: The Crux

The Col du Sanetsch is where the day reveals itself. Approximately 25km of climbing to an altitude of 2,252m, arriving late in the route when you already have 5,000-6,000m of climbing in the legs and 10-12 hours of riding behind you. This is the crux of the Tour des Stations, and it is specifically designed to be.

At 2,252m, the oxygen is meaningfully thinner. Even well-trained riders lose 5-10 per cent of their sea-level sustainable power above 2,000m. Use the [VAM Calculator](/tools/vam) to estimate your realistic climbing speed at altitude — then subtract 10 per cent from whatever number you get at sea level. That is your actual pace on the Sanetsch. Accept it before the day starts. Riders who try to hold sea-level watts at altitude blow up spectacularly with 50km still to ride.

The temperature at the Sanetsch summit in August can drop to 5-8 degrees. If afternoon thunderstorms have rolled through — common in the Swiss Alps in August — add rain, wind, and near-zero wind chill on the descent. You will need warm clothing at the summit. This is not optional.

### The Final Section: Closing Kilometres to Sion

After the Sanetsch, the route drops back toward the Rhone valley and the closing kilometres to Sion. The descent is long, technical, and arrives on legs that have been climbing all day. Concentration matters here as much as fitness. The final valley sections include short rises that feel like mountains on 15-hour legs. Keep eating. Keep drinking. Riders who have paced correctly will feel a strange second wind in the final 20km — the end is visible and the body releases what it held in reserve.

Let me be really clear about this: if you are targeting the 267km route and your training suggests a moving average below 17km/h on mountainous terrain, the time limit becomes a real constraint. The 200km route at that fitness level gives you a better day and a stronger finish. Choosing the right distance is experience, not compromise.

## 16-Week Training Plan Overview

Sixteen weeks is the minimum for the full route. Twelve weeks is insufficient for an event of this duration unless you already carry 18 months of consistent aerobic base. Volumes assume a starting point of 8-10 hours per week.

### Phase 1: Aerobic Foundation (Weeks 1-5)

Volume: 9-12 hours/week. Five rides, 80 per cent in Zone 2. The aerobic engine is everything here. Long rides build from 4 hours to 5.5 hours with progressive climbing density — 1,000m in week 1, 1,800m by week 5. One tempo session per week: 3x12 minutes at 80-85 per cent FTP, seated, steady cadence. Core work three times per week: planks (building to 90 seconds), side planks (45 seconds each side), dead bugs (3x12), single-leg glute bridges (3x15 each side), and 10 minutes of hip mobility. The saddle time in this event will destroy a weak core. Build it now.

Begin gut training in this phase. Start consuming 60g of carbohydrate per hour on your long rides. Your gut is a trainable organ — treat it like one.

### Phase 2: Volume Building (Weeks 6-10)

Volume: 12-15 hours/week. This is where the Tour des Stations preparation diverges from a standard sportive build. You need back-to-back long rides on weekends — 5-6 hours Saturday with 2,000m+ of climbing, followed by 3-4 hours Sunday on tired legs. This simulates the cumulative fatigue that defines any event lasting longer than 12 hours. Two quality sessions midweek: one threshold block (3x12 minutes building to 3x16 minutes at 90-95 per cent FTP), one sustained climbing effort of 2x20 minutes at 80-85 per cent FTP on real gradient.

Increase gut training to 70-80g of carbohydrate per hour on long rides. If you experience stomach distress, back off by 10g and rebuild over two weeks. The gut adapts. Give it time.

### Phase 3: Event Simulation (Weeks 11-14)

Volume: 15-18 hours/week. Peak block. The long ride extends to 7-8 hours by week 12 and 8-10 hours by week 13 or 14 — ideally with 3,500-4,500m of climbing. This is the over-distance ride. It is non-negotiable. Riders who have never ridden beyond 7 hours in training arrive at the Tour des Stations guessing about hours 10-17. The guess is wrong.

One weekend in this phase should be a genuine simulation: a 9-10 hour day with full race nutrition, race clothing, race pacing. Practise riding in the dark for 30-60 minutes if you have not done it before — you will start this event before sunrise. The [Fuel Planner](/tools/fuel-planner) calculates your exact hourly requirements across the full event duration.

Back-to-back days continue: 7-8 hours Saturday, 4-5 hours Sunday. Core work maintains at three sessions per week.

### Phase 4: Taper (Weeks 15-16)

Volume drops to 60 per cent of peak in week 15, 40 per cent in week 16. Two short rides with 10-minute openers at threshold to keep the legs sharp. One ride of 3-4 hours with moderate climbing in week 15. Everything else is recovery, sleep, hydration, and carb loading. The fitness is banked. Trust it.

## Key Sessions

**The Long Endurance Ride.** Build progressively: 4 hours in week 1, 5.5 hours by week 5, 7-8 hours by week 12, 8-10 hours by week 14. Always with climbing. Always with race nutrition. The goal is not speed — it is duration at sustainable intensity. Zone 2 with the occasional short tempo effort on climbs. If you are riding the long ride at tempo or above, you are missing the point.

**Back-to-Back Days.** Saturday long ride, Sunday 60-70 per cent of Saturday's duration on tired legs. The Sunday ride teaches you what riding at hour 10 feels like — heavy legs, reduced motivation, a lower power ceiling than you expect. Train in that state. Learn what 65 per cent of FTP feels like when the legs have 5 hours of climbing from yesterday still in them.

**Sustained Sub-Threshold Climbing.** 2x25 minutes at 82-88 per cent FTP on real gradient, building to 2x35 minutes by peak block. Recovery between efforts: 10 minutes spinning. This is the Sanetsch simulation — long, steady climbing on tired legs at a wattage you can maintain for an hour, not a wattage that impresses for 10 minutes.

## Nutrition Strategy

Here's where it gets really interesting — and where most riders who fail this event actually fail it.

The arithmetic is stark. A 75kg rider across 15 hours will burn 10,000-14,000 calories. Your body stores approximately 2,000-2,500 calories of glycogen. The gap must be filled by eating on the bike — roughly 8,000-11,000 calories over 15 hours, which means consuming 80-100g of carbohydrate per hour, every hour, without fail.

This is not a target you hit on race day without preparation. Gut training is as important as leg training. Start at 60g per hour in Phase 1 and build to 90-100g per hour by Phase 3. Use a mix: drink mix (40-50g per hour as baseline), gels (one every 30-45 minutes), bars or rice cakes (every 90 minutes), and real food from feed stations — sandwiches, bananas, Swiss bread with jam.

Flavour fatigue is real across 15 hours. If you have only practised with one brand of gel, you will want to throw it into a ravine by hour 8. Rotate flavours. Alternate sweet and savoury. A ham sandwich at a feed station at hour 10 is not a luxury — it is a psychological lifeline.

The feed stations on the Tour des Stations are well-organised, but the sheer duration of the event means you must be self-sufficient between them. Carry 2-3 hours of food at all times. Running out between stations at hour 12 is a crisis, not an inconvenience.

## Equipment and Gear

**Layering.** 12-16 degrees at the 4am start, 25-30 degrees in the Rhone valley by midday, 5-8 degrees at summit passes above 2,000m. You will cycle through all of these conditions multiple times. A packable gilet and arm warmers are the minimum. A thin rain jacket weighs 80g and saves your day if a storm rolls through at 2,200m. Long-finger gloves for the pre-dawn start and for the cold summit descents.

**Lights.** Front and rear, both charged the night before. You need 60-90 minutes of battery for the pre-dawn start.

**Gearing.** 34x32 minimum. 34x34 if your derailleur accommodates it. This is not a day for machismo gearing. Test your easiest gear on a 10 per cent gradient at the end of a 6-hour ride. If you are grinding at 45 RPM, change the cassette. Use the [Gear Ratio Calculator](/tools/gear-ratio) to compare options.

**Food storage.** You need to carry more food than a normal sportive. A top-tube bag or frame bag for bars and rice cakes. Three rear jersey pockets full. Plan where every item goes before race morning.

**Tyres.** 28mm or 30mm if your frame clears them. Grip and comfort matter more than marginal aerodynamic gains on a 15-hour day. Tubeless with sealant for puncture resilience.

## Race Day Pacing

Ultra-conservative. That is the entire pacing strategy, and it is precisely what separates the riders who finish from the riders who abandon.

Ride the first 100km at talking pace. Zone 2. The first half of this event is positioning for the second half. Nothing more.

Pace on power, not heart rate. Heart rate drifts upward at altitude and across ultra-long durations — it becomes unreliable after 8-10 hours. Set a ceiling of 60-65 per cent of FTP for the sustained climbing sections. Do not exceed it regardless of who passes you.

Save everything for the final 60km. If you arrive at the base of the Sanetsch with matches still to burn, you have paced correctly.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Going too hard in the first 100km.** Fresh legs, cool morning air, excitement — all of it conspires to push you above sustainable wattage. The riders who fly past you at 6am are the same riders you will pass at 3pm, sitting at the side of the road with empty bottles and empty expressions. Discipline in the first third is the entire race.

**2. Not training the gut.** You cannot absorb 90g of carbohydrate per hour on race day if you have never practised it in training. The gut adapts over weeks, not hours. Start gut training in week 1 of your preparation. If you arrive at the Tour des Stations having never consumed more than 40g per hour, you will bonk. It is not a question of if.

**3. Inadequate clothing for altitude cold.** August in Sion is 28 degrees. August at the Col du Sanetsch summit is 5-8 degrees. Add wind chill on the descent at 50-60 km/h and you are looking at conditions that cause genuine hypothermia on fatigued bodies. A gilet, arm warmers, and a rain jacket weigh 200g total. Carry them. Every time.

**4. Choosing the wrong route distance.** If your longest training ride has been 6 hours, the 267km route will break you — not because you lack toughness, but because 14-17 hours on the bike is a physiological demand that requires specific preparation. The 200km route at 6,000m of climbing is still one of the hardest days you will ever have on a bike. Choose it if it matches your fitness. Finish strong.

**5. Not practising riding in darkness.** You start at 4am. If you have never ridden in the dark, the first 60-90 minutes will feel disorienting. Do at least two pre-dawn training rides in the final six weeks. It removes the novelty and lets you focus on pacing and nutrition from the gun.

## Travel and Logistics

Sion is the capital of the Valais canton, roughly 2.5 hours by car from Geneva or Zurich airports. Swiss rail carries bikes — book a reservation in advance during August. Accommodation in Sion books quickly around the event; secure it as soon as your entry is confirmed. Nearby Rhone valley towns offer alternatives.

Entry for the full route is approximately CHF150-200. Registration typically opens in late winter or early spring, and places fill.

The event atmosphere is distinctly Swiss: precise organisation, excellent feed stations, medical support on course, and a genuine mountain-sport community rather than a commercial sportive machine.

Arrive 2-3 days early if possible. Ride a section of the early route at a tourist pace. Eat well. Sleep well. Check your bike. The preparation is done. Trust the work.

## Recovery Plan

Let me be really clear about this: the Tour des Stations demands a recovery proportionate to its severity. A 14-17 hour day with 8,000m of climbing inflicts muscular damage, glycogen depletion, and systemic fatigue that a single rest day does not repair.

Take a full week off the bike. Complete rest for the first three days — walking, light stretching, sleep. Days 4-7, gentle movement only: a 30-minute walk, hip mobility, easy swimming if it appeals. In week 2, ride twice, both under 90 minutes, both conversational. Full training resumes in week 3 at the earliest, starting at 60 per cent of your pre-event volume.

Nutrition in the first 48 hours post-event matters. Carbohydrate-rich meals, protein at every sitting, hydration with electrolytes, and one indulgent meal of whatever you want. You earned it.

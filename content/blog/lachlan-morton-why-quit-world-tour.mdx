**Lachlan Morton is an Australian professional cyclist on EF Pro Cycling's 2026 Far Beyond programme.** He is best known for turning a conventional road career into a professional mix of gravel racing, ultra-distance rides and record attempts without retiring from the sport or leaving EF.

The phrase “why he quit the WorldTour” makes a strong podcast hook, but it compresses a more interesting career. Morton left Garmin-Sharp's conventional road path after 2014, raced for Jelly Belly, returned to top-level road racing with Dimension Data and joined EF in 2019. What he ultimately left was the standard road calendar, not professional cycling itself.

## Lachlan Morton: quick facts

- **Born:** 2 January 1992 in Australia; UCI nationality record: Australian.
- **Current programme:** EF Pro Cycling Far Beyond in 2026.
- **Road-team history:** Garmin-Sharp, Jelly Belly, Dimension Data and EF.
- **Alt Tour:** roughly 5,500km in 2021, including Tour stages and transfers, unsupported.
- **Unbound:** winner of the 2024 200-mile race in 9:11:47; second in the 2025 Unbound XL.
- **Tour Divide route:** 2,670 miles in 12 days, 12 hours and 21 minutes in 2024.
- **Around Australia:** at least 14,200km in 30 days, 9 hours and 59 minutes in 2024.

## Did Lachlan Morton really quit the WorldTour?

Not in the simple, permanent sense implied by the phrase.

The [UCI rider record](https://www.uci.org/rider-details/75973) lists Morton with Garmin-Sharp from 2012 to 2014, Jelly Belly in 2015 and 2016, Dimension Data in 2017 and 2018, and EF from 2019. EF then announced in 2024 that his journey with the organisation would continue. Its [2026 Far Beyond announcement](https://www.efprocycling.com/culture/axelle-dubau-prevot-and-lachlan-morton-will-race-far-beyond-their-old-frontiers-in-2026/) presents him as a current EF athlete racing road, gravel and off-road events alongside new expeditions.

Morton's own explanation is personal rather than administrative. In the [full Roadman interview](/podcast/ep-21-my-untold-story-about-why-i-quit-world-tour-lachlan-morton), he says performing at his best on the road required a version of himself that was isolated and single-minded enough to damage other parts of life. He describes the change as gradual: bikepacking trips with his brother revealed another way to ride, and EF's alternative calendar eventually gave that direction a professional structure.

That is first-person testimony. It explains why he changed course; it does not mean every professional rider experiences the WorldTour in the same way.

## The Alt Tour: stages plus transfers

In 2021 Morton rode the Tour de France route outside the race. The crucial detail is that he also rode the transfers between stages. EF's [current official profile](https://www.efprocycling.com/our-team/lachlan-morton/) records roughly 5,500km, a solo unsupported ride and an arrival in Paris five days before the peloton.

The project also raised more than $700,000 for World Bicycle Relief. That charitable result is part of why the Alt Tour became more than an endurance stunt: it linked Morton's alternative calendar to a public purpose beyond a finishing time.

## Unbound Gravel: a verified race win

Morton's 2024 Unbound Gravel 200 result is a conventional win, even if the route and culture sit outside traditional road racing. EF's [official race report](https://www.efprocycling.com/racing/lachlan-morton-wins-the-2024-unbound-gravel/) records a 9:11:47 finish after Morton and Chad Haga reached the closing kilometres together.

The result matters to his career story because it shows the alternative calendar was not a retreat from competition. Morton used the road-racing skills he had accumulated—positioning, long-range attacks and a small-group sprint—to win the race that had helped start EF's alternative programme in 2019. In 2025, EF records him finishing second in the 560km Unbound XL.

## Tour Divide: speed with planned sleep

In 2024 Morton completed the Tour Divide route from Banff to Antelope Wells in 12 days, 12 hours and 21 minutes. EF's [ride record](https://www.efprocycling.com/culture/the-great-divide-mountain-bike-route/) lists 2,670 miles and 192,000 feet of climbing.

His approach is as important as the time. Morton planned 12 hours of rest in every 48, aiming for roughly six hours of sleep a night instead of minimising sleep at any cost. EF's pre-ride account also acknowledged debate over what counts as an official self-supported record when a media crew documents the ride. We therefore report the route and elapsed time rather than presenting an unqualified race record.

## Around Australia in 30 days

Morton's supported Around Australia attempt started and finished in Port Macquarie in September and October 2024. The [official EF completion report](https://www.efprocycling.com/culture/lachlan-morton-sets-the-around-australia-record/) records at least 14,200km in 30 days, 9 hours and 59 minutes—close to a week faster than the previous mark.

He averaged more than 450km per day. A support crew handled food, clothing, bike maintenance and logistics, so this result should not be described as self-supported. The ride also raised more than $127,000 AUD for the Indigenous Literacy Foundation by the date of EF's report.

## What does Lachlan Morton do now?

In 2026 Morton remains part of EF Pro Cycling's Far Beyond programme. EF describes a calendar spanning far-flung road races, major gravel events, remote fastest-known-time attempts and filmed expeditions. That is the cleanest current answer to “what team is Lachlan Morton on?”: he is an EF professional, but his role is not the standard WorldTour road schedule most fans associate with the team.

The Roadman interview adds the athlete's perspective. Morton says he now relies heavily on feel and curiosity and uses metrics as tools rather than the purpose of training. Those are his reported practices, not a universal training prescription. A time-crunched amateur should not infer that removing structure automatically recreates an elite rider's durability.

## Why the story matters

Morton's career challenges a false binary: stay in conventional road racing or stop being serious. He kept the professional support, performance standards and competitive ambition, but changed the calendar and the measures of success.

For an amateur, the transferable part is not the volume. It is the decision framework. Decide what kind of riding matters, build enough structure to prepare for it, and keep the training from consuming the reason you chose the goal.

It is 5:27am. The alarm went off three minutes ago and you have already turned it off, swung your legs out of bed, and sat there for a moment in the dark doing the maths. If you are on the turbo by 5:35, you can get a solid 50-minute session done, shower, and be standing in the kitchen making porridge by 6:40 — fifteen minutes before the first small person appears at the top of the stairs asking for Weetabix. The baby monitor sits propped against a water bottle on the handlebars. Your partner is still asleep. Nobody knows you are awake.

This is what training looks like when you have kids. Not a structured 12-week plan pinned to the fridge. Not a Saturday morning club run with coffee afterwards. It is a constant, quiet negotiation between the person you want to be on the bike and the person you need to be at home. And the thing nobody tells you — the part that no training article or YouTube video ever mentions — is that the hardest part is not the physical work. It is the guilt.

I have had this conversation with hundreds of riders through the podcast and the community. Emails that land at 11pm from blokes who just want someone to tell them it is okay to still want this. It is okay. But it requires a different system than the one the cycling internet sells you.

## The Guilt Problem (Let's Address It First)

Before we talk about sessions, scheduling, turbo trainers, or training zones, we need to talk about the thing that derails more parent cyclists than any lack of fitness. Guilt.

You feel it when you clip in and your three-year-old is pressing their face against the window. You feel it when your partner says "it's fine, go ride" in a tone that suggests it is not entirely fine. You feel it on a Sunday morning when you could be at the playground but instead you are 40 kilometres from home chasing a segment time. And you feel it in the other direction too — when you skip a ride to be present, and then spend the afternoon irritable because you have not moved your body in four days and the walls are closing in.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about this guilt: it is not a sign that you are selfish. It is a sign that you care about being a good parent. Selfish people do not feel guilty. The fact that you are wrestling with it means your priorities are in the right place. The problem is not the guilt itself — it is what you do with it.

Most riders handle it badly. They either train in secret — sneaking out, downplaying how long they were gone — or they stop training altogether, telling themselves they will get back to it when the kids are older. Both approaches fail. The secret trainer builds resentment in their partner. The martyr builds resentment in themselves.

The fix is transparency. Your training needs to be visible, negotiated, and reciprocal. Not because training requires permission — you are an adult — but because a household with young children is a team operation, and unilateral decisions about time create friction that compounds over months and years.

When I had Joe Friel on the podcast, he made a point that stuck with me: "The athletes who train consistently into their fifties are the ones who solved the relationship problem, not the training problem." He was not being flippant. He was describing a pattern he had seen across decades of coaching masters riders. The ones who stayed fit were not the most talented. They were the ones whose families supported the training — and that support was earned, not assumed.

Reframe the training. You are not escaping from your family to go cycling. You are investing in your physical health, your mental health, and your capacity to be a better, more patient, more present parent. A 45-minute ride at 5:30am costs nobody anything and gives you back your equilibrium for the entire day. Frame it that way — to yourself and to your partner — and the guilt starts to lose its grip.

## The 5am Window: Your Most Valuable Training Asset

If there is one piece of practical advice that transforms the parent cyclist's week, it is this: train before your family wakes up.

The early morning window — 5:00 to 6:30am, depending on when your household stirs — is the single most reliable training slot available to you. It does not compete with family time. It does not require childcare. It does not eat into your partner's weekend. It is yours, entirely, at no cost to anyone except your own sleep.

The cost is real, though, and it needs to be managed. Getting up at 5am to ride works if — and only if — you are in bed by 9:30pm the night before. Seven hours of sleep is the minimum for training to produce adaptation rather than accumulated fatigue. Six and a half is survivable occasionally. Anything less than six on a regular basis and you are training yourself into a hole that no interval session can dig you out of.

Here is the protocol that works:

**The night before:** Kit laid out. Shoes, bibs, jersey, heart rate strap. All of it ready to go in the room where the turbo lives. Bottles filled. The goal is to reduce the number of decisions between the alarm and the first pedal stroke to zero. Every decision point is an opportunity for your sleep-deprived brain to negotiate its way back to bed.

**The alarm:** One alarm. Across the room so you have to stand up to turn it off. Once you are standing, you are committed.

**The first ten minutes:** Do not think about the session. Get on the bike and pedal. Five minutes at 50% FTP — just moving, waking the body up. By minute ten, the fog lifts. By minute fifteen, you are glad you got up.

**The session:** Forty-five to sixty minutes. Enough for a warm-up, a meaningful main set, and a cool-down. On the turbo, 45 minutes of structured work delivers more training stimulus than 90 minutes of outdoor riding that included soft-pedalling to the main road, waiting at traffic lights, and a headwind home.

**The finish:** Off the bike by 6:15 or 6:30. Shower. Dressed. Downstairs making breakfast when the kids wake up. Your partner did not have to solo-parent while you rode. The only casualty was Netflix after 9pm, and you were not watching anything good anyway.

Three mornings a week on this schedule gives you 2.5 to 3 hours of quality training before most people have opened their eyes. Over 48 weeks, that is 120 to 144 hours of structured work. Enough to build real fitness. Enough to race on. Enough to hold your own in a sportive or a club time trial. All of it invisible to your family's daily life.

## The Turbo Trainer Changes the Equation

Here is the good news that most parent cyclists do not hear often enough: an indoor trainer is the most family-compatible piece of cycling equipment you will ever own.

The outdoor ride is beautiful, irreplaceable, and logistically expensive. It takes time to get to the good roads. You need daylight. You need dry weather. You need to tell your partner how long you will be, and that estimate is always wrong because you forgot about the headwind on the way back. And if something goes wrong — puncture, mechanical, bonk — you are stuck 30 kilometres from home while your partner is trying to get two kids fed and out the door.

The turbo eliminates all of that. You are in the spare room, the garage, or the shed. You are 10 metres from your children. If someone cries, you can hear them. If your partner needs a hand, you are there in 90 seconds. No travel time, no weather dependency, no risk of being stranded.

And the quality is better. A 45-minute structured session on the turbo — 10 minutes warm-up, 2x12 minutes at sweet spot with 4 minutes recovery, 7 minutes cool-down — delivers a precise, uninterrupted training stimulus. No freewheeling. No junctions. Every minute on the turbo is a working minute. The rider doing 45 minutes on the turbo three times a week is accumulating more productive training stress than the rider doing one two-hour outdoor ride at the weekend with nothing in between.

You do not need the most expensive setup. A direct-drive smart trainer and a screen are enough. Zwift, TrainerRoad, or a workout loaded from [TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com) — it does not matter. What matters is that the session is structured and the time is used well.

I am not saying never ride outside. Outdoor riding is where the joy lives, and joy is what keeps you in the sport for decades. But for weekday training sessions — the bread and butter of your fitness — the turbo is your friend, not a compromise.

## The Weekly Negotiation

The conversation that saves more training plans than any coaching methodology: the Sunday evening schedule review.

Sit down with your partner once a week — Sunday evening works well, before the week starts — and talk through the next seven days. Not just your training. Everything. Who is doing school drop-off on which days. Who has a work commitment on Wednesday evening. When your partner wants to go to the gym, see a friend, have an hour to themselves. And yes, which mornings you are getting up early to ride.

This conversation needs to happen even if your partner says they do not mind when you ride. Especially then. Because "I don't mind" often means "I don't mind right now, but three months of you disappearing every Saturday morning without asking will become a problem." The resentment that kills training programmes does not arrive as an argument. It arrives as a slow, quiet withdrawal of support. One day your partner stops saying "have a good ride" and starts saying nothing at all. That is the warning sign most cyclists miss.

Here is the framework that works:

**Be specific about times.** "I will be on the turbo from 5:15 to 6:00" is better than "I'm going to ride in the morning." Vagueness feels like a lack of respect for their time.

**Offer reciprocity.** If you are taking Saturday morning for a three-hour ride, your partner gets Sunday morning — or a weekday evening, or whatever time they want. The currency does not have to be exercise. It has to be time that is genuinely theirs, without children, without responsibility. Non-negotiable.

**Stick to the plan.** If you said you would be back by 7:30, be back by 7:30. Not 7:45. Not 8:00 with a mumbled excuse about a headwind. Coming home late is the fastest way to erode trust. Set realistic times and honour them. Every time.

**Talk about the bad weeks.** Some weeks, training is not possible. A child is ill. Work explodes. Your partner is running on empty. On those weeks, you skip the ride, you do not complain about it, and you show up fully. Your fitness can absorb a missed week. Your relationship cannot absorb a partner who resents every hour you spend on the bike.

## What a Realistic Training Week Looks Like

The training week changes shape as your kids grow. Here is what it looks like at three different stages.

### Toddlers (Ages 0-4): The Survival Phase

This is the hardest phase for training. Sleep is unreliable. Routines are fragile. You are tired in a way that coffee cannot fix. The goal here is not progression — it is maintenance. Keep the habit alive. Keep the body moving. Accept that this is a holding pattern, not a building phase.

- **Monday:** Rest
- **Tuesday:** 45 min turbo, 5:15-6:00am. Sweet spot intervals: 10 min warm-up, 3x8 min at 88-93% FTP with 3 min recovery, 5 min cool-down
- **Wednesday:** Rest or 20 min easy spin if the baby slept
- **Thursday:** 45 min turbo, 5:15-6:00am. VO2max: 10 min warm-up, 5x3 min at 115% FTP with 3 min recovery, 5 min cool-down
- **Friday:** Rest
- **Saturday:** 90 min outdoor ride (negotiated with partner — they get Sunday morning)
- **Sunday:** Family day

**Weekly total:** 3-4 hours. It does not feel like enough, but it is. Three sessions a week through the toddler years keeps your aerobic base intact and means you are not starting from zero when the children start sleeping through the night.

### Primary School (Ages 5-10): The Building Phase

Sleep is back. Routines are predictable. Children are in school from 9am. The morning window is now genuinely reliable, and weekend rides become easier to negotiate because the kids are old enough to be occupied.

- **Monday:** Rest or 30 min easy spin
- **Tuesday:** 60 min turbo, 5:00-6:00am. Threshold intervals: 10 min warm-up, 3x10 min at 95-100% FTP with 5 min recovery, 10 min cool-down
- **Wednesday:** 45 min turbo, 5:15-6:00am. Zone 2 endurance
- **Thursday:** 60 min turbo, 5:00-6:00am. VO2max: 10 min warm-up, 6x3 min at 115-120% FTP with 3 min recovery, 10 min cool-down
- **Friday:** Rest
- **Saturday:** 2.5-3 hr outdoor ride (morning, back by lunchtime)
- **Sunday:** Family ride with the kids, 45-60 min on quiet roads or cycle paths

**Weekly total:** 6-8 hours. This is the sweet spot for serious amateur training. Two hard sessions, one endurance session, one easy spin, and a family ride that doubles as recovery and bonding. You can race on this. You can target events on this. You can get genuinely fast.

### Teenagers (Ages 11+): The Expansion Phase

Teenagers do not need you minute-by-minute. They sleep later than you do. The negotiation shifts from childcare logistics to being present for the moments that matter — school events, weekend activities, the conversations that happen unpredictably.

- **Monday:** Rest or 45 min easy spin
- **Tuesday:** 75 min turbo. Threshold and tempo work
- **Wednesday:** 60 min turbo. Zone 2 or recovery
- **Thursday:** 75 min turbo. VO2max or race-specific work
- **Friday:** Rest
- **Saturday:** 3-4 hr outdoor ride (group ride, club run, or solo)
- **Sunday:** Active recovery or a ride with your teenager if they are interested

**Weekly total:** 8-10 hours. Close to what a serious age-group racer needs. The constraint is no longer childcare — it is the balance between cycling, work, and being present for a family that still needs you, even if they pretend they do not.

## School Holidays, Illness, and the Art of the Fallback Session

The training plan does not know that your child has been sent home from school with a temperature. It does not know that it is half-term and you have no childcare. It does not know that the baby was up four times last night and you physically cannot get out of bed at 5am.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about training through the parenting years: disruption is not the exception. It is the environment. You are not training around obstacles — you are training inside them. And the programme that works is the one that expects this and has fallback options built in.

**The 20-minute fallback.** When everything collapses and you have 20 minutes — exactly 20 minutes while your partner takes the baby for a walk around the block — you can still do meaningful work. A 3-minute warm-up, then 4x2 minutes at 120% FTP with 90 seconds recovery, then a 3-minute cool-down. That is 20 minutes. It is not a full session. But it maintains the neuromuscular connection, keeps the legs sharp, and — most importantly — keeps the training habit intact. A 20-minute session on a day when you planned 60 minutes is infinitely more valuable than no session at all.

**The bodyweight option.** When the turbo is impossible — in-laws' house over Christmas, baby napping in the room where the trainer lives — a 15-minute bodyweight circuit is a legitimate session. Press-ups, lunges, single-leg step-ups, planks, glute bridges. It is not cycling, but it supports cycling, and it keeps you in the rhythm of doing something every day.

**School holidays.** The six-week summer holiday is the biggest training challenge of the year. Childcare disappears. Routines evaporate. The early morning window still works, but the long weekend ride becomes much harder to negotiate when your partner has been solo-parenting all week. Plan for reduced training during school holidays. Drop from four sessions to three, or from three to two. Shorten the weekend ride. Increase the family rides. Accept that July and August might be maintenance months, and build your season plan around it. The riders who try to hold peak training through the school holidays either burn out or burn through their partner's goodwill.

**Illness.** When your child is ill, you do not train. That is not a suggestion. When a sick child needs a parent, the bike can wait. No session is more important than being there when your kid is frightened and unwell. The fitness loss from three or four days off the bike is negligible. The relationship damage from not being present is not.

When you are ill yourself — which happens more often when you have germ factories attending primary school — use the neck rule. Above the neck (head cold, sniffles), an easy 30-minute spin is fine. Below the neck (chest infection, fever, body aches), full rest until symptoms clear.

## Bringing the Kids Along

The best long-term strategy for training as a parent cyclist is to make cycling a family sport. Not because it gives you more training time — it does not. A family ride at your four-year-old's pace is not a training ride. But it transforms the cultural position of cycling within your household from "the thing that takes Dad away" to "the thing we do together."

That shift changes everything.

**Age two: balance bikes.** Get them on a balance bike as early as they can walk steadily. Not a bike with stabilisers — a balance bike. They learn to balance first, steer second, pedal later. And when your toddler is scooting along the pavement on a tiny bike while you walk alongside, cycling has become a shared activity without you having to clip in.

**Ages four to five: tag-along bikes or trail-a-bikes.** These attach to the back of your bike and let the child pedal (or not) while you do the work. You will not set any records. But you can ride to the park, to a cafe, along a canal towpath. The child is with you, on a bike, moving. The seeds are planted.

**Ages six to eight: their own bike, short family rides.** Quiet roads, cycle paths, rail trails. Two to five kilometres is plenty at first. Stop for ice cream. Let them ring the bell 400 times. Do not coach their pedalling technique. Let them ride.

**Ages nine to twelve: longer family rides.** A cycling-literate child can comfortably ride 15 to 25 kilometres on quiet roads. Family sportives start to become realistic. Riding with your child at this age is one of the most rewarding things in the sport.

**Bike maintenance.** Let them help. Give them a rag and let them clean the chain. Let them hold the torque wrench (supervised, obviously). Let them pump up the tyres. When they help maintain the bike, they develop ownership of the activity. They are not watching you disappear into the garage — they are part of what happens in there.

**Watching racing.** The Tour de France in July is a gift to the parent cyclist. Watch it with your kids. Explain the breakaway. Let them pick a favourite rider. If they develop even a passing interest in professional cycling, the entire dynamic around your training changes. You are not doing a strange thing that nobody else in the house understands. You are doing the thing they watched on the telly last Sunday.

## Race Prep When You Can't Train Like a Single Person

The internet says you need 12 to 15 hours a week and a six-month build. Which is fine if you are 25 and single. Less fine if you have a child who needs collecting from school at 3:15pm.

**Pick fewer events.** The child-free rider can target eight races across a season. You can target two or three. Pick the ones that matter, build towards them, and let the rest go.

**Extend the timeline.** Instead of a 12-week build, think 20 to 24 weeks. Longer timelines absorb the missed weeks that are inevitable with young children. If you lose a week to chicken pox in week six, you have 14 weeks left instead of six.

**Train the demands of the event.** Targeting a hilly sportive? Your limited sessions need climbing-specific work — sustained efforts at 90-95% FTP on the turbo with gradient simulation. Time trial? Threshold work at race-specific power. You do not have the volume to train everything, so train the thing that matters most.

**Adjust your expectations, not your ambition.** You can do a 160-kilometre sportive on six hours a week of training. You will suffer in the final third. But you will finish respectably, because six months of consistent work beats crash-course 15-hour weeks.

**Use the family as motivation, not as the obstacle.** When your seven-year-old hands you a water bottle at the feed station, when you cross the line and the first person you see is someone you love — that is why you do this. The training and the family are not in competition. They are the same project.

## The Long View: Why Consistency Beats Volume

Here is the perspective that changes everything, and it is the most important section of this entire piece.

The riders who are still fast at 50 are not the ones who trained the most in their thirties. They are the ones who trained the most consistently. Three sessions a week, every week, for ten years beats six sessions a week for six months followed by burnout and a bike that sits in the garage for two years.

I have seen this pattern hundreds of times through the podcast. The rider who did 8,000 kilometres a year for ten years is fitter than the rider who did 15,000 for three years and then stopped. Every session you do this week builds on the session you did last week. Miss too many weeks and you break the chain. Keep the chain intact — even if some links are smaller than others — and the adaptation accumulates in ways that surprise you.

Joe Friel's work with masters athletes hammers this home. After 40, the body's ability to recover from high-volume training diminishes. What does not diminish is its ability to respond to consistent, moderate training applied week after week. Friel's observation is that frequency is the primary driver of maintained fitness in the masters population. Not intensity. Not volume. Frequency.

Your children will not be small forever. The toddler phase lasts three or four years. The primary school phase lasts six. The question is whether you have maintained the habit, the base fitness, and the desire through those years — or whether you let it go and face rebuilding from scratch in your late forties.

Protect the minimum. Three sessions a week. Every week. Through holidays, through school plays, through sleepless nights. Some weeks those sessions will be 20 minutes on the turbo at 5am. Some weeks they will be proper 75-minute interval sessions. Both count. Both add to the chain.

You are not done yet. Not even close. But the path through the parenting years is not the one the cycling internet describes. It is quieter, more pragmatic, full of compromise and early alarms and sessions done in the dark while the house sleeps. And it works — because the constraints teach you something child-free riders often learn too late: that time is finite, that every session matters, and that the best training plan is the one you can sustain.

Your kids will grow up watching you ride. They will see you get up early, put in the work, come home energised, and show up for them. That is the example you are setting. And one day, they might clip in beside you — and every 5am alarm will have been worth it.

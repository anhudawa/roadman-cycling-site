You had a plan. Maybe it was the Etape, maybe it was a local sportive you'd circled on the calendar since January, maybe it was just the quiet goal of holding a number on the power meter that proved something to yourself. You were training. It was working. You could feel the form building week by week.

And then something happened.

A crash on a wet descent. A virus that knocked you sideways for three weeks. A new job that swallowed every morning you used to spend on the bike. A knee that just stopped cooperating. Whatever it was, the riding stopped — and every day since has felt like watching money burn.

I have been through this. More than once. I have spoken to hundreds of riders in [our community](/coaching) who have been through it too, and I have had World Tour coaches, sports scientists, and physiotherapists on the podcast explain what is actually happening — physiologically and psychologically — when a setback hits. The pattern is remarkably consistent. And the mistakes riders make in response are even more consistent.

This is the guide I wish someone had given me. Not platitudes. Not "everything happens for a reason." A practical framework for understanding what you have lost, what you have kept, and how to get moving again without creating a second setback that is worse than the first.

## Setbacks Are Not Optional

Here is the thing nobody tells you when you start taking cycling seriously: setbacks are not a risk factor. They are a certainty.

Every single rider who trains consistently for more than a couple of years will deal with at least one significant interruption. Most will deal with several. Illness, injury, life changes, performance plateaus that feel like hitting a wall — these are not signs that you are doing something wrong. They are the cost of doing something meaningful with your body over a sustained period of time.

The riders who last in this sport are not the ones who avoid setbacks. They are the ones who handle them well. That distinction matters more than any interval session or nutrition protocol I could write about.

Professor Stephen Seiler made a point on the podcast that stuck with me. He said the difference between the amateurs who sustain long-term performance and the ones who fade away is almost never talent or genetics. It is how they respond to the inevitable interruptions. The ones who treat a setback as a catastrophe tend to create a pattern — panic, rush back, get hurt again, lose more time, eventually drift away from the sport entirely. The ones who treat it as information tend to come back stronger.

## The Four Types of Setback

Not all setbacks are the same, and the response needs to match the problem. Let me break this down.

**Injury.** The most concrete and in some ways the simplest to deal with. You have a specific thing that is broken or damaged. There is usually a timeline. There is a rehabilitation pathway. The challenge is patience and the psychological weight of forced inactivity.

**Illness.** More insidious than injury because the timeline is less defined. A cold might cost you five days. A viral infection can linger for weeks, and the temptation to ride through it is enormous — and enormously counterproductive. Illness also carries a specific cardiovascular risk on return that most riders underestimate.

**Life changes.** A new job. A new baby. A relationship that needs your attention. Moving house. Caring for a parent. These are not temporary interruptions — they are permanent restructurings of your available time and energy. The riders who treat them as temporary problems to push through are the ones who burn out.

**Performance plateau or missed target event.** You did everything right and the result did not come. Your FTP has not moved in six months. The event you trained for got cancelled, or you could not start, or you started and had a terrible day. The fitness is there but the purpose has evaporated. This one is underestimated because there is no physical problem to point to — it is entirely psychological, and it is devastating.

## What Actually Happens to Your Fitness

Here is the good news, and I mean this straight: it is almost never as bad as it feels.

The science of detraining is well-studied, and the numbers are less frightening than the anxiety in your head is telling you. When you stop training completely, here is what happens on a rough timeline:

**Days 1-7.** Plasma volume drops. This is why you feel flat almost immediately — your blood literally has less volume to carry oxygen. But this reverses within days of resuming training. It is a fluid shift, not a structural loss.

**Weeks 1-2.** Aerobic capacity (VO2max) decreases by roughly 5-7 per cent. This sounds significant until you realise that it means a rider with a VO2max of 55 ml/kg/min drops to about 51-52. Noticeable? Yes. Catastrophic? No.

**Weeks 2-4.** The decline slows. VO2max continues to drop at roughly 1 per cent per week. Mitochondrial density starts to decrease. Lactate threshold drops slightly. But here is what most people miss — muscular endurance and neuromuscular coordination are far more resilient. Your muscles remember the movement patterns. The capillary network that delivers oxygen to working muscles takes months to fully regress.

**Beyond 4 weeks.** The losses continue but at a diminishing rate. A rider who takes two months completely off the bike does not lose twice as much fitness as someone who takes one month off. The curve flattens.

And here is the part that really matters: **previously trained athletes regain fitness significantly faster than they built it.** The myonuclei in your muscle fibres — the extra control centres for protein synthesis that you developed through training — persist for years. The neural pathways that coordinate your pedal stroke persist. The capillary infrastructure persists in a reduced but recoverable state.

You are not starting from zero. You have never been starting from zero. Your body has a memory, and it is waiting to be reminded.

## The Psychological Response Nobody Talks About

Losing fitness feels like losing a part of your identity. And if you are the kind of rider who reads articles like this one, cycling is probably more than a hobby to you. It is how you start your day. It is your stress management. It is the thing that makes you feel like the version of yourself you actually want to be.

When that gets taken away — whether by injury, illness, or life — there is a grief response. And I am not using that word loosely. It follows a recognisable pattern: denial (riding through pain or illness), anger (frustration at the situation, at your body, at the unfairness of the timing), bargaining (maybe if I just do easy spins on the turbo it will be fine), depression (withdrawal from the cycling community, loss of routine, feeling like you have lost something important), and eventually acceptance.

Most riders get stuck somewhere between anger and bargaining. They refuse to accept the scope of the setback, and that refusal drives the single most damaging behaviour in the entire cycle.

Let me be really clear about this: the feelings are not the problem. Frustration is rational. Grief is rational. You trained hard, you sacrificed, you were making progress, and now that progress is under threat. Anyone who tells you to just "stay positive" has never had their A-race taken away by a broken collarbone six weeks out.

The problem is not the feeling. The problem is what the feeling drives you to do next.

## The Panicked Return

This is the mistake I see more than any other, and it is the one that does the most damage.

A rider gets injured. They take two weeks off, which feels like an eternity. The anxiety about losing fitness builds every day. By day 14, they cannot stand it any more. They get back on the bike and immediately try to make up for lost time. Hard group ride on Saturday. Intervals on Tuesday. Long ride Sunday. Within ten days, the original injury flares back up — or a new one appears because compensatory movement patterns have loaded something that was not ready.

The second setback is almost always longer than the first. And the psychological hit is worse because now there is a layer of self-blame on top of the original frustration.

The same pattern plays out with illness. A rider gets a chest infection. After a week, they feel 80 per cent better and convince themselves that 80 per cent is close enough. They do a hard ride. The infection comes roaring back, or — and this is the really dangerous scenario — they develop post-viral cardiac complications because they loaded a cardiovascular system that was still fighting inflammation.

I had a sports cardiologist on the podcast who said something I think about regularly. He said the number one predictor of a serious cardiac event in a returning athlete is not the severity of the original illness. It is how soon they return to high-intensity training afterwards. The heart needs time to clear inflammation. Pushing through that window is not tough. It is reckless.

Here is the rule: **if you are debating whether you are ready to ride hard, you are not ready to ride hard.** When you are properly recovered, the question does not arise. You just feel right.

## A Structured Return Protocol

Whether your setback was injury, illness, or an extended break for any reason, the return follows the same principles. Volume first. Intensity later. Consistency over ambition.

**Week 1.** Cut your pre-setback volume by 50 per cent. All riding in Zone 1-2. No group rides. No Strava segments. If you were riding 10 hours a week before, ride 5. The goal this week is simple: prove to your body that the bike is safe again. Ride frequently — four or five shorter rides are better than two long ones.

**Week 2.** Add 10-15 per cent volume. Still Zone 1-2 only. Pay attention to how you feel 24 hours after each ride, not during. If you are consistently feeling fresh the morning after your rides, you are in the right zone. If you are dragging, you have done too much.

**Week 3.** Add another 10-15 per cent volume. Introduce one ride with some tempo efforts — 10-15 minutes at Zone 3, not flat out, just reminding the legs what it feels like to push a bit. This is a test, not a training stimulus.

**Week 4.** Continue building volume towards your target. If the tempo ride in week 3 went well — no pain, no excessive fatigue, no return of symptoms — you can add a second session with moderate intensity. Sweet spot or low-end threshold for short durations. 2x10 minutes is plenty.

**Weeks 5-8.** Progressive return to your normal training structure. Reintroduce intervals, but start with shorter durations and fewer repetitions than your pre-setback sessions. Build week on week. By week 8, most riders are back to 80-90 per cent of their previous capacity.

The key principle: **each week should feel slightly too easy.** If every ride feels like a stretch, you are progressing too fast. The cost of going one week slower than necessary is negligible. The cost of going one week faster than you should is potentially months.

## Dealing with Injury: What to Do While You Cannot Ride

The worst part of an injury is not the pain. It is the inactivity. The feeling of your fitness leaking away while you sit on the sofa watching other people's Strava uploads.

Here is what you can do, depending on the nature of the injury:

**If the injury allows upper body movement:** Swimming and upper body resistance work maintain cardiovascular fitness more than you would expect. Even 20-30 minutes of pool work three times a week preserves a significant portion of your aerobic base. Your heart does not know whether it is pumping blood to your legs or your arms — it just knows it is working.

**If you can use one leg:** Single-leg pedalling on a stationary trainer. This sounds extreme, but it works. Riders recovering from fractures or ligament repairs on one side can maintain neural pathways and cardiovascular fitness with the uninjured leg.

**If you are completely immobilised:** Isometric exercises for whatever can move without pain. Grip strength work. Breathing exercises. These sound trivial but they maintain neuromuscular engagement and give you something purposeful to do with the time — which matters more psychologically than physiologically.

**Strength work from day one of clearance.** As soon as your physiotherapist says you can load the injured area, start with bodyweight movements. Single-leg work — lunges, step-ups, single-leg press. Core stability — planks, dead bugs, pallof press. Band work for hip stability. Two sessions a week, 20-30 minutes each. This accelerates connective tissue adaptation and protects you when you get back on the bike. Keep the loads light and the movements controlled — this is about tissue tolerance and movement quality, not about testing your limits.

The critical thing is maintaining some form of daily physical practice. Even 15 minutes. The habit of being active is more fragile than the fitness itself, and once it breaks, getting it back is disproportionately hard.

## Dealing with Life Changes: Restructuring, Not Cramming

A new job that starts at 7am instead of 9am. A baby who wakes at 5:30. A partner who needs you home in the evenings. A parent who needs care.

These setbacks are different from injury or illness because they do not have an end date. And that is precisely why they require a different response.

The mistake I see constantly: a rider's available training time drops from 12 hours a week to 6, and they try to cram 12 hours of training quality into 6 hours of riding. Intervals every session. No easy rides. No recovery. The logic feels sound — "I have less time, so every minute has to count." The result is predictable. Burnout inside two months, or a stress fracture, or a relationship that cracks under the additional pressure.

Here is what actually works. Accept the new constraint as permanent, even if you hope it is temporary. Then rebuild your training around the time you actually have, not the time you wish you had.

Six hours a week is enough to maintain serious fitness. It is enough to improve, if the structure is right. Three rides during the week — two at 60-90 minutes, one with some intensity — and one longer ride at the weekend. That is a programme. That is not a compromise. That is a structured training week that will keep you competitive in any sportive or club race you enter.

The riders who handle life changes well are the ones who redesign their training rather than trying to preserve a schedule that no longer fits. The ones who do not handle it well are the ones who treat every missed session as a failure, build up resentment, and eventually stop riding altogether because the gap between what they want to do and what they can do becomes too painful.

Redesign. Do not cram.

## Missing Your Target Event

You trained for the Marmotte. You did the climbing blocks. You did the long rides. You hit the numbers in training that told you this was going to be the ride of your life.

And then you got injured three weeks out. Or you got ill the week before. Or the event was cancelled. Or you started and blew up at kilometre 80 because the heat was brutal and your nutrition plan fell apart.

This is a specific kind of devastating because there is no physical limitation holding you back — the fitness is right there, ready to go, with nowhere to go.

Here is what I have learned, both from personal experience and from talking to riders who have been through this: the fitness does not disappear because the event did.

Everything you built in those training blocks — the aerobic base, the climbing strength, the muscular endurance — that is yours. The adaptation happened. The event was the target, not the training itself. And the training adapts to new targets far more readily than you think.

Find a replacement event within 8-12 weeks. It does not have to be the same distance or the same prestige. It just needs to be a defined goal with a date attached. A local sportive. A personal challenge — ride every mountain pass in your region in a day, set a lifetime best on a segment you have always wanted to own, do a self-supported century.

The goal itself matters less than having one. Unanchored fitness — strength without purpose — is what leads riders to drift. Give it somewhere to go.

And six months from now, I promise you this: the event you missed will not matter. What will matter is whether you kept riding.

## When Setbacks Reveal Something Important

Sometimes the setback is not the problem. Sometimes it is the symptom.

A knee injury that comes from a bike fit you have been meaning to sort out for two years. An illness that keeps recurring because you have been chronically underslept and your immune system finally gave up. A performance plateau that persists because your training has no structure and you have been guessing for twelve months.

The riders who use a setback as a forcing function — who ask "why did this happen?" rather than just "how do I get past this?" — tend to come back in a fundamentally better position than they were in before.

I have seen this repeatedly in [our coaching community](/coaching). A rider breaks a collarbone, gets a proper bike fit during recovery, and comes back faster than they were before the crash. A rider gets glandular fever, is forced to confront a training load that was unsustainable, and rebuilds with a polarised approach that produces better results on fewer hours. A rider misses their target event, realises they were training out of obligation rather than enjoyment, and rediscovers why they started riding in the first place.

Setbacks strip away the momentum that was carrying you forward. And sometimes, when the momentum stops, you can see clearly for the first time whether the direction was right.

If the same type of setback keeps recurring — the same injury, repeated illness, chronic fatigue — stop treating the setback and start treating the pattern. Get a proper bike fit. Get bloods done. Talk to a coach about whether your training load is sustainable. Talk to someone about whether the balance in your life is actually working.

## Building Resilience: What Separates the Riders Who Survive Setbacks

I have watched riders go through identical setbacks with completely different outcomes. Two riders break a collarbone in the same month. One is back riding six months later, stronger and more committed. The other sells the bike.

The difference is not mental toughness in the jaw-clenching, push-through-the-pain sense. It is something more specific. The riders who handle setbacks well share three qualities:

**They separate identity from performance.** They are cyclists, but they are not only cyclists. When the riding stops, they do not lose their entire sense of self. They have other things — family, work, friendships, other interests — that anchor them while the bike is unavailable. The riders who collapse under setbacks are often the ones whose entire identity is wrapped up in their training numbers.

**They have a community.** Not just riding partners, but people who understand what the sport means to them. People they can talk to honestly about the frustration without getting empty reassurance. The riders who go through setbacks alone almost always struggle more and take longer to return. Connection is not soft — it is structural.

**They think in timelines, not absolutes.** Instead of "I have lost everything," they think "I am going to be in a different place in eight weeks." This is not positive thinking. It is accurate thinking. Eight weeks of consistent, structured training from any starting point will produce measurable, tangible improvement. Knowing that — really knowing it, not just believing it — makes the present moment bearable.

## The Forward Target Framework

The single most important question after any setback is not "how do I get back to where I was?" That question anchors you to the past and sets up a comparison you will lose every day until you reach an arbitrary number that may or may not be achievable on your current timeline.

The better question is: **"What is the next realistic milestone I can reach in 6-8 weeks?"**

Here is how to build that target:

**Step 1: Honest assessment.** Where are you right now? Not where you were. Not where you want to be. Where are you today? If you have a power meter, do a baseline test after two weeks of easy riding. If you do not, assess by feel — how far can you ride comfortably? How does a moderate effort feel? What is your current ceiling?

**Step 2: Set a milestone, not a destination.** You are not setting your season goal. You are setting a 6-8 week checkpoint. Something specific and measurable. "Ride 100km comfortably." "Hold 200 watts for 20 minutes." "Complete the club ride without getting dropped on the first climb." It needs to be achievable from your current starting point with consistent training.

**Step 3: Build backwards.** Once you have the 6-8 week target, work backwards. What does week 6 need to look like? Week 4? Week 2? This gives you a structure that is proportionate to where you actually are, not where your ego wants to be.

**Step 4: Reassess at the milestone.** When you hit the 6-8 week mark, take stock. Set a new milestone. Repeat. This rolling framework keeps you moving forward without the paralysis of looking at a peak that feels impossibly far away.

Forward targets beat backward comparisons every single time. The rider who says "I want to hold 220 watts for 20 minutes by September" is in a fundamentally better psychological position than the rider who says "I used to hold 280 watts and now I can only hold 190." They might be describing the same body on the same bike. But one framing drives action and the other drives despair.

## The Bit Nobody Wants to Hear

Some setbacks change the trajectory permanently. A serious crash that leaves you with hardware in your body. A medical diagnosis that puts limits on your training. An age-related decline that means last year's numbers are not coming back regardless of how well you train.

The "not done yet" ethos that drives everything we do at Roadman does not mean you will always get back to where you were. It means you are not finished. There is still more riding to do, more improvement to find, more of the sport to experience. The best version of you on the bike might look different from the version you are grieving. And that is fine. Different does not mean less.

The riders I admire most are not the ones who never got knocked down. They are the ones who got knocked down, sat with the frustration, made a plan, and got back on the bike with realistic expectations and genuine enthusiasm for whatever comes next.

That is what being not done yet actually means. Not clinging to old numbers. Not pretending setbacks do not hurt. Just refusing to let this be the end of the story.

If you are in the middle of a setback right now — injured, ill, overwhelmed, staring at a fitness graph that is heading in the wrong direction — here is what I want you to know. The situation is almost certainly less dire than it feels. Your body remembers more than you think. The path forward exists, and it starts with one easy ride, not a heroic effort.

And if you want to work through it with people who understand — riders who have been exactly where you are and come out the other side — [the community is here](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling). No platitudes. Just riders helping riders figure it out.

You are not done yet.

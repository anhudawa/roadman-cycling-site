A strength programme has to survive the week you actually live. There is little value in planning two gym visits if the second one is always the first thing to go when work runs late.

For a cyclist short on time, the question is how much gym work you can keep doing while still riding well. Start there. The number of sessions comes afterwards.

## Is twice a week the answer?

Two sessions gives you a straightforward way to spread the work. It is the structure used in our [12-week strength course](/strength-training). It is not a threshold below which lifting suddenly stops being worthwhile.

The [2025 systematic review by Llanos-Lagos and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40632222/) found benefits from heavy strength training for cycling performance, efficiency and anaerobic power. The authors rated the evidence as low certainty and could not make robust recommendations about the best way to programme it. That leaves room for coaching judgement; it does not establish one compulsory schedule.

One consistent session may be a sensible place to begin. Two may let you distribute the work more comfortably. Either choice needs a programme behind it: exercises, sets, loads and a way to progress. Counting visits alone tells you very little about the amount of training.

## Open the diary before choosing the exercises

Put in the rides you care most about. Add the commitments that are unlikely to move: work, travel, family. Now look for gym time you can keep through an ordinary month.

If there is one reliable opening, start by planning around that. If there are two, look at what comes after each. An empty Wednesday evening can be a poor choice when Thursday contains the hardest ride of the week.

The [strength-session planner](/tools/strength-session-planner) lets you compare possible placements. You still need to choose the work that goes into those slots.

Combining demanding sessions on one day can leave another day clear. Separating them can help you start each one fresher. The right arrangement depends on your priorities and response. Write down the arrangement you intend to try, then see what happens to the rides on either side.

## Make the session easy to repeat

Choose a small set of movements you can perform well and record consistently. If you are learning to lift, have someone qualified help with technique and starting loads. Our [gym-exercise guide](/blog/cycling-gym-exercises-best) explains the main options.

Keep a record of the weight, reps and how hard the work felt. Bring it back next time. Without that record, it is difficult to distinguish a sensible increase from an enthusiastic guess.

There is no need to change every exercise because a session has become familiar. Familiarity lets you compare the work. It also makes a change easier to understand: did the extra sets leave your legs heavy, or was it the long ride you added that weekend?

If strength work repeatedly compromises important rides, review the session with your coach. The amount of work, its timing or the speed of progression may need changing. Squeezing a missed gym session into an already demanding weekend is unlikely to answer that question.

## Judge the dose over a block

A single sore morning does not tell you whether a programme is working. At the end of the block, look back at three things:

- **The gym:** did you attend, complete the work and improve the movements or loads you were practising?
- **The bike:** could you still complete the rides that mattered?
- **The week:** did the routine leave enough room for sleep, meals and the rest of your life?

If you kept one session consistently and made progress, that is a better basis for the next decision than an arbitrary rule that says you must attend twice. If two sessions fitted well, there may be no reason to change them.

For a cycling performance goal, compare the relevant test under similar conditions. Your training log can help explain a result, but it cannot tell you precisely how much came from the gym rather than the riding, fuelling or recovery around it.

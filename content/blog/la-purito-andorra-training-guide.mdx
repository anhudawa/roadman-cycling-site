Joaquim Rodriguez never won a Grand Tour. He finished second at the Giro, second at the Vuelta, and spent a decade as the rider who climbed better than almost everyone but could never quite close the deal in a three-week race. What he could do — what earned him the nickname Purito and an entire career built on pure vertical — was climb with an intensity that burned everyone around him. Short, explosive, relentless. He attacked on gradients that made other riders sit down and grind, and he did it with a cadence and a fury that was unmistakably his own.

La Purito Andorra is named after that riding style, and it delivers on the promise. 145km through the Pyrenean principality with 4,600m of climbing, almost entirely above 1,000 metres altitude, with no meaningful flat sections for recovery. The route strings together a series of cols — Col d'Ordino, Port de Cabus, Coll de la Gallina, Collada de Beixalis — through a micro-state that measures 468 square kilometres and contains more vertical metres of paved road per square kilometre than almost anywhere in Europe. Andorra is a country built for climbing, and this event uses every metre of it.

What separates La Purito from comparable events in the Alps or even elsewhere in the Pyrenees is the altitude floor. Most sportives start at valley level — 300m, 500m, maybe 800m — and climb into the mountains from there. La Purito starts at 1,000m and stays above it for the entire day. When you crest a col at 2,300m, you have not climbed from a valley at sea level. You have climbed from a valley that is already at altitude. The air is thinner from the gun, and it gets thinner with every col. For riders coming from sea level — which is most of the field — this changes the character of the event in ways that the elevation profile alone does not communicate.

Entry sits at EUR 70-90 depending on when you register, and the event typically falls in September. The September timing matters: it sits at the tail end of the European cycling season, which means periodisation is different from a June event. You are not building fitness from winter. You are maintaining or sharpening fitness that you have been building all year — and you need to arrive at the start line with enough in the tank after a full season of riding.

This is the preparation framework we use with riders inside [Not Done Yet](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) who are targeting La Purito. The training is built around climbing volume, altitude awareness, and the specific pacing demands of an event that never lets you recover.

## Course Profile and Key Sections

La Purito's 145km route does not follow the structure of most sportives, where long valley sections connect the climbs and give you time to eat, drink, and recover between efforts. The [Quebrantahuesos](/blog/quebrantahuesos-training-guide) gives you rolling valleys between cols. The [Marmotte Pyrenees](/blog/marmotte-pyrenees-training-guide) has sections of flat road. La Purito has neither. The recovery sections are short descents and brief valley links that barely let the heart rate drop before the next ascent begins. The climbing density — roughly 32 metres of elevation gain per kilometre — is among the highest of any single-day event in Europe, exceeding even the [Raid Pyreneen](/blog/raid-pyreneen-training-guide) when measured on a per-stage basis.

### Col d'Ordino (10.3km, 5.5% average, summit 1,980m)

The first serious test of the day. The Col d'Ordino climbs out of the Valira valley through dense pine forest, gaining just over 550m across 10.3 kilometres. The gradient is steady — mostly between 5 and 6 per cent with brief kicks to 7 — and the road surface is good. It is the kind of climb that rewards rhythm. You settle into a cadence, keep the power controlled, and use it to calibrate how the altitude feels today.

Here is where it gets really interesting: the summit at 1,980m is already higher than the top of most Alpine sportive climbs. You are at this altitude within the first two hours of riding, and you will not drop significantly below 1,500m for the rest of the day. Riders who go too hard on the Ordino — because the gradient feels manageable and the legs feel fresh — arrive at the top with a heart rate that tells a different story from the power meter. The air is already thin enough to matter.

### Port de Cabus (12.5km, 6.2% average, summit 2,300m+)

The Port de Cabus is the highest point on the route and the col that introduces the full altitude effect. 12.5 kilometres of climbing at 6.2 per cent average, with the final 3 kilometres steepening to 7-8 per cent as the road approaches the border area near France. The summit sits above 2,300m — at this altitude, the air contains roughly 20 per cent less oxygen than at sea level.

The physiological cost is real. A rider producing 250 watts at sea level can expect to produce 225-235 watts for the same perceived effort at 2,300m. Heart rate rises, breathing rate increases, and the sense of sustainable effort shifts. If you are not acclimatised — and most riders will not be fully acclimatised regardless of preparation — the Port de Cabus is where you first feel the event's altitude signature.

The descent from Cabus is fast and technical in places. The road quality varies — patches of rougher tarmac appear near the summit — and the temperature drops noticeably above 2,000m. A gilet in the pocket is worth its weight here. Use the [wind chill calculator](/tools/wind-chill) to model what 8 degrees and 50kph of descending speed feel like on exposed skin. The answer is cold enough to affect your grip and your concentration.

### Coll de la Gallina (8km, 8% average, summit 1,910m)

This is the signature climb. The Coll de la Gallina arrives after 100+ kilometres of riding at altitude, and it is the steepest sustained ascent on the route. 8 kilometres at 8 per cent average, with pitches in the second half reaching 10-12 per cent. The road is narrower than the earlier climbs, the gradient more unforgiving, and the legs — loaded from everything that came before — are at their most vulnerable.

The Gallina is where the event is decided. Riders who paced the Ordino and Cabus conservatively, who fuelled consistently, and who managed the altitude with patience arrive at the base of the Gallina with cards to play. Riders who went too hard too early arrive with nothing. The [climb time calculator](/tools/climb-time) will give you projected ascent times based on your W/kg, and the [VAM calculator](/tools/vam) will show what climbing speed to target — expect your VAM here to be 10-15 per cent lower than it would be on a similar gradient at sea level.

### Collada de Beixalis (5.8km, 7.3% average, summit 1,796m)

The final classified climb. Shorter than the Gallina, less steep on average, but arriving on legs that have already absorbed 4,000m+ of climbing. The Collada de Beixalis is not where the event starts — it is where it finishes for riders who did not manage their effort across the first three cols. The gradient is consistent, the road is good, and the effort is about whatever you have remaining. No tricks, no tactics. Just the honest question of how much climbing you have left in your legs after 130 kilometres above 1,000m.

## Fitness Benchmarks

Your [power-to-weight ratio](/tools/wkg) determines the shape of your day. These benchmarks are adjusted for the altitude effect — at 1,500-2,300m, your real-world power output is lower than your sea-level FTP test number.

| Target | W/kg (sea-level FTP) | Altitude-Adjusted Equivalent | Approximate Finish Character | Notes |
|--------|---------------------|------------------------------|------------------------------|-------|
| Strong | 3.8+ W/kg | ~3.3-3.5 W/kg at altitude | Riding the Gallina with control, descending with confidence | Acclimatisation still matters even at this level |
| Solid | 3.2-3.7 W/kg | ~2.8-3.2 W/kg at altitude | Sustainable effort throughout, Gallina is hard but manageable | Sweet spot for well-prepared amateurs |
| Finish | 2.8-3.1 W/kg | ~2.4-2.7 W/kg at altitude | Gallina requires careful pacing, every watt counts | Pacing discipline and fuelling execution become critical |
| Survival | Below 2.8 W/kg | Below 2.4 W/kg at altitude | Very long day, possible walking on the steepest Gallina pitches | Consider the shorter route option |

Two important notes. First, these are sustained climbing numbers across 6-8 hours of riding, not your fresh 20-minute test on the turbo trainer. After five hours of riding above 1,500m, your real output is 85-90 per cent of your rested value — and the altitude takes another 5-10 per cent on top of that. The [race predictor](/tools/race-predictor) will model your finish time against the course profile and your power data, factoring duration and fatigue.

Second, the altitude adjustment is not linear. A rider at 3.5 W/kg who has spent a week acclimatising in Andorra will lose less power at altitude than a rider at the same W/kg who flew in from London the evening before. Acclimatisation is the variable that the numbers alone cannot capture.

## 14-Week Training Plan Overview

The plan assumes you are entering with a base of 7-9 hours per week and some climbing experience. If you are starting from less than that, add 4-6 weeks of steady riding before week one. September timing means this plan starts in mid-June — you should already have a season of riding in your legs.

### Phase 1: Base (Weeks 1-4)

Volume: 7-10 hours per week. Four to five rides. 80 per cent in Zone 2.

La Purito is a 6-8 hour day at altitude. The aerobic base — the steady, conversational-pace power you can hold for hour after hour — is the foundation. Without it, the intensity work in the build phase has nothing to sit on. Stephen Seiler's research on polarised training is unambiguous here: 80 per cent easy, 20 per cent hard. The riders who skip base work to chase threshold numbers arrive at long events with speed but no durability.

**Weekly structure:**
- 3-4 Zone 2 rides (1-2 hours midweek, longer on weekends)
- 1 quality session: sweet spot, 2x20 minutes at 88-93 per cent FTP
- 1 long ride building from 3 hours to 4.5 hours by week 4, with 600-1,000m of climbing
- 1-2 off-bike sessions: lunges, step-ups, Bulgarian split squats, single-leg press, core work (planks, side planks, dead bugs) — support climbing posture and hours in the saddle without loading the spine

**Key metric:** By week 4, a 4.5-hour ride with 1,000m of climbing should feel steady, not depleting. If it feels like a big effort, you need more time in this phase.

### Phase 2: Build (Weeks 5-9)

Volume: 9-12 hours per week. The climbing-specific work begins.

This is where the training starts to look like the event. Two quality sessions per week, long rides building to 5-6 hours with sustained climbing, and at least two weekends where you stack back-to-back riding days.

**Weekly structure:**
- 2 quality sessions (see Key Sessions below)
- 2-3 Zone 2 rides
- 1 long ride building to 5-6 hours with 1,200-1,800m of climbing
- 1 off-bike session (maintain strength, reduce volume — the climbing is doing the leg work now)

**The stacked weekend:** At least twice during the build phase, ride a 4-5 hour Saturday with 1,200m+ climbing followed by a 3-4 hour Sunday with 800m+ climbing. This teaches the body to climb on tired legs — which is exactly what the Coll de la Gallina demands after 100+ km of prior ascending.

**Altitude simulation:** If you train on a smart trainer with altitude simulation capability, set it to 1,500m for one quality session per week during weeks 7-9. This is not a substitute for real acclimatisation, but it introduces the reduced-oxygen sensation and teaches your body to manage effort at lower oxygen availability. If your trainer does not have this feature, the session still works at sea level — the fitness transfers, even if the altitude-specific adaptation does not.

### Phase 3: Sharpen (Weeks 10-12)

Volume: 10-12 hours per week. Event-specific intensity.

The long ride peaks at 6-7 hours with 1,500m+ of climbing. Quality sessions shift towards race-pace simulation — sustained efforts at the power you expect to hold on the Gallina and Cabus. One final stacked weekend in week 11, ideally on the steepest terrain you can access.

**Weekly structure:**
- 1 sustained climbing session: 2x30 minutes at 85-90 per cent FTP on a gradient, with 10 minutes recovery between
- 1 VO2max session: 5x4 minutes at 106-115 per cent FTP with 4 minutes recovery
- 1 long ride: 6-7 hours, climbing-heavy
- 2 Zone 2 rides
- Core and mobility work only (drop the weighted off-bike sessions)

**Key metric:** The 2x30-minute climbing efforts should feel hard but sustainable. If you cannot complete them at 85-90 per cent FTP after a warm-up with 1,000m of climbing beneath you, your threshold fitness is not yet where it needs to be for a controlled ride on the Gallina.

### Phase 4: Taper (Weeks 13-14)

Week 13: Volume drops to 7-8 hours. Keep one quality session — 3x8 minutes at threshold, 4x90 seconds at VO2max — to maintain sharpness. The long ride drops to 3-4 hours. Easy spinning fills the rest.

Week 14 (race week): Volume drops to 4-5 hours. Two short rides of 60-90 minutes with a few openers — 3x3 minutes at threshold on Tuesday or Wednesday. Thursday is a rest day or a 45-minute spin. Friday is a short ride to check the bike and loosen the legs. Saturday is the event.

The common mistake in the final fortnight is adding volume because nerves say you are not ready. The taper sheds fatigue without losing fitness. A properly rested rider gains 5-10 watts on race day compared to a rider who trained hard until Wednesday. You will feel that difference on the Gallina when the gradient hits 10 per cent and the altitude hits 1,900m.

## Key Sessions

These are the sessions that make the difference between arriving at La Purito fit and arriving prepared.

**Climbing repeats (15-30 minutes).** Find the steepest sustained gradient you can access — ideally 6-8 per cent — and ride repeats of 15-30 minutes at 85-92 per cent FTP. Start with 3x15 minutes in week 5 and build to 2x30 minutes by week 11. Recovery is the descent. This is the session that builds the specific muscular endurance for cols like the Gallina — the ability to hold power at a climbing cadence of 65-75 RPM for extended durations. If you lack gradients, overgeared flat intervals at 55-65 RPM simulate the muscular cost, though not the body position.

**Sustained tempo.** 60-90 minutes at 75-82 per cent FTP, ideally on rolling terrain with no stops. This simulates the valley links between cols where the road is not steep enough to call a climb but not flat enough to rest. La Purito has very little flat road. The "recovery" sections between climbs are gentle false flats that still demand 70-80 per cent of threshold to maintain reasonable speed. Train this effort level until it feels like breathing.

**Altitude simulation on the trainer.** If your smart trainer or training app supports altitude simulation, ride one session per week at a simulated altitude of 1,500-2,000m during the build phase. A simple approach: a 90-minute ride with 2x20 minutes at sweet spot, the entire session set to 1,800m altitude. The reduced oxygen forces your body to adapt its breathing patterns, blood oxygen management, and perceived effort calibration. This is supplementary — real altitude exposure in the days before the event matters more — but it reduces the shock of the first col.

**Layer management practice.** This sounds like logistics, not training. It is both. During at least three long rides in the build phase, practise adding and removing a gilet and arm warmers while riding. La Purito involves multiple summit-to-valley transitions where the temperature swings 10-15 degrees in the span of a descent. Fumbling with a gilet on a cold Pyrenean descent at 60kph is a safety issue. Practise until it is automatic.

## Nutrition Strategy

The altitude changes the fuelling equation. At 1,500-2,300m, appetite suppression is common — you feel less hungry than you are. Simultaneously, the increased breathing rate from thinner air accelerates carbohydrate oxidation. You burn more carbs per hour at altitude than at sea level, and you feel less like eating. This combination catches riders hard if they do not plan around it.

**Target: 80-90g of carbohydrate per hour from the first kilometre.** Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to calculate exact quantities against your bodyweight and projected ride time.

| Hour | What to Eat | Altitude Notes |
|------|------------|----------------|
| 1-2 | Drink mix (40-50g/hr) + 1 gel or bar per hour | Appetite may be normal at 1,000m — eat aggressively now |
| 3-4 | Drink mix + real food (rice cakes, banana, energy bar) | Above 1,500m, appetite begins to suppress — eat to schedule, not hunger |
| 5-6 | Gels and drink mix, small amounts frequently | At 2,000m+, nausea risk rises — liquid carbs are easier to tolerate than solids |
| 7+ | Gels and liquid carbs only | Gut function is compromised by altitude and fatigue — eat what sits |

**Hydration.** 500-750ml per hour. The dry mountain air at altitude increases respiratory water loss — you lose moisture with every breath that you would not lose at sea level. The [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) will model intake against bodyweight and conditions. Sodium intake of 500-800mg per litre is important: the combination of sweat loss on the climbs and respiratory water loss on the cols creates a dual dehydration pathway that plain water does not address.

**Pre-race.** 8-10g of carbohydrate per kg of bodyweight the day before. Breakfast 3-4 hours before the start — 2-3g/kg of carbs, moderate protein, low fibre, low fat. The September morning in Andorra may be cool enough that a warm breakfast (porridge, toast, eggs) sits better than a cold one. Eat what you have practised. Race week is not the time to experiment.

**The altitude appetite problem.** Here is the good news: this is fixable. If you train your gut to high carb intake at sea level, the mechanical habit of eating every 20-30 minutes transfers to altitude even when appetite says no. Set a timer on your bike computer. Every 20 minutes, eat something. Do not wait for hunger. At altitude, hunger signals arrive 30-60 minutes after the deficit has already begun to affect performance.

## Equipment and Gear

### Gearing

Run a compact crankset (50/34) with a 34-tooth cassette as the minimum. If you weigh above 80kg, consider a sub-compact (48/31 or 46/30) with a 34-tooth rear. The Coll de la Gallina at 8 per cent average with pitches to 12 per cent, arriving after 100+ km of climbing, demands gearing that lets you spin at 65-70 RPM without pushing power you no longer have. Use the [gear ratio calculator](/tools/gear-ratio) to model your cadence at different gradients and speeds — the aim is to have a gear that lets you maintain 60+ RPM on 10-12 per cent gradients at the power you will realistically have after five hours of riding.

Fit the cassette in week 5 of the plan. Train on the gearing you will race. The Col d'Ordino and the early kilometres will barely touch your largest sprocket — that is fine. The cassette is insurance for the Gallina, and you do not buy insurance the day you need it.

### Pyrenean Road Surfaces

Andorran roads are generally well-maintained — better than the French Pyrenean side roads, comparable to the best Alpine routes. That said, the upper sections of the Port de Cabus and some of the connecting roads have patches of rougher tarmac, and early-autumn rain can wash gravel onto the road at altitude. 25mm or 28mm tyres at 75-85 PSI (for a 75kg rider) give a good balance of comfort and rolling efficiency for the mixed surfaces. If you are used to racing on 23mm tyres at 100 PSI, this is the event to go wider.

### Layering for Altitude Changes

September in Andorra produces temperature swings of 15-20 degrees within a single stage. The morning start at 1,000m may be 10-12 degrees. The valley sections warm to 20-25 degrees by midday. The Port de Cabus summit at 2,300m can be 5-8 degrees with wind chill that makes it feel colder. Early-autumn storms — short, sharp, cold — are possible on the highest cols.

**Carry always:** arm warmers, a lightweight gilet, and a packable rain jacket. The gilet goes on before every summit and comes off in the valley. The rain jacket stays in the pocket unless conditions turn — but if conditions turn above 2,000m, you will want it immediately, not back at the car. A thin merino base layer under the jersey adds warmth without bulk for the cold start and the high cols.

Check the [wind chill calculator](/tools/wind-chill) against the forecast for each col the evening before. Descending the Port de Cabus at 55kph in 8-degree air produces a wind chill below zero. Numb fingers and a cold core affect braking, handling, and power output on the next climb. Dress for the descent, not the ascent.

## Race Day Pacing

La Purito does not reward aggression. The event is too long, the climbing is too relentless, and the altitude is too constant for anyone who goes hard early to survive the final third. The pacing strategy is simple in concept and difficult in execution: ride below what you think you can hold on every climb except the last one.

### Opening Kilometres to Col d'Ordino

**Target effort: 70-75 per cent FTP.** The start is at altitude. The legs feel fresh. The field pulls you forward. Ignore all three of those signals. The Col d'Ordino at 5.5 per cent average feels manageable — because it is manageable, at the right power. Ride it 10-15 watts below what feels easy. Your [VAM](/tools/vam) should be 700-800m/hour at most. The riders who attack the Ordino are the same riders who walk the Gallina six hours later.

### Port de Cabus

**Target effort: 72-78 per cent FTP.** This is the highest col of the day and the effort should be the most controlled. The gradient steepens in the final 3km — do not increase your power to match it. Let cadence drop slightly, keep the breathing controlled, and arrive at the summit with a heart rate that is below your lactate threshold, not above it. The altitude is doing enough work on your physiology — you do not need to add to it.

Eat on the lower slopes of the Cabus. Drink before the air dries your mouth. By the summit, appetite suppression will be at its peak — get the calories in while your gut still wants them.

### Coll de la Gallina

**Target effort: 85-92 per cent of what you have left.** This is not a number you can set in advance. It is the honest answer to the question: what can I sustain for 35-50 minutes on 8 per cent gradient after 100+ km of riding at altitude?

Pace the first 3km conservatively. The gradient is above 7 per cent from the base and the temptation to attack is strong — especially if you have ridden the first three-quarters of the event with discipline and feel you have something left. You might. You might also be 10 minutes from cracking. The Gallina reveals the truth, and the truth usually arrives around kilometre 5, when the gradient ramps to 10 per cent and the summit is still 20-25 minutes away.

Settle into 65-70 RPM. If you trained the 2x30-minute climbing repeats at 85-90 per cent FTP, this effort will feel familiar. If you did not, the Gallina will feel like nothing you have prepared for. This is the climb that separates trained from untrained, and no amount of race-day adrenaline substitutes for the specific muscular endurance that only sustained climbing intervals build.

### Collada de Beixalis and the Finish

**Target effort: whatever remains.** The Beixalis is shorter and less steep than the Gallina, but it arrives on legs that have absorbed the entire event. There is no tactical play here. Ride it at a sustainable effort, keep eating if you can, and know that the finish is on the other side. The descent from Beixalis back to the finish should be ridden safely — tired legs and cold hands on a mountain descent are the combination that produces crashes, not climbing.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Ignoring the altitude.** The single biggest error. Riders look at the gradient profile and compare it to events they have ridden at sea level. A 6 per cent climb at 2,000m is not the same as a 6 per cent climb at 400m. The reduced oxygen increases heart rate, accelerates glycogen depletion, and lowers sustainable power. Riders who pace to their sea-level numbers find themselves in oxygen debt within the first two hours and spend the rest of the day managing the consequences.

**2. Going too hard on the Col d'Ordino.** The first climb, the freshest legs, the gradient that invites effort. The Ordino is not the event. The Gallina is the event. Every extra watt you spend on the Ordino is a watt you will not have five hours later when the gradient doubles and the altitude is still present. Ride the Ordino as if it is a warm-up with vertical metres — because that is exactly what it is.

**3. Not acclimatising.** Riders who fly to Barcelona on Friday evening, drive to Andorra on Saturday morning, and race on Sunday morning from sea level are giving away 5-10 per cent of their performance. Even 48 hours at 1,000m — sleeping, walking, eating at altitude — produces measurable improvements in blood oxygen saturation and perceived effort on the higher cols. Three days is better. A week is better still. Andorra is a superb pre-event riding destination. Use it.

**4. Insufficient gearing.** An 11-28 cassette is fine for 5-6 per cent gradients with fresh legs. It is not fine for 10-12 per cent pitches on the Gallina after five hours of riding at altitude. The maths does not lie: a 75kg rider at 34x28 on 10 per cent needs roughly 280 watts to hold 70 RPM. At 34x34, the same cadence requires roughly 230 watts. After 100+ km of climbing, those 50 watts are the margin between riding the Gallina and walking it. Fit a 34-tooth cassette and train on it.

**5. Not carrying warm layers.** September in the Pyrenees is not August in Mallorca. Summit temperatures above 2,000m can drop below 8 degrees, and a sudden storm brings cold rain that turns a descent from exhilarating to dangerous. A gilet weighs 80g. A packable rain jacket weighs 120g. The cost of carrying them is negligible. The cost of not carrying them is hypothermia on a col, or at minimum a 20-minute shivering descent that drains core temperature and power for the next climb.

## Travel and Logistics

### Getting to Andorra

Andorra has no airport, no railway station, and one main road in from each side. This is part of its charm and part of the planning challenge.

**Barcelona El Prat (200km, 2.5-3 hours by car).** The most popular option. Fly into Barcelona, hire a car, and drive north through the Cadí tunnel and into Andorra. The road is a motorway for the first two-thirds and a well-maintained mountain road for the final hour. Most rental companies allow their cars into Andorra without additional charges, but check your contract.

**Toulouse-Blagnac (190km, 2.5-3 hours by car).** The French approach. Drive south through the Ariège valley and enter Andorra via the Pas de la Casa. This route crosses the Port d'Envalira — at 2,408m, the highest paved pass in the Pyrenees — which is spectacular but adds time and can be slow in traffic. An alternative is the Bourg-Madame route via the Cerdanya valley, which avoids the high pass.

**Perpignan (170km, 2-2.5 hours by car).** Shorter distance but the mountain roads start sooner. Fly into Perpignan-Rivesaltes, drive through the Cerdanya, and enter Andorra from the south. A good option if you find cheap flights, but the road is slower than the Barcelona route for a similar total journey time.

**Girona (260km, 3 hours by car).** Further, but if you are combining La Purito with a Girona training camp or a few days on the Costa Brava, the drive is straightforward motorway to the Cadí tunnel and then up into Andorra.

### Accommodation

Book in Andorra la Vella (the capital, 1,023m altitude) or Encamp (1,238m). Both are on or near the route, have a range of hotels at reasonable prices, and sit at the altitude that provides natural acclimatisation simply by sleeping there. Andorra la Vella is the larger town with more restaurant and shopping options. Encamp is quieter and sits slightly higher, which may be marginally better for acclimatisation.

Book early. The event weekend fills accommodation across the principality, and Andorra is a small country with a limited hotel supply. September is also the transition between summer hikers and winter skiers, so availability varies year to year.

### Arriving Early for Acclimatisation

This is strongly recommended. Arrive 2-3 days before the event — ideally earlier if you can combine it with pre-event riding. Even 48 hours of sleeping, walking, and eating at 1,000-1,300m measurably improves blood oxygen saturation and comfort on the higher cols. If you arrive a week early, ride the Coll d'Ordino, the road to Arcalis, and the Port d'Envalira as day rides from Andorra la Vella. These rides serve as both acclimatisation and course reconnaissance. Andorra's road network is dense and the traffic is light — it is legitimately one of the best cycling destinations in the Pyrenees, and combining the event with a training camp is a natural fit.

### Race Day Logistics

Collect your number and timing chip the day before. Check your bike, charge your devices, and lay out your clothing the evening before. The morning start at 1,000m will likely be cool — 8-12 degrees — so a warm-up is important but brief: 15-20 minutes of easy spinning on a turbo or a short loop near the start. Eat breakfast 3-4 hours before the gun. Queue for the start with your pockets packed: two bottles (one water, one drink mix with electrolytes), arm warmers, gilet, rain jacket, and enough food for the first 3-4 hours.

## Recovery Plan

La Purito is a deeply fatiguing event. The combination of 4,600m of climbing, 6-8 hours of riding, and the cumulative altitude exposure produces a level of physiological stress that exceeds what the distance and elevation numbers suggest. Recovery is not optional — it is the final phase of the event.

**Immediately post-finish (0-60 minutes).** Carb-protein recovery shake within 30 minutes. 1-1.2g of carbohydrate per kg of bodyweight plus 0.3g of protein per kg. This is the window when glycogen resynthesis is most efficient. Follow with a real meal within 90 minutes — something with carbs, protein, and salt. Andorra has good restaurants. Use them.

**Day 1 post-event.** Complete rest or a gentle 30-minute walk. No cycling. Hydrate aggressively — the altitude dehydration compounds with the event dehydration, and most riders are more fluid-depleted than they realise. Sleep is the single most effective recovery intervention. If you can sleep 9-10 hours, do it.

**Days 2-3.** Easy spinning only — 30-45 minutes at conversational pace if the legs want to move. No intensity. No climbing. The goal is blood flow, not fitness.

**Days 4-7.** Gradually reintroduce normal riding. A 90-minute Zone 2 ride on day 5, building to 2-3 hours by day 7. No structured intensity until day 7 at the earliest.

**Week 2 post-event.** Return to normal training, but monitor fatigue markers — resting heart rate, sleep quality, motivation. If any of these are still elevated, extend the recovery. September timing means you are at the tail end of the season anyway. There is nothing to prove in October that is worth compromising recovery from what may be the hardest single day of your year.

The altitude factor extends recovery timelines. Riders who raced at 2,300m often report lingering fatigue 3-5 days longer than they expect from a comparable event at sea level. The body's response to sustained altitude exposure includes increased red blood cell production, altered sleep patterns, and a mild inflammatory response that takes time to resolve. Do not rush this.

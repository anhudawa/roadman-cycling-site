You've done this before. You've looked at a training plan — maybe on TrainingPeaks, maybe a PDF from a coaching site, maybe something a mate swore by — and thought, right, this is the one. This is the plan that's going to get the FTP moving, drop those extra kilos, make that sportive feel manageable instead of miserable. You loaded it up. You did the first week. Maybe the second. Then real life arrived. The Tuesday session clashed with your kid's swimming lesson. The Thursday intervals felt brutal after a terrible night's sleep. The Saturday ride that assumed four hours free by 7am met the reality of a partner who also has plans.

By week three, you were modifying so much of the plan that it wasn't really the plan any more. It was a vague gesture in the direction of structure, held together by guilt and the sunk cost of whatever you paid for it. By week six, you quietly stopped opening it.

This is the most common outcome in amateur cycling. Not injury. Not overtraining. Not choosing the wrong sessions. Just choosing the wrong plan for the life you actually have.

After 1,400 episodes of the podcast, having spoken to coaches who work with World Tour riders, amateur racers, gran fondo riders, and comeback athletes, I can tell you the pattern is remarkably consistent. The plan itself is rarely the problem. The match between the plan and the person — their schedule, their recovery, their goals, their stage of life — that's where it breaks down. And it breaks down because most people choose plans based on the wrong criteria.

## The three paths: plan, coach, or self-coaching

Before you choose a plan, you need to choose a format. And this is not a quality decision. It's a self-awareness decision.

### Pre-built training plans

A pre-built plan is a fixed schedule of sessions, usually spanning 8 to 16 weeks, designed for a general goal — raise your FTP, prepare for a gran fondo, build an endurance base. You buy it, load it into your platform, and follow it.

**This works well if** you're disciplined, consistent, reasonably experienced, and your schedule is predictable enough that a fixed structure won't collapse in week two. If you've been riding for a few years, you understand the basics of training zones and periodisation, and you mainly need structure rather than guidance, a good plan at $50 to $150 is outstanding value.

**This breaks down if** you tend to override your own plan when you feel good, ignore recovery weeks because "I feel fine," or need someone to tell you when you're doing too much. A plan can't adapt to you. It can't notice that your heart rate has been elevated for a week, or that you've been sleeping badly, or that the stress at work is making every session feel twice as hard. It delivers the prescription. What you do with the prescription is entirely on you.

The real question: can you follow a plan as written, including the easy days and the rest days, without modification unless there's a genuine reason to modify?

If the answer is no — and there's no shame in that answer — a plan might not be the right format.

### Hiring a coach

A coach provides what a plan cannot: objective assessment of how you're responding, accountability when motivation drops, and adaptation based on what's actually happening in your life and your physiology.

When I've had coaches like Dan Lorang and John Wakefield on the podcast, the thing they consistently emphasise is that coaching is not about writing a better plan. It's about adjusting the plan in real time based on the athlete's actual response. The plan is the starting point. The coaching is what happens after that.

Good coaching costs $150 to $300 per month for online programmes. That's real money. But if you're the kind of rider who needs external accountability, who benefits from someone saying "that's enough for this week" or "you're sandbagging your Zone 2 rides again," the return on that investment can be enormous. We've covered [what to look for in a coach](/blog/cycling-working-with-a-coach-guide) and [how to make that relationship work](/blog/best-online-cycling-coach-how-to-choose) separately, so I won't rehash all of that here.

The question is not whether a coach is "better" than a plan. It's whether the specific thing you're lacking is structure (get a plan), feedback and accountability (get a coach), or knowledge (keep reading, and consider self-coaching).

### Self-coaching

Self-coaching is the path for the analytically minded rider who actively enjoys the process of learning about training science and applying it to themselves. It's not the lazy option — done properly, it's the most demanding option, because you're doing two jobs: athlete and coach.

The advantage is total control and deep understanding. After a year of disciplined self-coaching, you should understand your own physiology, your response to different training stimuli, and your seasonal patterns better than most coached athletes understand theirs.

The disadvantage is objectivity. You can't see your own blind spots. Self-coached riders systematically overweight their strengths in training, underestimate accumulated fatigue, and lack the external voice that says "the numbers say you need a rest week even though you feel invincible." We've built a [full self-coaching framework](/blog/cycling-self-coaching-framework-guide) if that's the path that fits you.

The part most people miss about these three paths: they are not permanent. You can self-coach through your base phase, hire a coach for a 12-week build to your target event, and go back to a structured plan for the off-season. The riders who get the best long-term results are the ones who match the format to what they need right now, not the ones who pick a path and refuse to deviate from it.

## What a good training plan actually looks like

Whether you're buying a plan or building your own, you need to know what separates a good plan from a plan that just looks good. These are the structural elements that matter.

### Visible periodisation

A training plan without periodisation is just a list of workouts. Periodisation — the deliberate organisation of training into phases with different priorities — is what turns random sessions into progressive adaptation.

At minimum, you should see three distinct phases in any plan longer than eight weeks:

**Base phase.** Higher volume, lower intensity. Building aerobic capacity, muscular endurance, and the physiological foundation everything else sits on. Professor Seiler's research is very clear on this: the aerobic base built during this phase determines the ceiling for everything that follows. Skip it or rush it, and the build phase has less to work with.

**Build phase.** Volume stabilises or slightly drops, intensity increases. This is where the targeted work happens — threshold intervals, VO2max sessions, race-specific efforts. The plan should be clear about what it's building toward and why.

**Peak and taper.** Volume drops significantly, intensity stays high but with more recovery. The body absorbs the work from previous phases and arrives at your target event fresh, fit, and ready. A plan that runs at full intensity right up to your event is a plan that doesn't understand how adaptation works.

Within those phases, recovery should be built in every third or fourth week. Not a complete rest week — a reduced week where volume drops by 30 to 40 per cent and intensity either drops or is removed entirely. This is non-negotiable. Plans that run four, five, six weeks at full load without a recovery week are either poorly designed or designed for riders with recovery capacity that most masters cyclists simply don't have.

### Progressive overload

The plan should get harder in a controlled, visible way. Volume or intensity should increase by roughly 5 to 10 per cent per week during loading weeks, then drop during recovery weeks before stepping up again.

If week one and week eight look identical, that's a problem. Your body adapts to training stress. If the stress doesn't progress, neither do you. But the progression needs to be gradual and systematic — not a sudden jump from 6 hours in week four to 10 hours in week five because the plan designer thought you'd be "ready."

### Zone-based intensity prescription

Every intensity session should be prescribed relative to your training zones — not in absolute watts, not as a vague "hard effort," and not as a percentage of someone else's numbers. If a plan says "ride at 250 watts" without knowing your FTP, it's a plan written for an audience, not for you. If it says "ride at 88-94% of FTP" or "mid Zone 3 to low Zone 4," it's a plan that scales to your actual fitness.

This is why knowing your zones matters. If you haven't tested recently, do that before you start any plan. The [FTP zones calculator](/tools/ftp-zones) will sort this for you in about two minutes. Training to inaccurate zones is not training — it's exercise with a schedule attached.

### Built-in flexibility

This one is harder to spot in a plan you're buying, but it's the difference between a plan that works in reality and a plan that only works on paper.

Good plans acknowledge that you will miss sessions. They tell you which sessions are essential (protect these) and which are flexible (move or drop these without guilt). They provide guidance on what to do when life intervenes — not just "make it up later," but genuine decision rules. If you miss your threshold session on Tuesday, do you push it to Wednesday, skip it entirely, or modify Saturday to compensate?

Plans that assume seven perfect training days per week are plans designed by people who have never trained someone with a full-time job, a family, and a commute.

## Red flags in bad plans

You can save yourself a lot of wasted time by learning to spot these quickly.

### No recovery weeks

This is the most common and most damaging red flag. A 12-week plan with 12 weeks of loading is a plan that doesn't understand physiology. Adaptation happens during recovery, not during training. Training provides the stimulus. Recovery provides the response. A plan without recovery weeks is all stimulus, no response.

For masters cyclists — and if you're reading this, that probably includes you — this is doubly important. Recovery capacity declines with age. Not dramatically, and not in a way that means you can't train hard. But it means the 3:1 loading-to-recovery ratio that works for a 25-year-old might need to become 2:1 for a 45-year-old, at least during high-intensity phases. A plan that ignores this is not designed for your physiology.

### Every week looks the same

If the plan is the same structure repeated for 12 weeks — same sessions, same volume, same intensity, just with a different date on top — it's not a periodised plan. It's a template with a calendar. You'll adapt to those sessions within three to four weeks, and then you'll stagnate. The plan should look noticeably different in week one versus week six versus week twelve.

### Intensity without context

A plan that prescribes "5x5 minutes at threshold" in week one, before any base-building phase, is a plan that's putting the hard work before the foundation. Intensity sessions should appear in the plan because the base phase has prepared you for them, not because they make the plan look impressive.

Similarly, watch out for plans that front-load the intensity — the hardest sessions in the first two weeks to make you feel like you're "doing something." Good plans often feel underwhelming in the early weeks. That's by design.

### Volume assumptions that don't match reality

A plan built around 14 hours per week is a fine plan. But if you have 8 hours per week, it's the wrong plan. Not a plan you can "adapt" by cutting sessions — a fundamentally different plan than you need, because a 14-hour plan structures its key sessions and recovery around the assumption that you have 14 hours of stress to manage.

Be honest about your available hours. Not your aspirational hours. Not the hours you could theoretically train if everything went perfectly. The hours you will actually, reliably, consistently have available. Then find a plan that fits inside that number with room to spare. An 8-hour plan completed consistently will always outperform a 12-hour plan completed erratically.

### No mention of fuelling or recovery

Training and nutrition are not separate topics. They are the same topic. A plan that tells you exactly which intervals to do but says nothing about how to fuel those intervals, what to eat on recovery days, or how to manage energy availability across the week is a plan that's missing half the equation.

You don't need a meal-by-meal prescription. But the plan should at least acknowledge that in-ride nutrition matters, that recovery nutrition matters, and that chronically underfuelling will destroy the adaptations the plan is trying to create. The [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) gives you a baseline for what different session types require.

## Matching plans to goals

This is where most people get it wrong, because they choose a plan based on what they want to feel like rather than what they need to prepare for.

### The gran fondo or sportive rider

Your event is long — probably 100 to 160 kilometres with significant climbing. The demands are sustained aerobic output over four to six hours, the ability to recover from hard efforts (climbs) and keep going, and the pacing discipline not to blow up at kilometre 80.

Your plan should be heavily base-focused. Long endurance rides are not optional — they're the centrepiece. Intensity work should be tempo and sweet spot, with some threshold work in the build phase but not a diet of VO2max intervals that prepare you for efforts you won't be doing on the day.

Time on the bike matters more for this goal than for almost any other. If you can't do at least one ride per week that approaches 60 to 70 per cent of your event duration by the end of the build phase, you're not prepared regardless of how sharp your intervals are.

### The club racer or criterium rider

Your event is short but intense — 45 to 90 minutes of surging, attacking, responding, and recovering in real time. The demands are repeated high-power efforts, the ability to go above threshold and recover quickly, and tactical positioning that's as much about reading the race as raw watts.

Your plan should include more VO2max and anaerobic work than the sportive rider's plan. Intervals at 105 to 120 per cent of FTP, with short recoveries, simulate the demands of racing. But don't neglect the base — you still need the aerobic engine to recover between those efforts. The athletes who fade in the final 20 minutes of a criterium are almost always the ones who skipped their base work.

### The time trial or hill climb specialist

Your event is a sustained effort at or near threshold — usually 10 to 40 minutes of controlled suffering. The demands are pure sustained power output and pacing.

Your plan should be threshold-heavy. Sweet spot and threshold intervals are the bread and butter, building time at the specific intensity you'll race at. Pacing practice matters enormously — the ability to start at 95 per cent of target power and finish at 102 per cent rather than the other way round. This is a discipline where the [training load tracker](/tools/training-load) becomes particularly useful, because managing fatigue across the build phase is critical to arriving at your event with the right balance of fitness and freshness.

### The comeback rider

You haven't trained seriously in a while. Maybe months, maybe years. The demands of your goal are secondary right now — the primary demand is rebuilding the foundation without injury, burnout, or the crushing disappointment of comparing current-you to past-you.

Your plan should be conservative. Longer base phase, gentler progression, more recovery. The temptation is always to jump straight to the training you used to do, but your cardiovascular system remembers faster than your connective tissue. Your engine might be ready for intervals in week three. Your tendons, ligaments, and joints might not be ready until week eight. A good comeback plan respects that mismatch.

We've covered the [comeback framework](/blog/comeback-cyclist-12-week-return-plan) in detail elsewhere, but the short version is: the first four to six weeks should feel too easy. That's by design.

## How to adapt a generic plan to your actual life

You've found a plan that's well structured, matches your goal, and fits your approximate training hours. Now comes the practical part: fitting it into the seven days you actually live, not the seven days the plan designer imagined.

### Step one: identify the non-negotiable sessions

Every good plan has two or three sessions per week that drive the adaptation. These are typically the intensity sessions — your threshold work, your VO2max intervals, your race-specific efforts. Everything else — the endurance rides, the recovery spins — supports those sessions but doesn't replace them.

Find those key sessions. Mark them. Those are the sessions you protect. They go on the days where you have the most energy, the most time, and the best chance of executing them properly. Not squeezed into a 45-minute lunch break. Not after a 12-hour work day. On the days where they get your best effort.

### Step two: map your actual week

Write down your real weekly schedule. Not what you wish it looked like — what it actually looks like. Work hours. Commute time. Family commitments. Social obligations. Sleep. Everything.

Now identify the realistic training windows. Be honest. If the only window on Wednesday is 5:30 to 6:30 am and you haven't voluntarily woken before 6:30 in a decade, that's not a training window. It's a fantasy. We've written about [fitting training around work and family](/blog/cycling-fitting-training-around-work-family-guide) in detail — the principles there apply directly to plan adaptation.

### Step three: place the key sessions

Put your two or three non-negotiable sessions into the best available training windows. Consider the spacing — don't put two hard interval sessions on consecutive days unless the plan specifically calls for a block structure. Leave at least one easy day or rest day between high-intensity sessions.

For most working riders, this means one intensity session during the week (often an indoor session on the trainer, where you can control the environment and hit precise targets) and one on the weekend. If you can fit a second weekday intensity session, great. If you can't, one hard weekday session plus a longer weekend ride with intensity woven in is a structure that works for thousands of riders.

### Step four: fill with endurance volume

Once your key sessions are placed, use remaining windows for endurance riding. This is your Zone 2 work — the foundation of the [polarised model](/blog/cycling-polarised-vs-pyramidal-training-guide) that the best coaches in the world build their programmes around.

Here's the critical point: this volume is flexible. If Wednesday's planned endurance ride gets cancelled because work ran late or the kids need collecting, that's fine. You haven't lost the week. The key sessions are in place. The aerobic work missed on Wednesday can partly be made up on Sunday, or simply accepted as a slightly lower volume week. The world does not end.

### Step five: protect recovery

The recovery sessions in your plan — rest days, easy spins, reduced weeks — are not optional. They feel optional because they don't feel productive. Sitting on the sofa doesn't feel like training. A 45-minute spin at recovery pace doesn't feel like progress. But that's where the adaptation happens. Every coach I've spoken to on the podcast says the same thing: the athletes who struggle most are the ones who can't leave intensity alone on recovery days.

If you're the kind of rider who turns every ride into a Strava segment hunt, this is worth being honest about. Use the [training readiness assessment](/tools/training-readiness) to check whether you are actually recovered before adding intensity that the plan didn't prescribe.

### A practical example

Let's say you have a 10-hour plan for a gran fondo, and your real life allows about 8 hours of training per week. Here's how the adaptation might look:

**Monday:** Rest day (as prescribed). Non-negotiable.

**Tuesday:** 75-minute indoor session with the plan's key intensity work — maybe sweet spot intervals or threshold efforts. This is a non-negotiable session.

**Wednesday:** The plan says 90 minutes of endurance. You have 60 minutes before work. Do 60 minutes of Zone 2. Not 60 minutes at Zone 3 because you feel guilty about the shorter session. Zone 2. The point of this ride is volume and aerobic development, and 60 minutes achieves that even if the plan wanted 90.

**Thursday:** Rest or easy commute spin.

**Friday:** The plan's second intensity session — indoor, 60 to 75 minutes. Another non-negotiable.

**Saturday:** Long ride. The plan says 3.5 hours. You have 3 hours. Do 3 hours. Include any prescribed intensity within the long ride if the plan calls for it.

**Sunday:** Easy spin, 45 to 60 minutes. Or rest if Saturday was hard and the body says no.

Total: roughly 7 to 8 hours. You've hit both key intensity sessions, you've done your long ride within 85 per cent of the prescribed duration, and you've supported the hard work with endurance volume and recovery. The plan's intent — the physiological stimulus it was designed to create — is intact, even though the specific schedule has shifted.

## The masters-specific planning conversation

Here's where we need to talk about something the fitness industry doesn't like saying plainly. The plans most commonly marketed to cyclists — the plans with the slickest websites, the most polished graphics, and the most aggressive performance claims — are designed by and for younger riders with more recovery capacity, fewer life obligations, and more training hours than you have.

That doesn't mean those plans are bad. It means they're built on assumptions that may not match your reality at 40, 45, or 50.

### Recovery is the variable that changes most

Your aerobic capacity doesn't fall off a cliff in your forties. Your ability to produce power doesn't evaporate. What changes most is how long it takes to absorb training stress. The session that needed 24 hours of recovery at 30 might need 36 or 48 hours at 45. Not always. Not for everyone. But enough that a plan built on 24-hour recovery assumptions will accumulate fatigue faster than you can absorb it.

Practically, this means your plan might need more rest days than the standard template provides. It means recovery weeks might need to be properly easy rather than "reduced but still fairly hard." It means two high-intensity sessions per week might be your ceiling during certain phases, where a younger version of you could handle three.

This is not decline. It's intelligent adaptation. The total training load you can handle over a season might be very similar to what it was ten years ago — but the way it's distributed across weeks and months needs to change.

### Consistency beats heroics

The single biggest advantage you have as a masters rider is the ability to be consistent over years, not just weeks. You're not training for a career. You're not trying to make a national team by next March. You have the luxury of building slowly, systematically, with patience that younger riders typically don't have.

Use that. Choose a plan that asks for 80 per cent effort, consistently, rather than a plan that demands 100 per cent and accepts the 50 per cent compliance that results when life inevitably intervenes. The compound effect of consistent training — even moderate training — over 12 months absolutely dwarfs the impact of an aggressive plan done in six-week bursts separated by six weeks of nothing.

### The training plan is only half the system

Your plan tells you what to do on the bike. It says nothing about what to do off it. Sleep, nutrition, stress management, strength work — these aren't accessories to your training plan. For masters cyclists, they're arguably more important than the plan itself.

A rider who sleeps 7 to 8 hours consistently, fuels their training properly, manages work stress actively, and does two or three sessions of bodyweight strength work per week will absorb more training and adapt faster than a rider who follows a "perfect" plan on 5 hours of sleep, three coffees, and no attention to anything outside the cycling sessions.

The plan is important. It's not sufficient.

## How to evaluate whether a plan is working

You've picked a plan. You're four weeks in. How do you know if it's working?

Not by FTP. Not yet. Four weeks is too early for meaningful FTP changes in most riders. The early indicators are more subtle.

**Consistency of execution.** Are you completing most sessions as prescribed? If you're hitting 80 per cent or more of the planned sessions, and the key sessions are being completed at the right intensities, the plan is working in the most important sense — it's getting done.

**Subjective feel during sessions.** Are the easy sessions feeling easy? Are the hard sessions feeling hard but manageable? If everything feels hard, the intensity or volume may be too high. If nothing feels hard, you may need to retest your zones or look for a more challenging plan.

**Recovery between sessions.** Are you arriving at each key session reasonably fresh and able to hit the targets? If you're consistently carrying fatigue into your interval sessions and underperforming the prescribed intensities, the overall load may be too high or the recovery inadequate.

**Motivation.** This one gets overlooked. If you're dreading every session and the plan feels like an obligation rather than a structure, something is wrong. It might be the plan. It might be the goal. It might be accumulated fatigue masquerading as loss of motivation. But persistent dread is a signal, not a weakness.

Give a well-matched plan 8 to 12 weeks before you judge results. Six weeks for your body to absorb the initial training block. Another two to four weeks to see the adaptations in your numbers and your riding. If you're executing consistently for 12 weeks with no measurable improvement and no clear explanation, that's the point to reassess — either the plan or the format.

## The bottom line

Choosing a training plan is not a shopping decision. It's a matching exercise. You're matching a structure to your schedule, your recovery, your goals, your personality, and your stage of life. The best plan on paper is worthless if it doesn't fit the person following it.

Start with honesty. How many hours can you actually train? What does your week actually look like? What's your real goal — not the aspirational version, the honest version? Are you the kind of person who thrives with external accountability, or do you work best when you're in control?

Then match the format — plan, coach, or self-coaching — to that honest assessment. Choose a plan that respects the structural principles: periodisation, progressive overload, recovery, and zone-based prescription. Adapt it to your real life by protecting the key sessions and letting the volume flex.

And remember: a completed moderate plan will always outperform an abandoned ambitious one. Every time. Without exception.

## The short answer

Your glutes already work when you pedal. The gluteus maximus contributes to hip extension, and the lateral gluteal muscles contribute to hip and pelvic control. Their timing and activity change with the cycling task.

A brief bridge-and-band routine can be a useful **primer** if it improves how you feel before riding. It is not a diagnostic test, does not “wake up” an anatomically dormant muscle and is not proven to add watts. To build lasting capacity, progressively train hip extension, single-leg force and lateral hip control one or two times per week.

## Five practical glute exercises for cyclists

| Exercise | Main job | Starting dose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step-up | Single-leg hip and knee extension | 2-3 × 6-10 per side |
| Split squat | Single-leg strength through a controllable range | 2-3 × 6-10 per side |
| Hip thrust or bridge | Horizontal hip extension | 2-3 × 8-12 |
| Romanian-style hinge | Hip extension with hamstring and trunk demand | 2-3 × 6-10 |
| Resisted lateral step | Lateral hip capacity | 2-3 × 8-12 steps per side |

Those doses are entry points, not a complete personalised programme. Start with a load that leaves technique stable and several repetitions in reserve. Progress one variable at a time.

### 1. Step-up

Use a step height that lets you control the full movement. Place the whole foot on the step, rise without launching off the trailing leg and lower under control. A higher box is not automatically more glute-specific or better.

### 2. Split squat

Choose a stance that feels stable and lower through a tolerable range. The split squat trains glutes and quadriceps together; it does not need to isolate one muscle to be useful for a cyclist.

### 3. Hip thrust or bridge

Use a floor bridge to learn the setup, then add resistance or move to a bench-supported hip thrust if appropriate. Finish with the hips controlled rather than arching the low back to chase height.

### 4. Romanian-style hinge

Keep the load close, soften the knees and move the hips back while the trunk remains braced. Stop at the range you can control. The hinge loads the posterior chain; it is not a test of whether the glutes fire before the hamstrings.

### 5. Resisted lateral step

Use a light band and maintain controlled foot, knee and pelvis positions as you step. The exercise can load gluteus medius and minimus, but a burn does not prove that it will correct knee pain or change on-bike tracking.

A [systematic review of 16 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32132843/) found very high gluteus-maximus EMG activity across many exercises, including step-up variations, hip thrusts, split squats, lunges, deadlifts and belt squats. A separate [review of 56 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344003/) identified several tasks that produced high gluteus-medius or minimus activity, including hip-hitch variations, standing hip abduction, lateral step-ups, single-leg bridges and resisted side-steps.

EMG can help describe muscle excitation. It does not by itself prove long-term strength, hypertrophy, pain relief or cycling transfer.

## A three-minute pre-ride glute primer

If you enjoy a primer, try:

1. Eight controlled bodyweight bridges.
2. Five bodyweight split squats per side.
3. Six resisted lateral steps per side.
4. Begin the ride with five to ten minutes of progressive easy spinning.

Stop the circuit well before fatigue. The aim is familiarity and readiness, not a mini strength session.

The cycling-specific evidence for a glute primer is thin. In a crossover study of [17 professional rugby players](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28761719/), a gluteal activation warm-up changed gluteus-maximus EMG but did not significantly change estimated muscle forces or ground-reaction-force performance. That is a small, non-cycling study—not proof for or against an individual cyclist's preferred routine.

A [systematic review of 35 post-activation studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37796168/) found very-low-certainty evidence and no support for recommending post-activation strategies to improve endurance performance. Progressive riding remains the most specific warm-up for riding.

## Why “switched-off glutes” is the wrong model

Cycling EMG research shows that lower-limb activation changes with power output, cadence, position, training status and fatigue. The [pedalling EMG review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18093842/) describes a coordinated, task-dependent pattern—not a simple contest in which the quads take over because the glutes forgot how to work.

Feeling a bridge in the hamstring does not establish a neurological firing fault. Sensation can change with foot position, joint angle, fatigue, technique and attention. The single-leg bridge is useful as an exercise and observation, but it has no validated pass/fail rule for “gluteal amnesia.” A study in [100 male soccer players](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35693869/) also found that single-leg bridge repetitions had only poor associations with measured hamstring torque, illustrating why a simple floor task should not be overinterpreted as a strength diagnostic.

## Activation versus strength

Low-load primers and progressive resistance training are different jobs.

- **Primer:** short, low fatigue, optional, immediately before selected rides.
- **Strength training:** progressively loaded, planned around key cycling, measured over weeks.

The claim that compound lifts merely reinforce dysfunction and that every cyclist must isolate a muscle first is not supported. Squats, split squats, step-ups, lunges, hinges and hip thrusts all recruit the glutes. Choose the movements a rider can perform and progress well.

A [2025 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40276368/) found a moderate effect of resistance training on gluteus-maximus hypertrophy across 12 included articles, with both single exercises and combined programmes producing growth. That evidence is not cyclist-specific and muscle size is not the same as cycling performance, but it supports progressive loading over endless “wake-up” drills.

## Where glute work fits around the bike

For a cyclist already training regularly:

- place one or two strength sessions on days that protect the most important ride;
- perform the demanding lift after an easy ride or on a separate day, depending on recovery;
- keep the pre-ride primer optional and non-fatiguing;
- reduce strength volume during dense racing or cycling blocks;
- progress load, repetitions or range gradually rather than changing everything at once.

The [strength training for cyclists guide](/blog/cycling-strength-training-guide) covers the whole programme, and the [strength-session planner](/tools/strength-session-planner) helps place the work around real rides.

In a small [10-week randomised trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39231694/), 37 well-trained male cyclists were allocated to off-bike resistance, high-resistance on-bike training or control. Both training groups improved several strength measures and Wingate performance compared with control, without a significant difference between the two resistance approaches. It supports trainable capacity; it does not isolate glute activation as the mechanism.

## Do not move the saddle just to feel more glute

Torso and bike position can change muscle timing and torque. In a study of [trained cyclists at 70% maximal aerobic power](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25996563/), lower time-trial torso angles altered crank-torque coordination and gluteus-maximus activation timing while also changing physiological measures.

That is why “move the saddle back to engage the glutes” is incomplete advice. A fit change can also affect knee and hip angles, pressure, comfort, aerodynamics and control. Use a [bike-fit process](/blog/bike-fit-guide-cyclists) and validate the whole outcome.

## Pain is not a glute diagnosis

Low-back pain, knee pain or tight-feeling hips do not prove that the glutes are weak, inactive or the single cause. Do not use a bridge, knee position or muscle burn as a diagnosis.

Persistent pain, meaningful weakness, numbness, trauma or worsening symptoms deserves assessment. Training load, bike fit, tissue capacity, health and previous injury may all matter. Exercise selection should follow the problem, not a viral label.

## What “before and after” should mean

Before and after a three-minute primer, you may notice a change in sensation or readiness. That is not new strength or muscle. Before and after a multi-week strength block, useful measures include:

- the load and repetitions completed with stable technique;
- a repeatable single-leg task;
- comfort and tolerance across the riding position;
- cycling performance measured under comparable conditions.

Avoid attributing every watt, pain change or visual difference to the glutes alone.

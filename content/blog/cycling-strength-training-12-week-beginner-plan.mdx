The best 12-week strength plan for a beginner cyclist is one you can repeat without wrecking the riding week. It should teach a small number of useful movements, increase the work gradually and make the next priority ride part of the success test.

This is a conservative Roadman framework. No cyclist study has tested this exact two-session, 12-week package and proved it optimal. The evidence supports resistance training as a category more clearly than it supports one beginner sequence.

## The plan in 30 seconds

| Phase | Weeks | Frequency and work | Progression test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Learn and tolerate | 1–2 | 1–2 sessions; 1–2 work sets; leave 3–4 reps available | Technique stays controlled and symptoms do not worsen over 24–48 hours. |
| Build repeatability | 3–6 | Move toward 2 sessions; 2 sets on main patterns; mostly 6–12 controlled reps | Both sessions repeat cleanly and priority rides retain quality. |
| Progress selected lifts | 7–12 | 2 sessions if recovered; 2–3 sets on selected main patterns | Add one variable only when the whole week remains productive. |

Power or plyometric work is **not** a compulsory final phase. A beginner can continue progressing controlled strength for all 12 weeks.

## Before week one

Seek appropriately qualified medical or rehabilitation guidance before beginning when you have a relevant cardiovascular, neurological or orthopaedic condition, osteoporosis, recent surgery, unexplained pain or concerning symptoms. Stop and seek urgent help for chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or rapidly worsening neurological symptoms.

For a healthy adult who is ready to begin, choose the simplest versions of these patterns:

- knee-dominant;
- hip-dominant;
- upper-body pull;
- upper-body push; and
- trunk control or carry.

Single-leg and calf work can be useful additions, not mandatory proof that the programme is “cycling-specific”. Use the [gym exercises for cyclists guide](/blog/cycling-gym-exercises-best) to compare the options.

## Session A

| Exercise pattern | Accessible options | Weeks 1–2 | Weeks 3–12 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Knee-dominant | Goblet squat or leg press | 1–2 × 6–10 | 2–3 × 6–12 |
| Hip-dominant | Supported hinge or Romanian deadlift | 1–2 × 6–10 | 2–3 × 6–12 |
| Upper pull | Seated row or pull-down | 1–2 × 8–12 | 2 × 8–12 |
| Calf | Standing or seated calf raise | 1–2 × 8–15 | 2 × 8–15 |
| Trunk/carry | Carry or Pallof press | 2 short efforts | 2 controlled efforts |

The numbers are starting ranges, not borders. Use less when technique or recovery requires it.

## Session B

| Exercise pattern | Accessible options | Weeks 1–2 | Weeks 3–12 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Knee/single-leg | Supported split squat or step-up | 1–2 × 6–10 each | 2–3 × 6–12 each |
| Hip-dominant | Hip thrust or kettlebell deadlift | 1–2 × 6–10 | 2–3 × 6–12 |
| Upper push | Push-up or dumbbell press | 1–2 × 8–12 | 2 × 8–12 |
| Upper pull | Row or pull-down | 1–2 × 8–12 | 2 × 8–12 |
| Trunk | Side plank or dead bug | 2 short efforts | 2 controlled efforts |

Use support on a split squat when balance hides the strength stimulus. Choose a machine when it improves control or progression. An exercise does not become inferior because it requires less coordination.

## Weeks 1–2: learn and tolerate

The first phase is not four weeks of generic mobility before “real” work. Movement learning and progressive loading can begin together using a suitable range, load and exercise variation.

Start with:

- one or two sessions per week;
- one or two work sets per movement;
- three or four good repetitions available at the end of each set;
- a controlled tempo and range; and
- at least one day before repeating the same demanding lower-body work.

A novice does not need a one-repetition-maximum test. Record the load, repetitions, sets and how the movement felt. Review the next 24–48 hours before adding anything.

New muscle soreness can occur, but soreness is not the goal. Joint pain, movement-altering pain or symptoms that worsen require regression and, where appropriate, qualified assessment.

## Weeks 3–6: build repeatability

Move toward two sessions per week when the first phase is stable. Use two work sets for the main knee- and hip-dominant movements. Six to twelve controlled repetitions is a useful beginner working range, not a magic physiological border.

Choose only one progression at a time:

1. add a repetition inside the planned range;
2. add a small amount of load; or
3. add one set to a selected main movement.

Do not increase repetitions, load, sets and exercise difficulty together. Repeatability is more useful than novelty.

## Weeks 7–12: progress selected movements

Keep the routine recognisable. A beginner does not need a new exercise menu every week.

When technique, joint comfort, recovery and riding are stable:

- progress two or three main movements;
- keep one to three technically sound repetitions available for most sets;
- use two or three work sets on selected main patterns;
- keep accessories at the smallest useful dose; and
- reduce work during race, travel or unusually demanding cycling weeks.

The 2025 [cyclist-only systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40632222/) found group-level benefits for cycling performance, efficiency and anaerobic power. It did not find significant pooled effects for VO2max, power at VO2max or maximal metabolic steady state, and the authors graded the evidence low certainty.

That supports progressing strength; it does not guarantee FTP gains from this exact plan.

## How hard should the sets feel?

For the first two weeks, finish most sets with three or four good repetitions available. From week three onward, selected work can move toward one to three repetitions available when technique and recovery are stable.

A 2026 [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42410632/) of 20 studies and 556 healthy adults found non-failure training as effective as failure training for most measured outcomes and slightly better for dynamic strength. It was not cyclist-specific, but it shows that exhausting every set is unnecessary.

Stop the set when:

- the movement changes materially;
- joint pain appears;
- balance becomes unsafe;
- unusual breathlessness or other concerning symptoms occur; or
- the planned repetitions no longer look controlled.

## Where to place the sessions

Protect the race, long ride or interval session most connected to the goal. Then choose one of two arrangements.

**Separate demanding sessions.** Put strength and the priority ride on different days or provide a longer gap. This is useful when either repeatedly loses quality on a combined day.

**Consolidate stress.** Complete the priority bike work first and lift later, then keep the next day easy. This can preserve full recovery days for time-crunched riders when both sessions remain productive.

A [same-session sequence meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/) found a small lower-body-strength advantage when resistance came first, but no sequence effect on aerobic capacity. That does not establish one compulsory cycling schedule.

Use the free [strength-session planner](/tools/strength-session-planner) to map one or two gym windows around your actual rides. It is a scheduling heuristic, not a recovery guarantee.

## Readiness: change the dose, not the goal

One poor sleep score or one low HRV reading does not diagnose under-recovery. Use a cluster:

- illness symptoms;
- sleep opportunity and continuity;
- energy and mood;
- muscle soreness and joint comfort;
- recent bike and gym load; and
- the next priority session.

When several signals worsen together, adjust in this order:

1. remove optional accessories;
2. reduce three sets to two;
3. keep familiar exercises and controlled load;
4. shorten the session; and
5. skip and reassess when illness, meaningful pain or unusual systemic fatigue makes training inappropriate.

Roadman's [training-readiness check](/tools/training-readiness) and [recovery screen](/tools/recovery-screen) organise the decision. They are not medical diagnoses.

## Do beginners need plyometrics?

No. Jump split squats and lateral bounds are not an automatic “power phase”. Strength, power, landing skill and tissue tolerance are separate qualities.

A rider may add low-volume explosive work when:

- the goal justifies it;
- controlled strength and landing mechanics are established;
- health and injury context make it appropriate;
- it can be performed competently or coached; and
- it does not damage the priority cycling work.

Continuing to progress controlled strength through week 12 is a valid beginner plan.

## How to measure whether the plan worked

Set a baseline in week one:

- load and repetitions on two or three main movements;
- one cycling outcome matched to the rider's goal;
- completion and quality of priority rides;
- body mass when power-to-weight matters; and
- soreness, pain and recovery response.

Repeat the same measures in week 12. A stronger goblet squat proves the squat improved. A cycling outcome is needed before claiming on-bike transfer.

Road cycling also should not be treated as a complete bone-loading strategy. A [cycling and bone-health systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23256921/) found road cycling does not appear to provide a meaningful osteogenic benefit and reported lower bone-mineral-density concerns in some groups. Riders with osteoporosis, previous low-trauma fracture or meaningful risk factors need individual clinical context.

## What comes after week 12

Do not automatically restart at week one or leap into an advanced power phase. Review the data.

- If technique and load progressed while the bike remained strong, continue progressing selected movements.
- If the gym improved but every key ride deteriorated, reduce volume or change placement.
- If a movement never became comfortable, choose a different variation or get qualified coaching.
- If cycling load is rising, keep familiar movements and reduce sets before adding novelty.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about pre-race hydration: you can drink two litres of water the morning of a sportive, and your body will pee most of it straight back out. Your blood volume barely changes. You feel hydrated. You're not. Not really.

Sodium loading fixes this. It's not a new idea — Andy Blow at Precision Hydration has been banging this drum for years, and the sports science has been solid since the early 2000s — but it's one of the most overlooked pre-race protocols in amateur cycling. You carb-load. You taper. You lay out your kit the night before. But almost nobody pre-loads their sodium, even though it takes 20 minutes and costs less than a gel.

Let me break this down.

## What Sodium Loading Actually Does

The mechanism is simple and well-understood. When you consume a concentrated sodium solution before exercise, your body retains more of the fluid you drink alongside it. Sodium creates an osmotic gradient that pulls water into your bloodstream and holds it there. Your plasma volume — the liquid portion of your blood — expands.

This matters because plasma volume is what determines how well your cardiovascular system works under heat stress. More plasma means more blood available for two competing demands: delivering oxygen to your muscles and sending blood to your skin for cooling. When plasma volume drops — through sweat, through inadequate pre-hydration — your body has to compromise. Heart rate drifts upward. Power output drops. You overheat.

Research from Sims and colleagues at the University of Otago showed that ingesting a sodium-loaded drink before exercise increased plasma volume by roughly 4 to 5 per cent compared to drinking the same volume of plain water. That might sound modest, but in a three-hour event in the heat, it's the difference between holding your target power in the final 45 minutes and watching it bleed away.

Here's where it gets really interesting. Drinking plain water before exercise can actually make things worse. Your body detects the dilution of blood sodium concentration and responds by increasing urine output — it's trying to maintain homeostasis. So you drink a litre of water, you urinate 700 millilitres of it, and you start the race with barely more plasma than if you'd drunk nothing at all.

Sodium loading tells your kidneys to hold onto that fluid. You drink the same amount of water, but your body keeps it.

## The Protocol: Timing, Dosing, Execution

The standard protocol that Andy Blow and Precision Hydration recommend — and that the research supports — is straightforward.

### Dose

Aim for 10 to 12 milligrams of sodium per kilogram of body weight. For a 75 kg rider, that's 750 to 900 milligrams of sodium in a single pre-race drink. In practice, most riders use 1,000 to 1,500 milligrams, because the products are dosed that way and the margin of error favours going slightly higher rather than slightly lower.

### Volume

Mix your sodium into 400 to 600 millilitres of water. The concentration matters — you want the drink to be noticeably salty but not unbearable. If you can't drink it, it doesn't work.

### Timing

Start sipping your sodium drink 90 minutes before the start. Finish it by 60 minutes before. This window gives your gut time to absorb the sodium, your kidneys time to adjust fluid retention, and your plasma volume time to expand — all before you clip in.

Drinking it too early (two to three hours before) means the excess sodium gets excreted before the start. Drinking it too late (30 minutes before) means it's still sitting in your stomach when the gun goes. Neither is useful.

### The Practical Steps

1. The night before your event, measure out your sodium source — salt, commercial sachet, or concentrated electrolyte drink.
2. Set an alarm for 90 minutes before your start time.
3. Mix the sodium into 500 millilitres of water.
4. Sip it steadily over 20 to 30 minutes. Don't gulp it down.
5. Follow it with small sips of plain water until 30 minutes before the start.
6. Urinate before you line up. You'll likely need to.

That's it. Twenty minutes of slightly unpleasant drinking, and you start the race with measurably more fluid in your bloodstream.

## Who Benefits Most

Sodium loading isn't equally useful for everyone. The benefit scales with three variables: how much you sweat, how salty your sweat is, and how hot and long the event is.

### Heavy Sweaters

Sweat rates among cyclists vary enormously. Some riders lose 500 millilitres per hour in moderate conditions. Others lose 1.5 litres. If you're at the high end — and you probably know if you are, because you're the one refilling bottles every 45 minutes — you're losing more plasma volume per hour, and starting with a bigger reservoir matters more.

### Salty Sweaters

This is the variable most cyclists don't think about. It's not just how much you sweat — it's how much sodium is in each litre of that sweat. The range is enormous: 200 to 2,000 milligrams of sodium per litre, depending on the individual.

Andy Blow has talked about this extensively. He's personally at the extreme high end — losing around 1,800 milligrams of sodium per litre of sweat — which is why he built Precision Hydration in the first place. Most people fall somewhere between 800 and 1,400 milligrams per litre, but the variation is mostly genetic and doesn't change much with training or acclimatisation.

If you're losing 1,500 milligrams of sodium per litre and you sweat 1.2 litres per hour, you're haemorrhaging 1,800 milligrams of sodium every 60 minutes. No amount of during-exercise electrolyte drink replaces that fully. Starting with expanded plasma volume buys you time before the deficit starts affecting performance.

### Hot Events

Temperature is the amplifier. In cool conditions — 10 to 15 degrees — sweat rates are lower and thermoregulation demands less blood flow to the skin. Your cardiovascular system isn't as compromised. In 30-degree heat, everything accelerates. Sweat rates double. The competition for blood flow between muscles and skin intensifies. Plasma volume becomes the limiting factor far earlier.

If you're doing a sportive in June or July, or you're racing in any summer event, sodium loading becomes significantly more valuable than it would be for a cold, wet March time trial.

### Long Events

The benefit compounds over time. For a 60-minute criterium in cool weather, the difference between pre-loading sodium and not is negligible. For a five-hour granfondo in 28-degree heat, it can be the difference between riding the last climb and being in the broom wagon.

The crossover point is roughly 90 minutes of hard effort. Below that, your starting plasma volume is probably sufficient. Above that, especially in heat, pre-loading starts paying dividends.

## DIY vs. Commercial Products

Let me be really clear about this: the active ingredient in every commercial sodium-loading product is sodium. The same sodium that's in the salt sitting in your kitchen cupboard. You don't need to spend $30 on branded sachets to get this right.

### The DIY Approach

Table salt is approximately 40 per cent sodium by weight. One level teaspoon of table salt contains roughly 2,300 milligrams of sodium. Half a teaspoon in 500 millilitres of water gives you about 1,150 milligrams of sodium — which is bang in the middle of the effective range for most riders.

Here's what that tastes like: salty. Noticeably salty. Not pleasant, but drinkable if you sip it rather than gulp it. Some riders add a squeeze of lemon or lime juice to mask the taste. Others add a small amount of no-sugar cordial. Whatever gets it down.

The good news is that it costs virtually nothing. You can sodium-load before every important ride for an entire season for less than the price of a single box of commercial sachets.

### The Commercial Approach

Precision Hydration's PH 1500 is the most widely used commercial option for sodium loading. A single sachet mixed in 500 millilitres delivers 1,500 milligrams of sodium — slightly higher than the DIY half-teaspoon method. It also tastes considerably better, which matters if you're the kind of person who gags on salt water.

The PH 1500 runs around $2.50 per sachet when you buy in packs of 20. Other commercial options include SaltStick caps (215 milligrams of sodium per capsule — you'd need five to seven for a pre-load dose), LMNT sachets (1,000 milligrams per sachet), and SiS GO Hydro tabs (though those are dosed for during-exercise use, not pre-loading).

Here's my honest take: if you race frequently and you know you're a heavy or salty sweater, the convenience and consistency of a commercial product is worth the money. You know exactly what you're getting every time. If you do two or three big events a year and you want to see whether sodium loading helps before committing, start with table salt. It works.

## Individualisation: Finding Your Own Numbers

The research gives us population averages, but hydration is deeply personal. Two riders on the same start line, in the same conditions, can have completely different sodium needs.

### Sweat Rate Testing

The simplest way to estimate your sweat rate: weigh yourself in minimal clothing before and after a one-hour ride in known conditions. Don't drink during the ride. Don't use the toilet. Every kilogram lost is approximately one litre of sweat.

Do this in different conditions — cool, moderate, hot — and you'll build a picture of your sweat rate across temperatures. That tells you how quickly you're losing plasma volume, and how much you benefit from starting with more.

### Sweat Sodium Concentration

This is harder to measure at home. Precision Hydration offers a sweat composition test at various locations (around $95 in the US) that measures your sweat sodium concentration precisely. It's a one-time test because your sweat sodium concentration is largely genetic and stable.

But you don't necessarily need a lab test. Here's a simple proxy that Andy Blow often talks about: look at your kit after a hard summer ride. If you see white salt residue on your jersey, your shorts, your helmet straps — that white crust is sodium chloride. The more visible the residue, the higher your sweat sodium concentration.

Other indicators of high sweat sodium:

- Your eyes sting from sweat running into them
- You crave salty food after hard rides
- You get a gritty or chalky feeling on your skin after riding
- You've experienced cramping late in long, hot events (not definitive, but suggestive)

If you tick several of those boxes, you're likely losing more sodium per litre than average, and sodium loading is more likely to benefit you.

### Putting Your Numbers Together

Once you have a rough sweat rate and a sense of whether you're a high or low sodium sweater, you can make smarter decisions about your pre-load dose.

| Sweat profile | Pre-load sodium dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Low sweater, low sodium | 500-750 mg | Minimal protocol; may still benefit in heat |
| Average sweater, average sodium | 750-1,000 mg | Standard protocol; the PH 1500 half-sachet range |
| Heavy sweater, high sodium | 1,000-1,500 mg | Full protocol; this is where it makes the biggest difference |
| Very heavy sweater, very high sodium | 1,500 mg | Top end; pair with aggressive on-bike sodium replacement |

The volume of water stays the same regardless: 400 to 600 millilitres. You're adjusting the sodium concentration, not the fluid volume.

## Practise in Training, Not on Race Day

This should go without saying, but I'm going to say it anyway because people ignore it every single season: never try sodium loading for the first time before a race.

The reason is simple. A concentrated sodium drink can cause GI distress in some people, particularly if you drink it too fast, on top of a heavy breakfast, or if you're anxious (which constricts gut blood flow). You need to know how your stomach responds before the stakes are high.

Pick a hard training ride — a club chain gang, a long weekend effort, anything with enough intensity that you'll notice the difference. Do the full protocol: mix your drink, sip it 90 minutes before, and ride. Pay attention to three things:

1. **Stomach tolerance.** Any bloating, nausea, or urgency? If yes, reduce the sodium concentration slightly next time, or extend the sipping window to 30 minutes instead of 20.
2. **Urine output.** You should need to urinate once about 30 to 45 minutes after finishing the drink. If you're urinating multiple times, the dose might be too high, or you might need to reduce the total fluid volume.
3. **How you feel in the final third of the ride.** This is harder to quantify on a single attempt, but over several trials, riders who benefit from sodium loading consistently report feeling more stable in the last 45 to 60 minutes — heart rate stays lower, power holds better, and the fade arrives later.

Do this two or three times before your target event. By race day, you'll know your dose, your timing, and your tolerance. No surprises.

## When NOT to Bother

Sodium loading isn't a blanket recommendation. There are clear situations where it's unnecessary, unhelpful, or potentially counterproductive.

### Short Rides

If your event is under 90 minutes, your starting plasma volume is almost certainly sufficient. The performance decline from plasma volume loss takes time to manifest — it's a function of cumulative sweat loss, not instantaneous effort. A 60-minute criterium in cool conditions doesn't create enough fluid deficit to justify pre-loading.

### Cool Conditions

In temperatures below 15 degrees, sweat rates drop significantly. The thermoregulatory demand — the blood-to-skin cooling mechanism that competes with blood-to-muscles delivery — is much lower. Your cardiovascular system isn't under the same pressure, so starting with extra plasma volume provides less marginal benefit.

### Low-Intensity Rides

A four-hour endurance ride at zone 2 in moderate conditions generates less heat and less sweat than a two-hour race at threshold. If you're riding easy, your normal hydration strategy is probably fine. Save the sodium loading for the days where effort and conditions combine to create real fluid stress.

### Riders With Hypertension

If you're managing high blood pressure, talk to your GP before adding 1,000 to 1,500 milligrams of sodium to your pre-race routine. For most healthy, active cyclists, the occasional sodium pre-load is harmless — you excrete the excess within hours. But if you're on medication for blood pressure, the interaction matters and needs a professional opinion.

### Already Sodium-Heavy Diets

If your baseline diet is already very high in sodium — heavily processed foods, lots of added salt — you may already have adequate sodium levels going into an event. Sodium loading assumes your starting point is a normal dietary sodium intake. If yours is already elevated, the marginal benefit shrinks.

## The Bigger Hydration Picture

Sodium loading doesn't replace good hydration practice during the ride. Think of it as expanding your starting reservoir, not as a substitute for drinking on the bike.

Your during-ride strategy should still include:

- **500 to 750 millilitres of fluid per hour**, adjusted for conditions and your sweat rate
- **500 to 800 milligrams of sodium per hour** in your bottles (via electrolyte mix, not plain water) for rides over 90 minutes
- **Consistent drinking from the first 15 minutes**, using a timer if you tend to forget

What sodium loading does is shift the entire curve. You start with more plasma volume, so the point at which dehydration starts affecting performance comes later. Instead of cardiac drift hitting at 90 minutes, it might not appear until 120 or 150 minutes. Instead of fading in the final hour, you're still holding your numbers.

The combination of sodium loading before the ride and proper electrolyte replacement during the ride is where the biggest gains sit. Either one alone helps. Together, they're significantly more effective.

## A Race-Week Sodium Timeline

Here's how sodium loading fits into the broader pre-race preparation. This is a practical timeline you can follow for any event over 90 minutes.

### 48 Hours Before

Focus on carbohydrate loading. Keep sodium intake normal — your regular diet provides enough. Don't start adding extra salt to meals yet.

### 24 Hours Before

Continue carb loading. Stay well hydrated with normal water intake. Avoid alcohol, which is a diuretic and counterproductive to everything you're trying to achieve.

### The Night Before

Eat your pre-race dinner. Include some naturally salty foods (soup, bread, pasta sauce) but don't force extra salt. Get to bed at a reasonable hour.

### Race Morning

Eat your pre-race breakfast three to four hours before the start. Drink 500 millilitres of water with breakfast.

### 90 Minutes Before Start

Begin sipping your sodium-loading drink: 1,000 to 1,500 milligrams of sodium in 500 millilitres of water. Finish it by 60 minutes before the start.

### 60 Minutes Before Start

Switch to small sips of plain water. Take your caffeine if using it. Urinate.

### 30 Minutes Before Start

Final pre-race gel or energy chews. Stop drinking. You should feel hydrated but not bloated.

### Start

Begin drinking from your bottles within the first 15 to 20 minutes. Your electrolyte mix on the bike takes over from here.

## The Bottom Line

Sodium loading is one of those rare interventions that's backed by solid science, costs almost nothing, takes minimal effort, and provides a measurable benefit for the riders who need it most. Heavy sweaters, salty sweaters, riders doing long events in the heat — if that's you, this protocol is worth adding to your pre-race routine.

The science isn't ambiguous. Pre-exercise sodium ingestion expands plasma volume, reduces cardiovascular strain, and delays the performance decline that comes with progressive dehydration. It won't turn a poorly trained rider into a contender. But it will stop a well-prepared rider from losing ground in the final hour because of something entirely fixable.

Try it in training first. Find your dose. Find your timing. Then use it when it counts.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about TSB: knowing the number is the easy part. Every cyclist with a TrainingPeaks account can see where their form line sits. The hard part — the part that separates people who peak well from people who arrive at races feeling either flat or buried — is knowing what to *do* with it.

If you need the background on what CTL, ATL, and TSB actually are and how the Banister model works, the [companion post covers the theory](/blog/training-load-ctl-atl-tsb-explained-cyclists). This is the practical application. You already know the definitions. What follows is how to use those numbers to arrive at your target event with the right legs, on the right day, by design rather than by luck.

## The race-day TSB target: +5 to +25

Let me be really clear about this. There is no magic number. There is a window, and for most amateur cyclists that window sits between +5 and +25 on race morning.

Below +5, you are still carrying meaningful fatigue. You might feel okay warming up, but 90 minutes into the race when the elastic is supposed to snap, it will not snap. The reserves that come from genuine freshness will not be there.

Above +25, you have probably rested too long. Fitness decays slowly — the 42-day time constant means your CTL drops only a couple of points per week during taper — but neuromuscular sharpness decays faster than the model shows. The legs lose that snappy, responsive feeling that comes from regular hard efforts. You arrive rested but flat, like a car that has been sitting in the garage for a month with a full tank but a flat battery.

The sweet spot within that window depends on you. Some riders perform best at +8. Others need +18 before the fatigue fog lifts. Here is where it gets really interesting: the variance between riders is bigger than most people expect. I have spoken to coaches on the podcast — Joe Friel, Stephen Barrett, John Wakefield — who all confirm the same observation: individual TSB targets can differ by 15 points between athletes they coach. The population average is a starting point, not a prescription.

## Finding your personal TSB sweet spot

This is the single most useful thing you can do with the Performance Management Chart over the course of a season: build a personal database.

After every race or hard group ride where you performed well — properly well, not just survived — note your TSB from that morning. Do the same after the days you felt terrible. After three or four data points in each column, a pattern will emerge. Maybe you raced brilliantly at +12, +10, and +14 but felt rubbish at +22 and +4. Now you know: your window is roughly +10 to +15, tighter than the population average, and you can taper to hit it deliberately.

The good news is this takes no extra equipment and no extra effort. Just a notebook and the discipline to record three numbers after your best and worst days: TSB, hours of sleep the night before, and a one-to-ten subjective readiness score. Over twelve months, that log becomes more valuable than any training plan.

One thing to watch: do not confuse race performance with race results. You might finish tenth but ride a personal best power for the course. That is a "performed well" data point regardless of the result. The TSB log tracks your physiology, not the strength of the field.

## The CTL trap

This is the most common mistake I see, and it is worth saying plainly because nearly everyone falls into it at least once.

CTL is seductive. It goes up when you train hard. It feels like a fitness score. And it is natural to start treating it like a high score — adding volume to push the number higher, skipping rest days because you do not want it to drop, filling recovery weeks with junk miles that serve no purpose except keeping the line from dipping.

Here is what most people do: they build CTL as high as possible, then try to taper down to a target TSB for their race. The logic seems sound. Higher CTL means higher fitness, right?

What actually works is different. A CTL of 80 built on well-structured polarised training — easy days actually easy, hard days truly hard, with proper recovery and race-specific intervals — will produce better racing than a CTL of 100 built on monotonous grey-zone riding, insufficient recovery, and no specificity. The PMC cannot distinguish between those two scenarios. Both show a nice, rising CTL line. Only one of them produces an athlete ready to race.

The question is never "how high is my CTL?" The question is "what went into building it?" If the answer is structured training with clear objectives for each session, good recovery practices, and progressive specificity toward your target event, then the CTL number — whatever it is — represents genuine, race-ready fitness. If the answer is maximum volume with minimum thought, the number is a comfortable fiction.

Let me break this down another way. Imagine two riders. Both have a CTL of 75 on the day they start their taper. Rider A got there with five quality sessions per week — two hard interval days, two endurance rides, one active recovery — plus a recovery week every fourth week. Rider B got there by riding hard six days a week with no recovery weeks, no interval structure, and no variation. Same CTL. Completely different preparedness. Rider A will respond to the taper with a sharp, race-ready peak. Rider B is so buried in accumulated fatigue that the taper will barely scratch the surface.

## Reading your PMC across a season

The Performance Management Chart tells a story across months, not days. Here is how to read the shapes.

**The build block.** Three weeks of progressive loading followed by a recovery week. On the PMC, CTL climbs steadily across the three build weeks. TSB drops into the minus 10 to minus 25 range — uncomfortable but productive. ATL is elevated, reflecting the week-to-week fatigue accumulation. In the recovery week, volume drops, ATL crashes, TSB swings back toward zero, and CTL gives back a point or two. That small CTL loss is the cost of recovery. It is always worth paying.

**The staircase.** Zoom out to three months and you should see CTL climbing in a staircase pattern — three steps up, one step back, three steps up, one step back. Each "landing" (the recovery week) sits higher than the one before. This is progressive overload made visible. If the staircase is flat, the training stimulus is not progressing. If it is a straight line with no steps, you are not recovering.

**The pre-race taper.** In the final two weeks before a target event, the PMC shows a distinctive pattern: CTL holds roughly steady or drops slightly, ATL falls sharply, and TSB climbs from negative territory into the positive window. The divergence between the fatigue line falling and the form line rising is the visual signature of a well-timed taper. If TSB is climbing but CTL is dropping like a stone, the taper started too early or cut too much.

**The post-race dip.** After a target event and the recovery that follows, CTL will drop. This is fine. It is the natural consequence of reducing training load after a peak. The drop usually bottoms out after two to three weeks, and the next build block starts from a slightly lower CTL baseline. This is normal periodisation, not a crisis.

## The taper protocol: what to cut and what to keep

The mechanics of raising TSB for race day come down to a simple trade: reduce fatigue (ATL) faster than you lose fitness (CTL). Because ATL uses a 7-day time constant and CTL uses a 42-day constant, this is mathematically guaranteed to work — as long as you get the dosing right.

**Volume is what you cut.** Drop total weekly training volume by 40 to 60 per cent. If you normally ride 12 hours a week, taper weeks should be 5 to 7 hours. This is where the fatigue reduction comes from. Every low-TSS day pulls ATL down, and ATL drops fast.

**Intensity is what you keep.** Maintain two short, sharp sessions per week during the taper. These are not long threshold efforts. They are brief, punchy openers — 4 to 6 intervals of 30 seconds to 2 minutes at race pace or above, with full recovery between efforts. The purpose is neuromuscular: keeping the fast-twitch fibres firing, maintaining pedalling efficiency at high cadences, preserving the snap that prolonged rest erodes.

**Duration is what you shorten.** Your interval sessions get shorter but not easier. A build-phase session of 5x5 minutes at VO2max becomes 3x3 minutes. A threshold session of 2x20 minutes becomes 2x10. The stimulus stays sharp; the volume drops. This is the key distinction that most riders get wrong. They cut intensity along with volume and arrive at the race rested but blunt.

**Timeline: 10 to 14 days for a single-day event.** Start the taper 10 to 14 days before your target race. For most riders, 12 days is the sweet spot — long enough for meaningful fatigue clearance, short enough that fitness does not erode. For a stage race or multi-day event, shorten to 7 to 10 days. You need more residual fitness for sustained efforts across multiple days, so the taper is shorter and less aggressive.

**What a two-week taper costs.** Expect to lose 5 to 10 CTL points over a 14-day taper. That is normal. What you gain in return — a TSB swing of 20 to 30 points from pre-taper to race day — is a far better trade. The research consistently shows a well-executed taper delivers a 2 to 3 per cent performance improvement. On a 60-minute climb, that is nearly two minutes. On a 40-kilometre time trial, that is over a minute. The CTL cost is trivially small compared to the performance gain.

## TSB is a lagging indicator — plan backwards

Here is a mistake I see constantly. A rider checks their TSB on Monday, sees it at minus 15, panics because their race is on Saturday, and slashes everything to try to get the number positive by the weekend.

That is not how this works.

The TSB you see today reflects the training decisions you made 7 to 10 days ago. It is a trailing indicator, not a real-time readout. The fatigue from last weekend's big ride is still washing through the 7-day exponential average. Today's rest day will not show up meaningfully in your TSB until midweek.

This means taper planning works backwards from race day, not forwards from today. You look at the calendar, find your race date, count back 12 days, and that is where the taper begins. The decisions about what to ride in those 12 days are made in advance, not reactively adjusted based on yesterday's number.

In practice, this means your taper week plan should be written before it starts. The daily TSB readings during taper are confirmation that the plan is tracking, not inputs for last-minute changes. If TSB is climbing roughly as expected, stay the course. If it is climbing faster than expected, you might add one short session. If it is not climbing at all, something unusual is happening — poor sleep, illness, life stress — and the response should address the cause, not pile on more rest.

The riders who peak well are the ones who trust the plan. The ones who peak badly are the ones who react to every daily TSB reading like it is a stock price.

## What TSB cannot tell you

TSB is a model, and every model has blind spots. Here is what the number leaves out.

**Sleep.** A rider sleeping six hours a night and a rider sleeping eight hours a night can have identical TSB values. Their readiness to perform is not remotely similar. Sleep is the single largest recovery variable that the PMC ignores entirely. If your TSB says +15 but you have slept badly for five nights, trust the sleep data over the model.

**Nutrition.** A rider fuelling properly during and after training sessions recovers faster and more completely than one who is chronically under-eating or skipping post-ride nutrition. The PMC treats both identically. A TSB of +12 on race morning means something very different if you have spent the taper eating well versus if you have been restricting calories to "make race weight."

**Life stress.** Work deadlines, family pressure, moving house, relationship strain — none of it appears on the PMC. But all of it affects the autonomic nervous system, suppresses recovery, disrupts sleep, and changes the effective fatigue load your body is carrying. I have seen riders with textbook TSB values perform terribly because their life outside cycling was absorbing all the recovery capacity that was supposed to go toward the taper.

**Specificity.** TSB does not know what kind of training produced the load. A CTL of 80 built entirely on flat Zone 2 rides will not prepare you for a hilly road race. A CTL of 80 built on indoor trainer sessions will not prepare you for a gravel event in hot conditions. The model counts stress. It does not evaluate whether that stress matches the demands of the event you are tapering for. That judgement sits with you and your training plan.

**Accumulated fatigue across seasons.** TSB resets its fatigue calculation every 7 days. But fatigue from two years of hard training without a proper off-season does not follow a 7-day decay curve. Deep, systemic fatigue — the kind that comes from year after year of pushing without adequate breaks — sits below the model's resolution. You can have a positive TSB and still be chronically tired.

## Warning signs in your PMC

The Performance Management Chart is at its most valuable not when things are going well, but when things are going wrong. Here are the patterns that should make you pause.

**TSB stuck below minus 30 for more than two weeks.** A planned overreach block might dip below minus 30 for a few days. That is fine if it is deliberate and followed by genuine recovery. But if TSB has been below minus 30 for two weeks or more and is not climbing, you are in overreaching territory. The risk of illness, injury, and burnout rises sharply. Back off. Add rest days. Reduce volume by 50 per cent for a week and watch whether TSB recovers. If it does not, something deeper is going on.

**CTL rising but performance falling.** This is the classic overtraining precursor. You are doing the work, the numbers say you should be getting fitter, but your power numbers in sessions are stagnant or dropping, your heart rate is elevated at rest, and you feel terrible. The PMC shows a healthy picture, but the body is telling a different story. When CTL and performance diverge, always trust performance.

**Form that never recovers.** You take a recovery week, TSB climbs back toward zero, but you still feel flat. You take another easy week, TSB goes positive, and you still feel awful. When subjective feel and TSB disagree persistently, the model is missing something — usually sleep deficit, nutritional inadequacy, or life stress. The fix is not more rest. The fix is identifying what the model cannot see and addressing it directly.

**Flat CTL for months.** If CTL has not moved in eight weeks despite consistent training, you have hit a plateau. The training stimulus is no longer sufficient to force adaptation. This usually means the intensity distribution needs to change — more time at the extremes (easy or hard), less time in the moderate middle — or the overall volume needs to step up. A flat CTL line is not necessarily a problem if you are in a maintenance phase, but if you expect to be getting fitter and the line disagrees, something needs to change.

## Masters-specific considerations

If you are over 40, the standard TSB model needs a mental adjustment. Not because the maths is wrong — it is the same model for everyone — but because the time constants it uses are population averages that skew toward younger athletes.

The practical reality for masters cyclists: fatigue takes longer to clear. The model uses a 7-day time constant for ATL, but many riders over 40 have an effective fatigue decay closer to 9 or 10 days. The consequence is that the TSB number on your screen is slightly more optimistic than your actual readiness. The model says your fatigue should have cleared by Wednesday; your legs say Thursday or Friday.

This is why masters riders typically perform best at the higher end of the TSB window — closer to +20 than +10. Not because they need more rest in theory, but because the model underestimates how much residual fatigue they carry. An extra few days of taper costs a negligible amount of CTL but allows the fatigue that the model does not fully capture to clear.

Three practical adjustments for masters riders:

**Extend your taper by two to three days.** If the standard recommendation is 12 days, plan 14 to 15. The additional days allow the slower fatigue clearance to finish its work. You will lose an extra CTL point or two, but you will arrive with properly fresh legs rather than legs the model says should be fresh.

**Target TSB +15 to +25 for your A-race.** Younger riders can get away with racing at +5 to +10 because their fatigue clears on schedule. Masters riders who race at +5 often find that residual fatigue — invisible to the model — dulls the performance. Push the target higher and see whether your race-day legs improve.

**Watch recovery weeks more carefully.** During a recovery week, track whether TSB actually returns to zero or stays negative. If you consistently finish recovery weeks with a TSB of minus 5 or worse, your recovery weeks are not recovering you. Reduce the volume further, or extend the recovery to five days instead of four. The recovery has to actually land, not just appear to land on the chart.

## Putting it together

TSB is not the answer. It is a very good question — asked in numbers — about whether you are ready. Pair it with subjective feel, sleep data, and awareness of what life is throwing at you, and it becomes legitimately predictive. Treat it as gospel and it will mislead you at least as often as it helps.

The riders I see peak well for their target events share a few traits. They build fitness deliberately, with quality sessions and real recovery. They plan their taper backwards from race day, not forwards from wherever they happen to be. They know their personal TSB target from experience rather than from a generic chart. And they treat the PMC as one input among several, not as the single source of truth.

If you do nothing else after reading this: go back to your three best races or hard efforts from the past twelve months, note the TSB from each, and find the pattern. That one step will tell you more about how to peak for your next event than any amount of theory.

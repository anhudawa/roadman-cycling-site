**Joe Friel, Dan Lorang and Dylan Johnson do not teach one combined cycling training-plan method.** Friel describes a way to organise a season around an important event. Lorang explains how to make load repeatable within the athlete's whole life. Johnson documented an unusually high-volume experiment for his own 2025 gravel season.

That distinction matters. Turning the three interviews into one formula creates prescriptions none of the sources made. This page compares their actual positions, identifies the evidence type behind each one and shows what an amateur can use without copying an elite calendar.

## Source ledger

- **[Joe Friel on the Roadman Cycling Podcast, 11 August 2025](/podcast/ep-40-how-joe-friel-structures-the-ideal-cycling-training-week).** Establishes what Friel said about working backwards from an A-priority race, taper/build timing, training frequency and athlete context. It is an expert interview, not a controlled comparison of planning models.
- **[Joe Friel's K.I.S.S. Periodization article](https://joefrieltraining.com/kiss-periodization/).** Establishes Friel's own definition, phase names and typical—not mandatory—phase lengths. It describes his coaching framework.
- **[Dan Lorang on the Roadman Cycling Podcast, 29 April 2024](/podcast/ep-2134-roglics-coach-builds-a-training-plan-for-amateur-riders-dan).** Establishes Lorang's views on sustainable load, recovery, context, heart rate, power and sleep. It was recorded during his Bora–Hansgrohe tenure and is not a fixed amateur calendar.
- **[Lidl-Trek role announcement, effective 1 August 2026](https://racing.trekbikes.com/stories/lidl-trek/a-new-chapter-begins-lidl-trek-announces-leadership-transition-following-the-2026-tour-de-france).** Establishes Lorang's current Head of Performance role, not the effectiveness of a training method.
- **[Dylan Johnson on the Roadman Cycling Podcast, 2 April 2025](/podcast/ep-2062-dylan-johnson-reveals-his-weird-2025-training-plan).** Establishes the structure, purpose and caveats of Johnson's own 2025 experiment. It was an elite N=1 plan discussed before the target season result was known.
- **[2023 systematic review of periodisation in trained cyclists](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36640771/).** Establishes the size and direction of the comparative cycling evidence base: seven eligible studies and no universal individual prescription.

The dates are deliberate. They stop a 2024 interview being used as proof of a 2026 job title, or a pre-season experiment being retold as a validated result.

## Comparison at a glance

### Joe Friel

- **Core idea:** work backwards from the event and make training increasingly specific to its demands.
- **Evidence type:** longstanding coaching framework and expert interview.
- **Best use:** organising a season and asking what becomes more race-like.
- **Do not turn it into:** one compulsory phase length, intensity ratio or number of A-races for every rider.

### Dan Lorang

- **Core idea:** choose load the athlete can repeat and recover from after work, family, travel, sleep and other demands are counted.
- **Evidence type:** elite-practitioner interview.
- **Best use:** testing whether a planned week is sustainable and interpreting data in context.
- **Do not turn it into:** a universal “80% for 12 weeks” rule or a scaled-down WorldTour calendar.

### Dylan Johnson

- **Core idea:** alternate very high- and lower-volume weeks for his own Unbound-focused preparation.
- **Evidence type:** elite N=1 self-experiment.
- **Best use:** understanding how one athlete tested concentrated volume.
- **Do not turn it into:** a validated amateur oscillation protocol.

## Joe Friel: race-back periodisation

In the Roadman interview, Friel begins with an A-priority event and works backwards. He describes roughly two weeks as an adequate taper for many cyclists and approximately eight to ten weeks of race-specific build work before it. In his own [K.I.S.S. explanation](https://joefrieltraining.com/kiss-periodization/), his simplest definition is that training should become more like the race as the event gets closer.

That is more useful than memorising a calendar. “More like the race” can refer to the frequency, intensity and duration of race-relevant work. A criterium and a six-hour mountainous event do not require the same rehearsal. Friel's published table gives typical lengths—such as a twelve-week base and an eight-to-nine-week build—but his argument is about purpose and progression, not dates detached from the rider.

The 2025 interview also contains an important qualification that summaries often lose. When Anthony Walsh asks for the perfect distribution of ten weekly hours, Friel says there is no perfect distribution for everybody and asks about the athlete's lifestyle. He then uses five available riding days as an example and stresses frequent, regular training. The example is conditional, not a five-day law.

Friel's own account of the [revised Cyclist's Training Bible](https://joefrieltraining.com/the-all-new-cyclists-training-bible/) reinforces that boundary: later editions moved beyond one simple seasonal method and one way of training every rider, adding several planning methods and individualisation by rider characteristics.

**What an amateur can use:** define the target demand, work backwards, decide what should become more specific and protect enough consistency to complete it.

**What the source does not prove:** that every cyclist needs the same base length, two-week taper, weekly intensity split or single-race calendar.

## Dan Lorang: sustainable load in context

The cited Lorang interview is not a week-by-week “Roglič plan for amateurs.” Lorang's practical message is that training load and recovery must balance over time. Professionals may accept load close to the limit because performance is their occupation; an amateur needs a larger reserve because training sits beside work, family, appointments, travel and sleep.

He also resists interpreting one number alone. Power records the external work; heart rate helps show the body's response. Perceived effort and context help explain why the relationship changes with fatigue, heat, illness or stress. More data is useful only when it improves a decision.

For recovery, Lorang places sleep first. That is not a promise that sleep fixes every problem; it is his priority when deciding which recovery behaviour is dependable and does not interfere with the training adaptation.

Lorang is now [Head of Performance at Lidl-Trek](https://racing.trekbikes.com/stories/lidl-trek/a-new-chapter-begins-lidl-trek-announces-leadership-transition-following-the-2026-tour-de-france), effective 1 August 2026. The Roadman conversation was recorded in April 2024 while he was at Bora–Hansgrohe. Both facts are stated so the page does not silently rewrite the historical interview.

### Correction: the unsupported 80% claim

An earlier version of this article said Lorang told Roadman that “80% of the value” of a plan came from executing it consistently for twelve weeks. We reviewed the cited transcript and could not substantiate that percentage or timeframe. The attribution has been removed.

The supported point is narrower: consistent, repeatable load needs adequate recovery. That idea does not require a fabricated number to be useful.

**What an amateur can use:** put the rest of life on the calendar, choose a load that leaves an appropriate reserve, and interpret power, heart rate and perceived effort together.

**What the source does not prove:** a fixed weekly volume, a mandatory recovery cycle or a mathematical conversion between work stress and training load.

## Dylan Johnson: an elite 2025 self-experiment

Johnson described alternating approximately 30–35-hour weeks with 10–15-hour weeks while preparing for the demands of Unbound and his 2025 gravel calendar. He called the approach “oscillation training” because volume moved sharply between high and low weeks.

The strongest part of the interview is Johnson's own caveat. He said the pattern was **not the gold standard**, that there was not good research on the exact theory and that he did not know a study testing repeated high-volume/low-volume alternation in that form. At the time, the Lifetime Grand Prix season had not started, so early power numbers could not establish whether the approach would deliver the target race outcome.

That makes the interview a valuable N=1 case, not weak content. It shows how an elite athlete identified an event demand, chose an unusual stimulus and accepted uncertainty. The mistake is changing its evidence label from “self-experiment” to “proven model.”

A 2019 systematic review found small favourable effects for block periodisation on some variables but cautioned that studies were small and generally low quality ([Mølmen, Øfsteng and Rønnestad, PubMed 31802956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31802956/)). It did not test Johnson's exact 30–35/10–15-hour sequence and cannot validate it by resemblance.

**What an amateur can use:** the habit of stating the hypothesis, event demand, measures and stop conditions before trying a training change.

**What the source does not prove:** that alternating twelve- and six-hour weeks is a safe or effective “scaled” version for an employed rider.

## Where the three approaches genuinely overlap

The overlap is a decision process, not a formula:

1. **Name the target.** The event or performance task determines what specificity means.
2. **Count the athlete's context.** Recent training, available time, health, sleep, work and family change what is repeatable.
3. **Choose a priority.** A block cannot maximise every adaptation at once.
4. **Create enough consistency to observe a response.** Constantly changing the method makes interpretation difficult.
5. **Review and adapt.** Compare completed work, subjective response and relevant performance measures with the original purpose.

This is also the boundary between a calendar and a plan. The calendar says what was scheduled. The plan explains why it was selected and what evidence will change it.

## What independent research can and cannot decide

A 2023 systematic review found only seven eligible studies describing periodisation, intensity distribution and volume in trained road cyclists. Traditional, block and day-by-day approaches all produced improvements in included studies, and the authors found no evidence favouring one periodisation model over eight to twelve weeks ([Galán-Rioja et al., PubMed 36640771](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36640771/)).

Intensity distribution is a separate question from seasonal periodisation. A 2025 individual-participant network meta-analysis found no overall difference between polarised and pyramidal interventions for VO2max or time-trial performance when intensity was calculated by heart-rate time in zone; athlete level also modified the response ([Rosenblat et al., PubMed 39888556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/)).

These reviews do not say that structure is irrelevant. They say the evidence does not award one famous label to every cyclist. Expert frameworks can organise decisions, but the strength of a recommendation depends on the population, intervention, outcome and individual response.

## A safe synthesis for a self-coached rider

Do not average the three approaches. Use them as questions in this order.

### 1. Write the rider brief

Record the goal and date, event demands, last eight to twelve weeks of completed training, protectable hours and days, longest available session, relevant health context, equipment, work, travel and family constraints.

### 2. Use Friel's race-back question

Ask what must become more event-specific and when. Use phase labels only if they make the purpose clearer. Do not assign a standard number of weeks before checking the starting point and event date.

### 3. Apply Lorang's sustainability check

Ask whether the athlete can repeat the proposed load while recovering within the rest of life. Keep reserve for disruption. Use power, heart rate and perceived effort as complementary signals rather than allowing one noisy metric to govern the week.

### 4. Treat experiments as experiments

If testing a different block structure, write the hypothesis, the smallest meaningful change, the measures, the review date and the conditions for stopping. Do not borrow Johnson's volume or infer a fixed amateur percentage from it.

### 5. Set modification rules before the week starts

Decide what happens after illness, persistent pain, repeated session failure, travel or a missed workout. The default should not be to stack missed work into the next available day.

## What not to copy

- fixed base, build, peak or taper lengths without checking the rider and event;
- Johnson's elite weekly volume or an invented percentage-scaled version;
- the unverified claim that Lorang prescribed an 80% value or twelve-week rule;
- one universal 80/20, polarised or pyramidal prescription;
- a mandatory three-to-one recovery cycle for every athlete;
- a fixed number of hard sessions based only on weekly hours;
- daily HRV, resting heart rate, weight or power readings treated as commands;
- professional load with amateur recovery time.

## Scope and safety

This comparison is educational and does not diagnose illness, injury, low energy availability or overtraining. Stop or reduce training and seek suitable clinical assessment for persistent pain, chest symptoms, fainting, unexplained performance loss or illness. A coach's reputation does not replace medical scope, and a training plan should state when referral is required.

The practical Roadman position is simple: borrow the questions, preserve the evidence labels and build around the rider. The more famous the source, the more important it is to quote the boundary as carefully as the headline.

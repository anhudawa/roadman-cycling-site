I have watched hundreds of riders line up for sportives and road races. Within the first five minutes, you can tell who prepared and who did not. The prepared riders are quiet, focused, already rolling through their warm-up. The unprepared ones are borrowing pumps, frantically tapping at head units, or asking strangers whether the feed station serves real food or just water.

Race preparation does not start when the alarm goes off at 5am. It starts 48 hours before the gun. Everything between now and the start line is either building your readiness or eroding it, and the margins are thinner than most people realise.

This is the complete guide to the 48-hour preparation window — everything from bike servicing to breakfast, kit to caffeine, warm-up to start line. We have also built a free [Race Day Checklist tool](/tools/race-day-checklist) that walks through every step interactively, so nothing gets missed.

## The 48-Hour Window

Professional teams do not wake up on race morning and improvise. They begin their preparation days in advance, with mechanics, soigneurs, nutritionists, and directeurs sportifs each running through their own checklists. You do not have a team car or a personal mechanic, which means you need to be your own support crew — and that means starting early.

### Bike Preparation

Two days out is the time to deal with anything mechanical. Not the night before, when you discover the problem with no time to fix it. Not race morning, when the bike shop is closed and your mate with the torque wrench is still asleep.

**The 48-hour bike check:**

- **Tyres.** Inspect for cuts, embedded glass, and sidewall damage. If the tread is worn smooth, replace them now. If you are running tubeless, check sealant levels — dried-out sealant is no sealant at all. Fresh tyres for a target event is not extravagant; it is sensible.
- **Chain.** Clean it, measure it, lube it. A worn chain skips under load, and there is no worse time to discover this than on the first steep climb of a sportive. If your chain checker shows 0.75% wear or more, replace it.
- **Brakes.** Squeeze both levers hard and check for pad wear, rotor rub, and cable stretch. Disc brakes should not squeal, and rim brakes should not bottom out on the bar. Adjust now.
- **Shifting.** Run through every gear. Front and rear. Under load if possible, on a turbo trainer. A skipping rear mech at 350 watts is a distraction you do not need.
- **Bolts.** Saddle, stem, handlebars, bottle cages, cleats. A quick check with an Allen key takes two minutes and prevents the saddle-slipping-mid-race nightmare that ruins entire events.

### Equipment

Pack your spares 48 hours out, not the morning of. You need:

- Two inner tubes (or a tubeless repair kit with plugs and a spare tube as backup)
- CO2 inflator with at least two cartridges, or a mini pump — ideally both
- Tyre levers
- Multi-tool with Allen keys and a chain breaker
- Emergency cash and identification
- Phone, fully charged

If you are driving to the event, pack this bag and put it by the front door. If you are riding to the start, mount what you can and bag the rest in jersey pockets.

### Kit Selection and Weather

Check the forecast — not the five-day outlook, the hourly forecast for your specific region on race day. Then lay out kit for the expected conditions, plus one layer for the conditions you hope to avoid.

A rain gilet weighs nothing and fits in a jersey pocket. Arm warmers take up less space than a gel. There is no award for suffering through an unexpected cold descent because you chose to leave the extra layer at home.

If the event requires a race number, pin it on the night before. Four safety pins, pulled tight so it does not flap. This takes thirty seconds the night before and five anxious minutes on race morning when your hands are shaking from cold and caffeine.

### Route Study

Know where you are going. Not vaguely — specifically.

- Where are the climbs? How long, how steep, and at what kilometre mark?
- Where are the feed stations? What do they typically offer?
- Where does the road surface change? Are there gravel sections, rough tarmac, or cattle grids?
- Where are the descents, and are any of them technical?
- What is the prevailing wind direction, and which sections will be exposed?

Load the route onto your head unit 48 hours out. Check that the file works, the turn-by-turn cues are accurate, and the device is fully charged. On race morning is too late to discover a corrupted GPX file.

## Why Most DNFs Start the Night Before

There is a persistent myth in amateur cycling that DNFs are caused by insufficient fitness. The rider simply was not strong enough, did not train hard enough, did not have the legs. Sometimes that is true. But far more often, the DNF starts long before the point where the rider climbs off.

The data from large sportives is consistent: the majority of amateur DNFs are attributable to mechanical failure or nutritional collapse, not lack of fitness. A flat tyre with no spare tube. A seized derailleur that could have been caught with a thirty-second check. A bonk at kilometre 80 because the rider ate one gel in the first three hours and then wondered why their legs stopped working.

None of these are bad luck. They are preparation failures.

**Mechanical failures** are almost entirely preventable. The flat tyre is just a flat tyre — it becomes a DNF only when there is no spare tube, no pump, and no phone signal to call for help. The snapped chain is an inconvenience with a chain breaker and five minutes of patience; it is a race-ending disaster without one.

**Nutritional failures** follow a predictable pattern. The rider either fuels too little (the classic bonk), fuels too late (playing catch-up with glycogen depletion), or fuels with something untested (the mystery gel from the feed station that triggers gastrointestinal revolt). All three are preventable with a plan made the day before.

Use the [In-Ride Fuelling Calculator](/tools/fuelling) to work out your carbs-per-hour target before the event. Write it on a piece of tape and stick it to your top tube. Set a timer on your head unit to remind you every 20 minutes. Fuelling is not something you should be thinking about during the race — it should be automated, mechanical, and boring.

## Nutrition Loading: The 48-Hour Protocol

Carb loading is one of the most misunderstood practices in amateur cycling. The typical approach — eating an enormous bowl of pasta the night before — is not carb loading. It is overeating. Actual carb loading is a deliberate, spread-out protocol that starts 48 hours before the event.

### The Science

The target is 8-10g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight per day, spread across the final 48 hours before the event. For a 75kg rider, that is 600-750g of carbohydrate per day — a significant amount that requires conscious effort to reach.

This is not about one meal. It is about every meal. Breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks across two full days, all biased heavily toward carbohydrate.

### What That Looks Like in Practice

**Breakfast:** Large bowl of porridge with honey and banana, plus toast with jam. Or a stack of pancakes with maple syrup. The point is volume and carbohydrate density, not culinary ambition.

**Lunch:** Rice bowl with chicken, or a large pasta dish with a light sauce. White rice and white pasta are preferable to wholegrains here — lower fibre means less GI bulk going into the event.

**Dinner:** Similar to lunch. Rice, pasta, or potatoes as the centrepiece, with a modest protein portion. Nothing spicy, nothing rich, nothing you have not eaten before a hard training day.

**Snacks:** Rice cakes, crumpets, energy bars, fruit, jam sandwiches. Sip a sports drink between meals to add carbohydrate without additional food volume.

### Hydration

Hydration in the 48-hour window is about consistency, not volume. Sipping water and electrolyte drinks throughout the day keeps plasma volume topped up without overloading the kidneys. The old advice to "drink until your urine is clear" overshoots — pale straw-coloured is the target.

Do not over-hydrate the night before. Excessive fluid intake before bed means disrupted sleep from bathroom trips, which defeats the purpose.

## Kit Preparation and the Night-Before Protocol

The night before a race, I lay everything out. Jersey, bib shorts, socks, shoes, helmet, gloves, glasses, gilet. Everything I will wear, in the order I will put it on, on the floor next to the bed. My race bag — with spares, nutrition, bottles, and number — sits by the front door.

This is not obsessive. It is the opposite: it is lazy. It removes every decision from race morning so that my half-awake brain does not need to think. The concept is sometimes called a "zero-decision morning," and it is borrowed from military and aviation protocols where the stakes of a forgotten item are considerably higher than a missed sportive.

### The Night-Before Checklist

- **Pin your race number** to your jersey. Four pins. Do it now, while your hands are steady and the light is good.
- **Fill your bottles.** One with water, one with electrolyte mix — or whatever your tested protocol calls for. Put them in the fridge.
- **Load gels into jersey pockets.** Count them against your fuelling plan. If you need six gels for a 160km sportive, do not pack five and hope for the best.
- **Charge your head unit.** And your phone. And your rear light. Plug everything in and leave it overnight.
- **Load your route** onto your head unit if you have not already. Check it displays correctly.
- **Set two alarms.** The first at wake-up time, the second five minutes later. If one fails, the other catches it. The cost of two alarms is zero. The cost of sleeping through the start is the entire event.
- **Lay out your kit.** Everything. Including chamois cream, sunscreen, and arm warmers. If you might need it, it should be visible.

### The Night-Before Dinner

A carb-rich dinner, eaten at least three hours before you intend to sleep. The meal should be familiar — something you have eaten before hard training days, something your stomach knows and trusts. This is not the night for a new restaurant, a spicy curry, or a rich cream sauce.

Rice and chicken. Pasta with a simple tomato sauce. Baked potato with tuna. The meal does not need to be exciting. It needs to be reliable.

### Sleep

Here is the truth about pre-race sleep: you probably will not sleep well. Pre-event anxiety is normal. The unfamiliar bed (if you have travelled), the early alarm, the racing thoughts about tomorrow — all of it conspires against a good night's rest.

The good news is that this matters less than you think. Research on sleep deprivation and performance suggests that one poor night has minimal impact on next-day physical performance, provided you have slept well in the preceding days. And lying quietly in a dark room — even without sleeping — provides roughly 80% of the restorative benefit of actual sleep.

So do not stress about not sleeping. Set your alarms, get into bed at a reasonable hour, and rest. If sleep comes, excellent. If it does not, you will still be fine.

## Race Morning: The Three-Hour Countdown

The alarm goes off. Here is what happens next, in order.

### T-minus 3 Hours: Breakfast

Eat breakfast 2-3 hours before the start. This gives your body time to digest, absorb, and begin processing the carbohydrate without sending you to the start line feeling bloated or nauseous.

**What to eat:** Familiar food. Porridge with honey. Toast with jam and peanut butter. Rice cakes. A banana. Whatever you have rehearsed in training. Aim for 1-2g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight — enough to top up liver glycogen without overloading the stomach.

**What not to eat:** Anything new. Anything high in fat or fibre. Anything from a hotel breakfast buffet that you have never tried before. Your gastrointestinal system does not care about optimal macronutrient ratios if it rejects the food entirely. Familiar and tolerated beats theoretically optimal every single time.

### T-minus 60 Minutes: Caffeine

If you are a regular coffee drinker, take your [caffeine](/blog/cycling-caffeine-performance) 30-60 minutes before the start. The research supports 3-6mg per kilogram of body weight for an endurance performance benefit. For a 75kg rider, that is 225-450mg — roughly 3-5 strong espressos or 1-2 caffeine tablets.

If you do not normally drink coffee, do not start on race morning. The GI side effects of caffeine in a non-habitual user — urgency, cramping, loose stools — will negate any performance benefit and then some. Stick with water.

### T-minus 30 Minutes: Final Preparation

- **Chamois cream.** Apply generously. There is no such thing as too much chamois cream for an event over two hours.
- **Sunscreen.** On exposed skin. Ears, neck, and the backs of hands are commonly missed.
- **Final tyre pressure check.** Tyres lose pressure overnight. A quick squeeze or a gauge reading takes ten seconds.
- **Head unit on, route loaded, recording started.** Confirm GPS lock before you leave. Starting to record after you have been riding for ten minutes means losing that data permanently.
- **Emergency contact information on your person.** A card in your jersey pocket or a wristband with your name, blood type, emergency contact, and any allergies. This is not pessimism — it is responsibility.

### T-minus 15 Minutes: Warm-Up

Ten minutes of easy spinning with 2-3 short efforts of 10-15 seconds at high cadence. That is all. You are not trying to tire yourself out. You are priming the engine.

The physiology is straightforward: cold muscles produce less peak power, and the cardiorespiratory system takes longer to reach steady state when it starts from rest. A short warm-up raises muscle temperature, increases blood flow, and primes the neuromuscular pathways that you will need in the first few kilometres.

For sportives and road races that start at moderate pace, ten minutes is sufficient. For time trials, criteriums, or any event that starts at or above threshold, extend to 15-20 minutes and include progressive efforts up to race pace. Arriving at the start line with a raised heart rate and warm legs is a significant advantage when the first ten minutes are fast.

If the venue does not allow a warm-up ride — some events have restricted areas around the start — spin on a turbo trainer, do ten minutes of leg swings and activation exercises, or simply ride the last few hundred metres to the start line with purpose rather than coasting.

## Common Race-Morning Mistakes

Over the years, through the podcast and through coaching riders in the Roadman community, I have seen the same mistakes repeated so often that they have become almost predictable. Here are the five that cost the most time, energy, and finishing positions.

### 1. Eating Too Close to the Start

Breakfast at T-minus 60 minutes instead of T-minus 180 minutes. The food has not digested, the stomach is still full, and the first hard effort sends blood away from the gut and toward the working muscles. The result: nausea, bloating, and sometimes a roadside stop that could have been entirely avoided. Eat early. Give your body time.

### 2. Trying New Nutrition

The feed station offers a gel brand you have never tried. A friend gives you a bar they swear by. The energy drink at the start is a different formulation than the one you trained with. Every untested product is a gamble with your GI system, and the odds are not in your favour during sustained exercise. Nothing new on race day. This rule has no exceptions.

### 3. Starting Too Hard

The adrenaline is flowing, the group is fast, the first kilometres are flat, and you feel fantastic. So you ride the opening 20 minutes 15% above your target power, burning through glycogen reserves at a rate your fuelling plan cannot replace. By the halfway mark, you are cooked. By the final quarter, you are survival-mode pedalling at a pace you could have sustained from the start.

Use a [pacing strategy](/blog/pacing-strategy-cycling-sportive). The first 20 minutes should feel easy — almost too easy. That is the point. Discipline in the opening kilometres buys you the legs to finish strong.

### 4. No Warm-Up

Rolling to the start line cold and expecting to perform from the gun. The first climb hurts more than it should. The legs feel wooden. The heart rate spikes disproportionately to the effort. A ten-minute warm-up prevents all of this. It is the smallest possible investment for a meaningful return.

### 5. Forgetting Essentials

No spare tube. No pump. No race number (and a 15-minute queue at registration to get a replacement). No emergency contact information. No gels because they are still sitting on the kitchen counter next to the note that says "don't forget the gels."

Every one of these is prevented by a checklist completed the night before. Which brings us to the final point.

## The Checklist Habit

In aviation, even the most experienced pilots use checklists. Not because they do not know their aircraft — they know it better than anyone — but because the human brain under pressure is unreliable. It forgets things. Specifically, it forgets the simple, obvious things while focusing on the complex, interesting ones. A pilot can execute a flawless instrument approach and forget to lower the landing gear. A cyclist can nail their taper, their nutrition plan, and their pacing strategy, and then leave both inner tubes on the workbench.

The value of a written checklist is not that it contains information you do not know. You know you need a spare tube. You know you need to charge your head unit. You know you should eat three hours before the start. The value is that it catches the thing you forgot while you were busy remembering everything else.

Professional teams use them. Military units use them. Surgeons use them. The evidence from every high-stakes domain is the same: checklists reduce errors, and the errors they catch are disproportionately the ones with serious consequences.

Neck pain is one of cycling's most common complaints, and almost certainly its most ignored. Riders who would never tolerate knee pain for a week will put up with a locked, aching neck for an entire season. They chalk it up to being in the drops too long, pop a couple of ibuprofen, and ride through it.

That is a mistake. Not because cycling neck pain is dangerous — most of the time it is not — but because it is fixable. The causes are well understood, the interventions are specific, and most of them cost nothing beyond a few minutes of daily attention.

Here is the thing nobody tells you: neck pain on the bike is rarely just a bike problem. It is a postural problem, a muscular weakness problem, and a desk-job problem that cycling exposes and amplifies. Fix the root causes and the neck pain goes away. Ignore them and it gets worse, ride by ride, year by year.

## Why Cyclists Get Neck Pain: The Biomechanics

The cervical spine — the seven vertebrae that make up your neck — was designed for intermittent movement, not sustained static loading. It is supposed to rotate, flex, and extend throughout the day. Cycling asks it to do something it was never built for: hold one position, under load, for hours.

When you ride in the drops or on the hoods with any meaningful torso angle, your upper body tilts forward and your neck must extend to look at the road ahead. This is called cervical extension, and it is the single biggest mechanical driver of cycling neck pain.

The angle matters. A rider on the hoods with a moderate torso angle of 40-45 degrees from horizontal might require 20-25 degrees of cervical extension to see the road. A rider in the drops with a more aggressive 25-30 degree torso angle might need 35-40 degrees. That difference sounds small. Over three hours at 85 rpm, it is the difference between manageable muscular fatigue and the kind of locked-up, burning neck pain that ruins the last quarter of every long ride.

### The Muscles Under Siege

The muscles doing the work are the cervical extensors — a group that includes the upper trapezius, levator scapulae, splenius capitis, and the deeper semispinalis group. These are not large, powerful muscles. They are fine motor controllers designed for head positioning, not sustained load-bearing.

When you ride, these muscles contract isometrically — they hold position without moving through a range. Isometric contractions are particularly fatiguing because they compress the blood vessels within the muscle, reducing oxygen delivery precisely when demand is highest. The result is a predictable fatigue curve: comfort for the first 45-60 minutes, mild tension at 60-90 minutes, and genuine pain beyond 90 minutes if the underlying factors are not addressed.

Phil Burt, who spent years as head physiotherapist at British Cycling and has fitted hundreds of elite and amateur riders, calls this the "accumulation effect." Each pedal stroke does not hurt. But the cervical extensors do not get a rest between pedal strokes either. The load is constant, the recovery window is zero, and the fatigue is cumulative.

### The Antagonist Weakness Problem

Here is where it gets interesting. The muscles on the front of the neck — the deep neck flexors, primarily the longus colli and longus capitis — are supposed to counterbalance the extensors. They stabilise the cervical spine from the front and prevent the head from drifting into excessive extension.

In most cyclists, these muscles are weak. Cycling does not train them. Desk work actively inhibits them. The result is an imbalance: the extensors do all the work because the flexors cannot do their share. Strengthening the deep neck flexors is the single most effective exercise intervention for cycling neck pain, and it is one that almost nobody does. More on this below.

## The Five Causes You Need to Understand

Neck pain in cyclists is almost never a single-cause problem. It is usually a combination of factors — some on the bike, some off it — that stack up until the cervical spine can no longer cope. Let me break this down.

### 1. Reach That Is Too Long

Reach — the horizontal distance from the saddle to the handlebars — is the most underrated variable in cycling neck pain. When reach is too long, the rider stretches forward to meet the bars. The shoulders round, the thoracic spine flexes, and the neck must extend further to compensate.

A stem that is 10-20mm too long does not feel dramatic. You can reach the hoods. You can hold the drops. But that extra stretch adds 5-10 degrees of cervical extension, and over a four-hour ride, those degrees compound into significant muscular fatigue.

The tell: if you find yourself sliding forward on the saddle during long rides, or if your elbows are locked rather than slightly bent when on the hoods, your reach is probably too long. Phil Burt's fitting philosophy emphasises that the elbows should carry a soft bend — approximately 15-20 degrees — on the hoods, with the shoulders relaxed and dropped. If you have to shrug to reach the bars, the neck is paying for it.

### 2. Bars That Are Too Low

Drop — the vertical distance between the saddle and the handlebars — directly controls how far your torso tilts and therefore how much your neck must extend. Lower bars mean a more aerodynamic position, but they also mean more sustained cervical extension.

For masters riders, the calculus is straightforward. The aerodynamic benefit of dropping the bars 20mm is approximately 2-4 watts at 30 km/h. The neck pain cost is measurable, progressive, and eventually performance-limiting in its own right. A rider who cannot hold a comfortable head position for the last hour of a race is not racing — they are surviving.

Raising the bars by 10-20mm — one or two headset spacers — is the simplest and cheapest intervention for cycling neck pain. At amateur speeds, the aerodynamic penalty is negligible. The comfort benefit is immediate.

### 3. Helmet Weight and Position

This one surprises riders, but the physics are clear. An adult head weighs approximately 4.5-5.5 kg. A road helmet adds 200-350g, depending on the model. That weight sits at the end of the cervical lever arm, and any additional grams are multiplied by the distance from the base of the neck.

A helmet that weighs 300g versus one that weighs 200g adds 100g to the end of that lever. Over three hours of sustained cervical extension, the cumulative extra load on the cervical extensors is meaningful — research suggests heavier helmets can increase cervical extensor load by up to 20 per cent compared to the lightest alternatives.

Position matters too. A helmet that sits too far forward on the head shifts the centre of gravity anteriorly, increasing the moment arm and making the extensors work harder. Many riders wear their helmets tilted slightly forward — it feels more secure — without realising the knock-on effect on the neck.

The fix: choose the lightest helmet that fits well and meets safety standards. Adjust it so it sits level on the head, not tilted forward. If you are shopping and the choice is between a 280g helmet with marginal aero gains and a 200g helmet without them, the lighter helmet is the better choice for any ride over 90 minutes.

### 4. Muscular Weakness and Imbalance

Cycling creates a specific pattern of muscular imbalance in the neck and upper back. The cervical extensors and upper trapezius become chronically overworked. The deep neck flexors become weak from disuse. The lower trapezius and serratus anterior — the muscles that stabilise the scapulae and provide a platform for the neck — are often undertrained.

This imbalance means the extensors are doing three jobs: holding the head up, stabilising the cervical spine, and compensating for weak scapular stabilisers. They were designed for the first job. The other two are overflow work, and it is the overflow that causes pain.

A targeted strengthening programme — which takes less than ten minutes, four days per week — addresses this imbalance directly. I will outline the specific exercises in the programme section below.

### 5. The Desk-to-Bike Pipeline

This is the one that ties everything together, and the one riders are least willing to confront. If you work at a desk, you spend the majority of your waking hours in forward head posture. The head drifts forward of the shoulders, the suboccipital muscles at the base of the skull shorten, the deep neck flexors weaken, and the cervical extensors carry a low-grade isometric load all day, every day.

Then you go ride. The extensors that have already been working for eight hours are now asked to hold a more demanding position for two to four more. They are starting the ride pre-fatigued. This is why desk-bound riders often report that their neck pain onset has crept earlier over the years — 90 minutes becomes 60, then 45. It is not that cycling is getting harder on the neck. It is that the desk is pre-loading the system until the remaining capacity is too small.

Phil Burt's bike fitting data from British Cycling consistently showed this pattern. The riders most affected by neck and upper back restrictions were not full-time professionals. They were age-group riders — our demographic — combining 40-60 hours of desk work with 8-12 hours of riding. The desk was the bigger problem, and it had to be addressed before any bike fit adjustment would hold.

## Self-Assessment: Where Is Your Neck Pain Coming From?

Before you change anything, spend five minutes working out where the problem originates. This saves you chasing the wrong fix.

### The Chin Tuck Test

Stand with your back against a wall, heels touching the skirting board, shoulder blades flat. Without tilting your head up or down, try to touch the back of your head to the wall by drawing your chin straight back — like making a double chin.

If you cannot touch the wall, or if it feels like a genuine effort, your deep neck flexors are weak and your cervical extensors are shortened. This is the most common finding in desk-bound cyclists and it points directly at the strengthening programme below.

### The Ride Onset Test

Note when neck pain starts on your next three rides. If it consistently appears before 60 minutes, the primary driver is likely muscular weakness or severe positional restriction — the system has very little capacity. If it appears between 60-120 minutes, it is more likely a bike fit issue amplifying a moderate weakness. Beyond 120 minutes, you are dealing with normal muscular fatigue that can be managed with fit tweaks, helmet choice, and in-ride position changes.

### The Desk Posture Check

Set a phone timer for random intervals during a work day. When it goes off, notice your head position. Is it forward of your shoulders? Is it tilted to one side? Are your shoulders raised toward your ears? Three checks in a row showing forward head posture confirms the desk-to-bike pipeline is active.

## The Fix: Strengthening Programme

This is the foundation. Without addressing muscular weakness and imbalance, every other fix is temporary. The good news: the programme is short, requires no equipment, and produces noticeable results within three to four weeks.

Do this four times per week. It takes eight to ten minutes.

### Deep Neck Flexor Holds (Chin Tucks)

This is the single most important exercise for cycling neck pain. It trains the longus colli and longus capitis — the muscles that stabilise the cervical spine from the front and take load off the extensors.

Lie on your back with your knees bent. Gently draw your chin toward your throat — the movement is a nod, not a crunch. You should feel the muscles at the front of your neck engage, just above the collarbone. Hold for 10 seconds. Release.

- **Sets and reps:** 3 sets of 10 holds
- **Progression:** Once you can complete 3 x 10 with a 10-second hold, increase hold time to 15 seconds, then 20
- **Common mistake:** Using the sternocleidomastoid (the large muscle on the side of the neck) instead of the deep flexors. If you feel the muscle on the side of your neck bulging, you are pushing too hard. Reduce the intensity — this is a gentle contraction, not a maximal effort

### Upper Trapezius Stretch

The upper traps bear the brunt of cervical extension on the bike and are chronically tight in most cyclists.

Sit upright in a chair. Drop your right ear toward your right shoulder. Place your right hand gently on top of your head — not pulling, just adding light weight. Hold for 30 seconds. You should feel a stretch along the left side of the neck from the base of the skull to the top of the shoulder. Repeat on the other side.

- **Sets and reps:** 2 x 30 seconds per side
- **When:** After every ride, and once on non-ride days

### Cervical Rotation Stretch

Cycling holds the neck in extension with almost no rotation. This stiffens the cervical rotators and contributes to the locked-up feeling after long rides.

Sit upright. Turn your head to the right as far as is comfortable. Place your right hand on your chin and apply gentle overpressure — just enough to feel a stretch, not to force movement. Hold for 20 seconds. Repeat on the left side.

- **Sets and reps:** 2 x 20 seconds per side
- **When:** After rides and during desk breaks

### Scapular Retraction (Wall Slides)

The scapulae need to provide a stable platform for the neck muscles to work from. Weak scapular stabilisers force the upper trapezius and levator scapulae to work harder, which feeds directly into neck pain.

Stand with your back against a wall, arms at your sides, elbows bent to 90 degrees. Press the backs of your hands against the wall. Slowly slide your arms up the wall, maintaining contact with your hands, elbows, and shoulders. Slide up until you feel the shoulder blades start to tip forward, then return to the start.

- **Sets and reps:** 3 sets of 10 repetitions
- **Key cue:** Squeeze the shoulder blades together and down as you slide — the movement comes from the scapulae, not the arms

### Prone Y Raises

This targets the lower trapezius, which is chronically weak in cyclists and essential for scapular stability.

Lie face down on the floor with your arms extended overhead in a Y position, thumbs pointing toward the ceiling. Lift both arms 10-15 cm off the floor, squeezing the shoulder blades together. Hold for 3 seconds at the top. Lower slowly.

- **Sets and reps:** 3 sets of 12 repetitions
- **Progression:** Add a 0.5 kg weight in each hand once 3 x 12 bodyweight becomes easy

## The Fix: Bike Fit Adjustments

Fit changes are the second line of intervention — they reduce the demand on the cervical extensors so that your strengthening work has time to build capacity. Make one change at a time and ride for at least a week before assessing.

### Raise the Handlebars

Add a 5mm headset spacer under the stem. Ride for a week. If neck pain onset is delayed, add another 5mm. Most riders with persistent neck pain find that 10-20mm of additional bar height makes a measurable difference without any perceptible change in aerodynamics at typical amateur speeds (25-35 km/h).

If you have no spacers available — the steerer tube has been cut flush — a stem with a higher rise angle (10-17 degrees rather than 6 degrees) achieves a similar effect.

### Shorten the Reach

If the chin tuck test or your riding posture suggests excessive reach, try a stem that is 10mm shorter than your current setup. This is a $30-50 experiment that reduces how far the neck must extend to see the road.

Be conservative here. Going from a 110mm stem to a 90mm stem in one step changes handling characteristics and may introduce other problems. Trial 10mm at a time.

### Check Saddle-to-Bar Drop

The combination of saddle height and bar height determines the torso angle, which in turn determines cervical extension demand. A drop of more than 6-8 cm (saddle to bars) is aggressive for most masters riders and should only be maintained if the cervical strength is there to support it.

Phil Burt advocates fitting the rider to the bike, not the bike to the rider's ambitions. If you aspire to a professional-level position but your neck cannot sustain it for more than an hour, the position is wrong for you — regardless of what it looks like.

### Tilt the Bars

On drop handlebars, rotating the bars upward by 5-10 degrees changes the wrist and hand position on the hoods and can reduce the tendency to drop the chest, which in turn reduces cervical extension. This is a subtle adjustment, but riders with persistent neck pain often find it provides noticeable relief.

## The Fix: On-Bike Habits

Some neck pain management happens during the ride itself. These habits reduce cumulative load and extend the window before fatigue sets in.

**Move your head.** Every 10-15 minutes, deliberately rotate your neck through its full range — look left, look right, drop your chin to your chest, tilt your head side to side. Five seconds of movement breaks the isometric hold and allows blood flow back into the compressed vessels within the cervical extensors.

**Change hand position frequently.** Moving between tops, hoods, and drops shifts your torso angle and therefore your cervical extension angle. Riders who lock onto the hoods for the entire ride accumulate fatigue faster than those who vary their position every 15-20 minutes.

**Drop your shoulders.** Under fatigue, the upper trapezius activates to help stabilise the head, and the shoulders creep up toward the ears. This shortens the muscle, increases compression, and accelerates pain. Consciously drop the shoulders every time you change hand position. Make it a habit.

**Stand up periodically.** Standing completely unloads the cervical extensors because the torso moves to a near-vertical position and the neck returns to neutral. On long rides, stand for 10-20 seconds every 15-20 minutes. Use it as a reset.

**Wear the right eyewear.** Sunglasses that sit too low on the nose force you to tilt the head further back to see over the frames. Glasses with a higher frame profile or a wrap-around design that does not obstruct your line of sight reduce this tendency.

## The Fix: Off-the-Bike Posture

If the desk-to-bike pipeline is feeding your neck pain, you need to address the source, not just the symptom.

**Screen height.** The top of your monitor should be at eye level. If you work on a laptop, a $20 laptop stand and an external keyboard solve the most common postural driver of forward head position. This single change reduces the daily load on your cervical extensors more than any bike fit adjustment.

**Chair position.** Sit with your hips slightly above your knees. Avoid deep, soft chairs that encourage slumping. A chair with adjustable lumbar support helps maintain the natural spinal curves, which keeps the head in a better position relative to the shoulders.

**Micro-breaks.** Every 30 minutes, stand up, look at something more than six metres away, and do five chin tucks. This takes 30 seconds and prevents the cervical extensors from accumulating the low-grade fatigue that carries over into your riding.

**Phone posture.** Holding a phone at chest height rather than waist height dramatically reduces the forward head angle. If you spend significant time on your phone, this is a free fix that adds up over weeks and months.

## When to See a Physio

Most cycling neck pain is muscular and positional. It responds to the measures in this guide within three to six weeks. But some presentations need professional assessment, and it is worth knowing where that line sits.

**See a physiotherapist or sports medicine clinician if:**

- Neck pain is accompanied by numbness, tingling, or weakness in one or both arms. These are nerve compression symptoms — either from a cervical disc bulge or foraminal narrowing — and they require proper clinical assessment before you continue riding.
- Pain radiates from the neck into the shoulder blade, shoulder, or down the arm. Radiating pain follows a nerve distribution and suggests the nerve root is irritated or compressed.
- You experience headaches that start at the base of the skull and travel over the top of the head. Cervicogenic headaches originate from the upper cervical spine and can be effectively treated with manual therapy, but they need accurate diagnosis first.
- Symptoms have not improved after four weeks of consistent strengthening, fit changes, and posture adjustments. If the basics are not working, there may be an underlying structural issue that self-management will not resolve.
- You have had a crash, even a minor one, and neck pain started or worsened afterward. Post-traumatic cervical pain needs examination to rule out ligament damage or fracture.

The good news: even in these cases, the vast majority of cycling neck pain resolves with the right combination of physiotherapy, strengthening, and positional adjustment. Surgery is extremely rare for cycling-related cervical issues.

## Putting It Together: The Four-Week Protocol

If you are a masters rider dealing with persistent neck pain, here is the practical sequence.

**Week 1:** Start the strengthening programme — chin tucks, upper trap stretches, cervical rotation stretches, scapular retractions, and prone Y raises — four days per week. Adjust your screen height at work. Note your current neck pain onset time on rides.

**Week 2:** Raise your handlebars by 5-10mm. Continue the strengthening programme. Start the micro-break habit at your desk — five chin tucks every 30 minutes. Implement in-ride head movement every 10-15 minutes.

**Week 3:** Assess. If neck pain onset has improved by 15-20 minutes, you are on the right track. Continue everything. If no change, add a 10mm stem length reduction. Check helmet fit and weight.

**Week 4:** Reassess. Most riders report meaningful improvement by this point. If symptoms persist, book a professional bike fit that includes an off-bike postural assessment. If symptoms include radiating pain or numbness, see a physiotherapist.

This is not glamorous work. There is no single fix that resolves cycling neck pain overnight. But the protocol works because it addresses all five causes — reach, drop, helmet, muscular imbalance, and desk posture — simultaneously. The riders who commit to it almost always fix the problem. The riders who do not tend to manage it indefinitely, losing the last hour of every long ride to a problem that was always within their control.

## Key Takeaways

- Neck pain in cyclists is caused by sustained cervical extension — looking up from a forward-leaning position — loading small muscles that were not designed for hours of isometric work
- The deep neck flexors are the key. Strengthening them with chin tuck holds (3 x 10, four days per week) is the single most effective intervention
- Raise your handlebars by 10-20mm before anything else — the aerodynamic cost at amateur speeds is negligible, the comfort gain is immediate
- Shorten reach by 10mm if you are stretched out — locked elbows on the hoods mean you are too far forward
- The lightest well-fitting helmet you can find reduces cumulative cervical load more than riders expect — position it level, not tilted forward
- Fix the desk: screen at eye level, micro-breaks every 30 minutes with chin tucks, and conscious attention to head position throughout the day
- Move on the bike — rotate the head every 10-15 minutes, change hand position, drop the shoulders, and stand periodically
- Numbness, tingling, or radiating arm pain are red flags that need clinical assessment before you continue riding

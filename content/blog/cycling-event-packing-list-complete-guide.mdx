Every sportive morning looks the same. You're standing at registration, number pinned, bike racked, coffee in hand. The rider next to you is tearing through a bag, pulling out tubes, cables, wrappers — searching for a CO2 cartridge he swore was in there last night. His mate is asking if anyone has a spare 5mm Allen key. Somebody else is wondering whether arm warmers will be enough for the descent off the first col.

This is a solved problem. Not by carrying more, but by thinking about it once — properly — the night before.

I've done enough sportives, gran fondos, and multi-day events to know that the riders who finish well are not always the strongest. They are the ones who never had to stop for something preventable. They never bonked because they planned their nutrition. They never froze on a descent because they packed a gilet. They never DNF'd at 140km because they had two tubes instead of one.

Packing for a cycling event is not complicated. But it does require a system. This is the one I use.

## The Philosophy: Weight Versus Contingency

There is a tension in event packing that every cyclist feels. You want to carry as little as possible because weight matters and jersey pockets are finite. But you also want to be self-sufficient, because the support car might be 40km behind you when your chain snaps.

The answer is not to carry everything. It is to carry the right things.

A spare tube weighs 90g. A CO2 cartridge weighs 38g. A rain gilet weighs 70-90g. A quick link weighs 3g. None of these will slow you down. All of them can save your event.

On the other side: carrying four energy bars "just in case" when you know there are feed stations every 30km is dead weight. Bringing a full-size pump when two CO2 cartridges will do is unnecessary mass in your back pocket. A spare set of brake pads for a one-day sportive is paranoia, not preparation.

The principle is simple. Carry what fixes the problems that would end your ride. Leave behind anything that only makes a comfortable ride marginally more comfortable.

## On-Bike Essentials: The Non-Negotiable Kit

These items go on every ride, every event, regardless of distance. If you roll out without them, you are gambling.

### Inner Tubes (Two Minimum)

One puncture is bad luck. A second puncture with no spare tube is a preventable DNF. For any event over 80km, two tubes is the floor. If you are riding tubeless, still carry a tube — sealant does not fix every cut, and a sidewall tear will leave you stranded without one.

Make sure your tubes match your tyre width and valve length. This sounds obvious until you are standing on the roadside with a 48mm valve and 60mm-deep wheels.

### Tyre Levers

Two quality tyre levers. Not the cheap ones that flex and snap. Pedro's or Lezyne make levers that actually work. Practise using them at home before race day — tight tyre-rim combinations exist, and finding out yours is one of them while 200 riders pass you is not ideal.

### CO2 Inflator and Cartridges

Two 16g CO2 cartridges and a threaded inflator head. This gets a tyre from flat to rideable in under three minutes. The alternative — a mini pump — takes 200 strokes and five minutes of sweating to reach 80 psi. CO2 wins on speed every time.

The trade-off: each cartridge is single-use, and they are sensitive to cold. In winter events or mountain sportives with long descents, the cartridge can freeze in your pocket and deliver less gas than expected. For those conditions, bring a compact mini pump as backup.

### Multi-Tool

A quality multi-tool with 4mm, 5mm, and 6mm Allen keys covers 90% of bolt adjustments on a modern road bike. A T25 Torx is increasingly common on disc brake rotors and newer components. Chain tool capability is a bonus.

Topeak, Crankbrothers, and Lezyne all make multi-tools under 100g that do the job. Test yours at home — some cheap multi-tools have such poor leverage that they cannot actually turn a tight bolt.

### Chain Quick Link

A single quick link weighs 3g. It can be taped to the inside of your seatpost or slotted into a saddle bag. If your chain breaks mid-ride — which happens more often than people think — a quick link and a chain tool get you rolling again in two minutes. Without one, you are walking.

Make sure the quick link matches your drivetrain speed — a 12-speed quick link will not work on an 11-speed chain.

### Identification and Emergency Contact

Carry some form of ID with your name, blood type if you know it, an emergency contact number, and any medical conditions or allergies. A laminated card in a jersey pocket works. Several companies sell silicone wristbands or shoe tags with this information. Road ID is one.

This is one of those items that feels unnecessary until the moment it becomes the most important thing you are carrying.

### Phone

Your phone is your emergency beacon, your navigation backup, your camera, and your connection to whoever is waiting for you at the finish. Keep it in a waterproof pouch or a zip-lock bag in your centre jersey pocket. Turn on low-power mode before the start if battery life is a concern.

### Emergency Cash

A folded banknote in a small zip-lock bag, tucked behind your phone. If you mechanical out of range of the support car, cash buys you a coffee, a taxi, or a train ticket home. In mainland Europe, a EUR20 note covers most emergencies. In the UK, GBP20 does the same. This is insurance you will probably never use. Carry it anyway.

## Nutrition Planning by Distance

Nutrition is where most sportive riders get it wrong. Not because the science is complicated — it is not — but because they fail to plan, portion, and pack their food the night before. Race morning is chaos. Do the thinking in advance.

The target for most endurance cycling is 60-90g of carbohydrate per hour. For hard efforts or events over four hours, some riders push toward 100-120g per hour. Your gut needs training to handle the higher end of that range, so practise in training first.

### 100km Sportives (3-4 Hours)

A 100km event at moderate pace takes most amateur riders 3-4 hours. At 60-90g of carbohydrate per hour, that is 180-360g total.

**What to carry:**

- **6-10 gels** (depending on carb content per gel — a 40g-carb gel means fewer sachets)
- **1-2 energy bars or chews** for variety and to give your palate a break from gel sweetness
- **2 bottles** — one with electrolyte mix, one with plain water
- **1 spare gel** beyond your plan as insurance

Pre-open or pre-cut gel corners the night before if your brand is difficult to tear one-handed. Tape gels to your top tube if your jersey pockets are already full. Some riders use a bento box on the top tube for bars and chews — works well, keeps food accessible without reaching behind your back.

Feed stations at organised sportives are a bonus, not your plan. Use them for water refills and whatever food looks appealing, but do not rely on them for your primary fuel. They may be out of gels by the time you arrive. The food they offer may not sit well with your stomach. Carry enough to finish without them.

### 150-200km Sportives (5-8 Hours)

Longer events change the equation. You are on the bike for 5-8 hours. Flavour fatigue becomes real — eating nothing but sweet gels for six hours is miserable and eventually your gut will rebel.

**What to carry:**

- **10-16 gels** (or equivalent carbs from bars, chews, and drink mix)
- **2-3 savoury options** — rice cakes, small sandwiches wrapped in foil, salted boiled potatoes. Asker Jeukendrup's research showed that savoury options reduce flavour fatigue and improve total intake on long efforts
- **Electrolyte tabs or powder** for 3-4 bottle refills (you will refill at feed stations)
- **Caffeine gels** saved for the final third
- **Emergency bar** — one calorie-dense bar that stays in your pocket as the last resort

At this distance, your nutrition plan should be time-based, not feel-based. Set a repeating 20-minute alarm on your bike computer. Every alarm, eat something. By the time you feel hungry on a 200km ride, you are already 30-45 minutes behind on fuel and the deficit is very difficult to recover.

### Multi-Day Events (Stage Races, Audax, Bikepacking)

Multi-day events add a dimension that single-day sportives do not have: recovery nutrition between stages.

**On-bike nutrition** follows the same principles as a 200km event, scaled to the stage distance. The critical addition is what happens when you stop.

**Post-stage recovery (the 30-minute window):**

- Recovery shake with 30-40g protein and 60-80g carbohydrate, pre-mixed or in a shaker with powder ready to add water
- 500ml electrolyte drink
- A real meal within 90 minutes of finishing — not just a protein bar

Pack your Stage 2 nutrition before Stage 1 starts. When you finish a stage exhausted, the last thing you want is to be sorting through a bag of mixed gels trying to figure out what goes where tomorrow. Pre-portion, label, and have it ready.

## Clothing Layers: The Descent Problem

Clothing is the area where sportive riders most consistently underpack. The logic is understandable: you start warm, you are working hard, you do not want to carry extra weight. But cycling has a problem that no other endurance sport shares to the same degree — descents.

You climb a col at 10-15kph, working at threshold, generating enormous heat. Then you crest the summit and descend at 60-80kph for 15-20 minutes, generating almost no heat. The temperature differential between the climb and the descent can be 15-20°C when you factor in wind chill. If you are soaked in sweat from the climb and wearing only a jersey, hypothermia is a genuine risk on an exposed mountain descent.

### Base Layer

A lightweight mesh base layer (Craft, Castelli, or similar) worn under your jersey manages moisture and provides a thin insulation layer. It weighs 50-70g and packs to almost nothing. On cold descents, it is the layer that stops the wind from hitting wet skin.

### Arm Warmers

Arm warmers are the most versatile piece of cycling clothing ever made. They weigh 40-50g for the pair, roll down to your wrists when you are warm, and pull up in seconds when the temperature drops. For any event with a start temperature below 18°C or a significant descent, arm warmers are not optional.

### Gilet (Wind Vest)

A packable gilet — Castelli Superleggera, Rapha Pro Team, or similar — weighs 60-90g and compresses into a jersey pocket. It blocks wind across your chest and core, which is where you lose the most heat on a descent.

This is the single most important item for mountain sportives. A gilet is the difference between a comfortable descent and ten minutes of uncontrollable shivering that ruins your legs for the next climb.

### Rain Jacket

A lightweight rain jacket (Gore Shakedry, Castelli Idro, or equivalent) weighs 90-130g and provides genuine waterproof protection. In summer sportives with a stable forecast, you can often leave this behind. In spring, autumn, or mountain events — bring it. Rain at altitude is cold rain, and cold rain at 70kph on a descent is dangerous.

The question most riders ask: "Do I need both a gilet and a rain jacket?" For events under 100km in settled weather, one or the other. For mountain events or anything over 150km where conditions can change, both. They weigh 150-200g combined. That is less than a bidon of water.

### Temperature Planning

Check the forecast the night before. Check it again at 6am. Look specifically at:

- **Start temperature** — what you need for the first 30 minutes before you warm up
- **Summit temperature** — often 8-10°C colder than the valley
- **Wind speed** — anything over 30kph at summit level means a gilet is mandatory
- **Rain probability** — above 30% and the rain jacket comes

If in doubt, pack it. Nobody ever abandoned a sportive because their jersey pockets were 90g heavier. Plenty have abandoned because they were hypothermic on a descent.

## Tech and Electronics

### Bike Computer

Your head unit is your pacing tool, your navigation, and your nutrition alarm system. Pre-load the route the night before. Set up a data screen with the fields you actually need: power (or HR), speed, distance remaining, time of day, and elapsed time. You do not need 12 data fields — you need five that you can read at a glance while descending at 65kph.

If your event has a GPX file available (most do), load it onto your computer and test that it routes correctly before race morning.

### Charging Strategy for Long Events

For events under 6 hours, a full charge the night before is sufficient for most modern head units. Garmin Edge units typically last 15-20 hours; Wahoo ELEMNT units last 15-17 hours. Turn off backlight auto-brightness and reduce GPS recording to "smart" rather than "every second" to extend battery.

For multi-day events or ultra-distance rides, carry a small power bank (5,000mAh weighs around 100g) and a short charging cable. You can top up your head unit during a feed stop or in a transition bag between stages.

### Phone Battery

Low-power mode from the start. Aeroplane mode if you do not need live tracking or messaging. A phone that dies at 160km of a 200km event when you need to call for a pickup is a problem. Some riders carry a slim 3,000mAh power bank specifically for their phone — 60g of insurance.

## The 100km Packing List

Everything you need for a standard 100km sportive. Nothing more.

**On the bike / in pockets:**

- 2 inner tubes (matched to tyre width and valve length)
- 2 tyre levers
- CO2 inflator + 2 cartridges
- Multi-tool (4, 5, 6mm Allen, T25 Torx)
- Chain quick link (correct speed)
- 6-10 gels (pre-portioned)
- 1-2 bars or chews
- 2 full bottles (1 electrolyte, 1 water)
- Phone in waterproof pouch
- Emergency cash
- ID / emergency contact card
- Arm warmers (if start temp below 18°C)
- Gilet (if climbing or descents involved)

**At registration / in car:**

- Spare bottles (pre-mixed)
- Post-ride recovery drink
- Warm clothes for after the finish
- Towel
- Sunscreen (applied before the start, tube in the car for reapplication)

## The 200km Packing List

Everything from the 100km list, plus:

**Additional on-bike items:**

- 1 extra inner tube (3 total)
- Rain jacket (conditions dependent, but bring it for anything over 150km)
- Additional gels/bars to cover 5-8 hours at 60-90g carbs per hour
- 2-3 savoury food items (rice cakes, sandwiches, salted potatoes)
- Extra electrolyte tabs for bottle refills
- Caffeine gels for the final third
- Emergency energy bar
- Chamois cream (small tube or single-use sachet for a mid-ride reapplication)
- Lip balm with SPF
- Small power bank + short cable (for phone or head unit)
- Front and rear lights (if the event may run past sunset)

**In drop bag (if the event offers one):**

- Fresh bottles with pre-mixed electrolyte
- Spare gels and bars
- Dry base layer (if rain is possible)
- Any medication you take regularly

## The Multi-Day Packing List

Everything from the 200km list for each stage, plus a transition bag.

**Transition bag (between stages):**

- Clean base layer
- Clean bib shorts (or at minimum, fresh chamois cream application)
- Recovery shake (protein + carbs, pre-measured powder or ready-to-drink)
- 500ml electrolyte recovery drink
- Compression socks
- Anti-chafing cream (Assos, Chamois Butt'r, or similar)
- Pain relief (ibuprofen, paracetamol — whatever you normally use)
- Massage ball or small foam roller (a lacrosse ball weighs 140g and works on glutes, quads, and calves)
- Phone charger and cable
- Head unit charger and cable
- Spare batteries for lights if using
- Clean socks
- Stage 2 nutrition (pre-portioned, labelled, ready to transfer to pockets)
- Zip-lock bags for wet or dirty kit from Stage 1
- Small first aid: plasters, antiseptic wipes, blister treatment

**Label everything.** When you are exhausted after a 180km stage, searching through an unlabelled bag for your charger cable is time and energy you do not have. Separate items into labelled zip-lock bags: "Recovery Nutrition," "Stage 2 Fuel," "Charging," "Fresh Kit."

## The Night-Before Routine

This is the part that separates prepared riders from panicked ones. It takes 15-20 minutes and it removes every ounce of race-morning stress.

### Step 1: Lay Everything Out

Clear a space on the floor — a bed works too, but floor is better because you can see everything at once. Lay out every item you plan to carry: tubes, tools, food, clothing layers, electronics, cables, ID. All of it, visible.

### Step 2: Photograph It

Take a photo with your phone. This is your insurance policy. If you forget something in the morning rush, check the photo. It takes five seconds and catches more forgotten items than any written checklist.

### Step 3: Pre-Portion Nutrition

Count your gels. Check the total carbohydrate against your plan. Pre-cut corners on any gels that are difficult to open. Wrap savoury items in foil, ready to pocket. Fill your bottles or prepare electrolyte sachets so you only need to add water in the morning.

Separate your on-bike food from your drop-bag food from your post-ride food. Do not leave this for the morning.

### Step 4: Charge Everything

Plug in your head unit, phone, lights, and power bank. All of them, every time. A flat head unit because you forgot to charge it is one of the most common — and most avoidable — problems on event morning.

### Step 5: Check the Weather (Final Time)

Look at the hourly forecast for the event location. Not your home, the event. Mountain events can have dramatically different weather at the summit versus the start village. Adjust your clothing plan. If rain has appeared in the forecast overnight, the rain jacket goes from "in the car" to "in the pocket."

### Step 6: Set Out Your Morning Clothes

Pin number on jersey. Lay out your kit in the order you put it on. Socks, shorts, base layer, jersey, shoes. This sounds trivial until you are stumbling around a dark bedroom at 5:30am trying to find your left shoe.

### Step 7: Set Your Alarm (and a Backup)

Allow time for breakfast, coffee, a proper warm-up, and a calm drive to the start. Rushing through the morning undoes all the preparation from the night before.

## The Riders Who Finish Well

The difference between finishing a sportive and finishing it well is rarely about fitness. The strongest rider in the field can be undone by a second puncture with no spare, a bonk from under-fuelling, or hypothermia on a cold descent. All preventable. All solvable by packing properly.

The riders who finish well are the ones who treated the preparation with the same seriousness as the training. They laid out their kit. They counted their gels. They checked the forecast. They thought about the rain before it arrived.

Packing is not glamorous. Nobody posts their floor layout to Instagram. But it is the foundation that everything else sits on. Get it right once, build a system you trust, and race mornings become calm instead of chaotic.

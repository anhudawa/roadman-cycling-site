Here is the thing nobody tells you about London to Brighton. The ride itself is not hard. Fifty-four miles through Surrey and Sussex on mostly decent roads, with nothing steeper than a motorway flyover until the final six miles. A reasonably fit person who rides twice a week can finish it. Thousands of people who barely ride at all finish it every year on bikes that have been collecting dust in the shed since the previous summer.

And yet. There is Ditchling Beacon. 1.3 kilometres at 9-10 per cent average, arriving at mile 48 when your legs have been turning for four hours. On paper, a short climb. In practice, a reckoning. Not because the gradient is savage — it is not — but because everything you did or failed to do in the preceding 48 miles reveals itself on that slope. The riders who walk Ditchling Beacon are almost never the least fit people on the ride. They are the ones who went too hard through Surrey, ate nothing for three hours, and arrived at the foot of the climb running on fumes and stubbornness.

This guide is about making sure you are not one of them. Eight weeks of simple, structured preparation. Three rides per week. One hill. One nutrition plan. And the quiet confidence at mile 48 that you have been ready for this moment since week four.

- London to Brighton covers 54 miles with approximately 700-900m of climbing — a genuine challenge for a first-time rider but entirely achievable with 8 weeks of structured preparation
- Ditchling Beacon at mile 48 is the defining moment — 1.3km at 9-10 per cent average after 48 miles of riding, and the riders who walk it are almost always the ones who went too hard in the first 30 miles, not the ones who lack fitness

## Course Profile and Key Sections

The terrain rolls gently south through two counties and then, right at the end, throws one proper hill at you. Understanding the shape of the day helps you manage it.

### The Start: Clapham Common and the Congestion (Miles 0-10)

Ten thousand cyclists do not leave Clapham Common in an orderly fashion. The first few miles are stop-start, bunched, slow. Riders on carbon road bikes sit behind riders on Halfords hybrids. Nobody is going anywhere fast.

What most people do: get frustrated, weave through gaps, burn nervous energy trying to find clear road. What actually works: accept it. The congestion clears within 10-15 miles. Eat your first snack. Drink. Find a comfortable wheel to follow. This is where you set the tempo for the next 44 miles.

### The Surrey Rollers (Miles 10-30)

Once the field thins, the route opens up through Surrey's lanes. Rolling terrain — short rises of 3-5 per cent, shallow descents, the occasional false flat that costs more effort than it looks. Nothing that individually registers as a climb, but the cumulative elevation is steady. This stretch accounts for roughly half the ride's total climbing.

The mood is festival. Charity jerseys, fancy dress, cowbells at the roadside. This section should feel comfortable. If it feels hard, you are going too fast.

### The Sussex Approach (Miles 30-45)

Crossing into Sussex, the terrain eases and the ride finds its rhythm. Groups settle. Feed stations are well-placed. By mile 35, the arithmetic starts: fifteen miles to go.

Here is where it gets really interesting. Fifteen miles is nothing on fresh legs. But you have been riding for three hours, and your body is burning through its fuel reserves whether you notice or not. The riders who eat at mile 35 arrive at Ditchling with something in the tank. The riders who think fifteen miles is too short to bother eating arrive with nothing.

Eat at mile 35. Eat again at mile 40. This is not negotiable.

### Ditchling Beacon and the Descent to Brighton (Miles 48-54)

Let me break this down. Ditchling Beacon is 1.3 kilometres long. Average gradient 9-10 per cent, with ramps that touch 12-13 per cent near the top. On a fresh pair of legs, it is a four-minute effort. After 48 miles, it is five to eight minutes of sustained climbing at the end of a long day.

It is not Alpe d'Huez. But after 48 miles on a hybrid bike with flat pedals, it feels close. The road kicks up sharply after a left-hand bend and you see riders ahead — some grinding, some walking, some stopped at the roadside. Ignore all of them. Find your gear. Find your rhythm. Stay seated as long as possible. The whole thing is over in minutes.

And then the top. The view south across the Downs to the sea. The descent into Brighton — fast, open, the finish pulling you forward. Three minutes of downhill that feel like a reward for everything you have done. The seafront arrives. You are finished.

## Fitness Benchmarks

The distance is manageable and the climbing is modest, but a baseline of fitness makes the difference between enjoying the day and surviving it.

| Comfortable finish (hybrid) | 1.5-2.0 | 20-24 km/h | 4.5-5.5 hrs | Regular cyclist, heavier bike |
| First-timer completion | 1.2-1.8 | 16-22 km/h | 5-7 hrs | Minimal cycling background |

For a 75kg rider at 1.8 W/kg, London to Brighton burns roughly 2,000-2,500 calories. That number tells you how much you need to eat — and most first-timers eat less than half of it.

Use the [gear ratio calculator](/tools/gear-ratio) to check whether your lowest gear is low enough for Ditchling. A 34/28 cassette gives you a gear ratio of 1.21 — manageable for a fit rider. A 34/32 gives you 1.06 — much more comfortable for a first-timer. If your hybrid has a triple chainring, you probably have gears low enough. If it does not, check before event day.

## 8-Week Training Plan Overview

This plan assumes you can currently ride 15-20 miles comfortably. If your longest ride is under 10 miles, start four weeks earlier and spend the first month simply building saddle time with two easy rides per week.

### Phase 1: Getting Started (Weeks 1-3)

**Volume:** 3-4 hours per week across 3 rides.

The goal is simple: get on the bike regularly, build saddle time, and establish a routine. Eighty per cent of your riding at [Zone 2](/blog/zone-2-training-complete-guide) — conversational pace, the effort where you could talk to a riding partner without gasping.

**Weekend long ride:** Building from 20 miles (week 1) to 28 miles (week 3). Flat to gently rolling terrain. No need to find hills yet. Practise eating and drinking on the bike from ride one — a flapjack and a bottle of water every 45 minutes. This trains your gut as much as your legs.

**Midweek rides:** Two rides of 45-60 minutes each. One easy spin. One slightly more purposeful — ride at a pace that feels like you are working but could sustain for an hour. Not hard. Just not idle.

**Off the bike:** Two sessions per week of core stability, 15 minutes each. Planks (3x30 seconds), side planks (3x20 seconds each side), dead bugs (3x8 each side), and single-leg glute bridges (3x10 each side). A strong core holds your position on the bike for five hours. A weak one means lower-back pain from mile 30 onward.

### Phase 2: Building Distance (Weeks 4-6)

**Volume:** 4-5.5 hours per week.

The long ride is the centrepiece now. Everything else supports it.

**Weekend long ride:** Building from 30 miles (week 4) to 38-40 miles (week 6). Full fuelling practice on every long ride — eat what you plan to eat on event day.

**Hill practice:** From week 4, find a local hill of 8-12 per cent gradient — 500 metres to a kilometre long. Ride it three or four times per visit. Not intervals. Just ride up at a sustainable pace, recover, ride back down, go again. Find the gear that lets you spin at 55-65 rpm without stalling. That gear is your Ditchling gear.

**Midweek rides:** One easy 45-minute spin. One moderate ride of 60-75 minutes with 2x10 minutes at a tempo you could hold for an hour but would not want to hold for two.

**Core:** Maintain twice weekly. Same movements, progress to 3x45-second planks and 3x30-second side planks. Add bird-dogs (3x8 each side) for rotational stability.

### Phase 3: Ditchling Readiness and Taper (Weeks 7-8)

**Week 7: Peak Week**

**Volume:** 5-6 hours.

**The big ride:** 42-45 miles with at least one significant hill. Full dress rehearsal — the kit you will wear, the food you will carry, the pacing you plan to hold. If you finish tired but functional, you are ready. If you finish destroyed, your pacing or your fuelling needs adjustment — both are fixable.

**Hill repeat session:** One more visit to your local climb. Four repeats at Ditchling pace — seated, steady, controlled breathing.

**Midweek:** One easy spin, one moderate ride. Nothing heroic.

**Week 8: Taper Week**

**Volume drops 40-50%.** The fitness is banked. You cannot build meaningful fitness in seven days. You can arrive at Clapham Common fresh or fatigued — and freshness matters more than one extra training ride.

**Tuesday or Wednesday:** 40 minutes easy with 3x2-minute efforts at moderate tempo — openers, not builders.

**Thursday:** 20-30 minutes easy. Light spin, light gears, light effort.

**Friday and Saturday:** Rest. Eat well. Sleep well. Lay out your kit.

**Sunday:** Event day. Fresh legs, full bottles, pockets full of food.

## Key Sessions

### The Weekend Long Ride

The most important session in the plan. Build from 20 miles to 45 miles over seven weeks. Every long ride includes fuelling practice — 40-60g of carbohydrate per hour, 500ml of fluid per hour. If you can only do one session per week, make it this one.

### Hill Practice

Find a hill. Not a mountain — a hill. Something between 8 and 12 per cent gradient, somewhere between 500 metres and a kilometre long. Ride it at a pace you could sustain for eight minutes. Recover. Ride it again. Three to four repeats, once a week from week 4 onward. The goal is not fitness. The goal is familiarity. Ditchling Beacon is not hard if you know what a 10 per cent gradient feels like.

### Easy Midweek Spins

Forty-five to sixty minutes at conversation pace on flat terrain. These rides keep the legs turning without accumulating fatigue — and for a first-timer, the habit matters as much as the fitness.

## Nutrition Strategy

Let me be really clear about this. The wall at mile 40 is not a fitness problem. It is a nutrition problem. And it is entirely fixable.

Your body stores roughly 90-120 minutes of glycogen at moderate cycling intensity. London to Brighton takes four to seven hours. The arithmetic works against you if you do not eat. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to calculate your exact requirements.

**From the start:** Eat within the first 30 minutes. A flapjack, a banana, half a cereal bar — something. You will not feel hungry. Eat anyway.

**Every 45-60 minutes:** 40-60g of carbohydrate. A gel, a bar, a banana, a handful of jelly babies. Whatever your stomach tolerates. Test this in training, not on event day.

**Mile 35-40:** Eat your last solid food here. This fuel takes 20-30 minutes to become available. You want it arriving as you hit the foot of Ditchling Beacon.

**Ditchling Beacon:** Do not try to eat on a 10 per cent gradient. A gel at the bottom of the climb — taken with a swig of water — is your last chance if you are behind on fuel.

**Hydration:** 500ml per hour in cool conditions, 750ml if it is warm. June in England is unpredictable. Check the forecast.

**The night before:** Carbohydrate-rich meals — pasta, rice, bread, potatoes. Familiar food, normal portions, spread across the day.

**Breakfast:** Three hours before the start. Porridge, toast with honey, a banana, coffee. Nothing new.

## Equipment and Gear

Any bike in working order is good enough. Road bike, hybrid, gravel bike, mountain bike — thousands of people finish every year on bikes that would make a bike fitter weep. Here is what does matter.

**Saddle comfort.** If your longest ride is currently 90 minutes, you have not tested your saddle for five hours. Do that in training. Chamois cream is strongly recommended for anything over three hours.

**Tyres.** Inflate them to the correct pressure for your weight on event morning. Under-inflated tyres cost you watts and increase puncture risk. Check the sidewall for the recommended range.

**Gears.** Make sure they shift cleanly, especially into the easiest gear. You will need that gear on Ditchling Beacon. If your gears skip or refuse to shift under load, get them serviced. A bike shop adjustment costs less than walking up the one hill everyone told you about.

**Spares.** One spare inner tube, tyre levers, and either a CO2 inflator or a mini pump. Know how to change a tube before the ride.

**Clothing for June.** Jersey, shorts, arm warmers in a back pocket if the morning is cool, a lightweight waterproof if rain is forecast. Sunscreen if it is warm.

**Pockets.** Three flapjacks, two gels, a banana. A phone. A bank card. That is all you need.

## Race Day Pacing

The simplest pacing strategy for London to Brighton: conversation pace all day. Save 10 per cent for Ditchling.

**Miles 0-15:** Accept the congestion. Settle in. Eat your first snack. Do not chase riders who pull away through gaps in the crowd. The road opens up soon enough.

**Miles 15-40:** Steady, sustainable effort. You should be able to talk. If you are breathing hard through Surrey, you are going too fast and you will pay for it on Ditchling. This is precisely the section where disciplined riders build their advantage — not by going fast, but by going easy.

**Miles 40-48:** You will feel the day in your legs. Normal. Eat at mile 40. Drink. Six miles to the bottom of the climb, eight minutes up it, three minutes down the other side.

**Ditchling Beacon:** Your pace. Not the rider next to you, not the group ahead. Find your gear, stay seated, breathe. The crowd at the roadside will carry you.

**The descent to Brighton:** Enjoy it. You have earned every metre.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Starting too fast.** The biggest error and the most common. The Surrey rollers invite a pace that feels sustainable but is not — not over 54 miles. Start slower than feels necessary. You will not regret it at mile 48.

**2. Not eating.** Fifty-four miles does not feel like the kind of distance that requires a nutrition plan. It does. The riders who bonk on Ditchling Beacon are the riders who ate a banana at mile 10 and nothing else. Eat from the start. Eat on schedule. Eat when you are not hungry.

**3. Wrong clothing.** June in England is unpredictable. Too many layers and you overheat through Surrey. Too few and you freeze if the weather turns. Check the forecast the night before and pack a gilet you can stuff in a back pocket.

**4. Trying to race.** London to Brighton is not a race. It is a charity ride with ten thousand other people, many of whom are riding their first organised event. Weaving through crowds and sprinting between groups wastes energy you need for Ditchling and makes the ride worse for everyone around you.

**5. Skipping Ditchling preparation.** If you spend eight weeks riding flat routes and arrive at a 10 per cent gradient having never ridden anything steeper than a Thames towpath, the shock is real. Four visits to a local hill — any hill with a steep section — is enough to remove the fear entirely.

## Travel and Logistics

**Getting to Clapham Common.** Clapham Common or Clapham Junction stations are the closest by train. By car, parking is limited and traffic is heavy from early morning — public transport is easier. If driving, arrive very early and follow the event organiser's parking instructions.

**The start.** Give yourself at least 45 minutes before your wave time. Registration and the charity atmosphere take longer than you expect — nervous energy, matching charity jerseys, people who have never been clipped into a pedal trying to figure out their shoes. Enjoy it.

**The train home from Brighton.** Book your return ticket in advance. Thousands of cyclists flood Brighton station on Sunday afternoon carrying bikes. Trains are crowded. Reservations help. Alternatively, stay the night — Brighton on a summer Sunday evening, having just ridden 54 miles, is a very good place to be.

**The seafront finish.** The crowd, the sea, the medal, the knowledge that Ditchling Beacon is behind you. Take a photo. Eat chips. Sit on the beach. You have earned all of it.

## Recovery Plan

London to Brighton is demanding for a first-timer but it is not a century. Recovery is proportionate.

**Immediately after:** Eat within 30 minutes — carbohydrate and protein. Fish and chips on the seafront counts. Drink until you are no longer thirsty.

**Day 1-2:** Rest or a short walk. Stiff legs are normal.

**Day 3-4:** Easy 20-30 minute spin if you feel like it. Return to normal riding when the legs feel ready. Most riders feel normal within four or five days.

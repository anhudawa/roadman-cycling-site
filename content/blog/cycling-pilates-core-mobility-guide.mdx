## What Cycling Actually Does to Your Body

Here's the thing nobody tells you when you get serious about cycling: the fitter you get on the bike, the more broken you become off it.

Spend ten, twelve, fifteen hours a week in that forward-flexed position and your body adapts — but not in the way you want. Your hip flexors shorten and tighten because they're held in a contracted position for hours. Your glutes, which should be your primary power source, gradually switch off because cycling never asks them to work through full extension. Your thoracic spine — the middle and upper back — stiffens into a permanent curve. And your core? It's barely working at all. The saddle supports your pelvis, the handlebars support your upper body, and your deep stabilising muscles get to take the afternoon off.

I see this constantly. Riders who can hold 300 watts for an hour but can't stand on one leg for ten seconds without wobbling. Cyclists who've built a massive engine but bolted it into a chassis made of wet cardboard.

This is where Pilates comes in. Not as a trend, not as something your partner does on a Tuesday evening, but as the single most efficient way to address the specific damage that cycling inflicts on your body. Joseph Pilates designed his method around controlled movement, deep stabilisation, and restoring balance to bodies that had been locked into repetitive patterns. He was working with injured soldiers and dancers in the 1920s. Nearly a hundred years later, the principles map almost perfectly onto what cyclists need.

## The Evidence: Core Stability and Cycling Performance

Let me break this down with actual numbers, because vague claims about "core strength" mean nothing without context.

A 2019 study published in the Journal of Sports Sciences — Dello Iacono and colleagues — put trained cyclists through a six-week core stability programme. The result: a 4.1 per cent improvement in cycling economy. That means the same power output required measurably less oxygen. Not a bigger engine — a more efficient connection between the engine and the drivetrain.

Professor Stuart McGill, who spent decades at the University of Waterloo studying spinal biomechanics, has consistently argued that core stability — not core strength — is what matters for athletic performance. The distinction is important. Doing a hundred crunches builds rectus abdominis strength. What cyclists actually need is the ability of the deep core muscles — transverse abdominis, internal obliques, multifidus — to hold the pelvis and lumbar spine stable while the legs produce force.

Pilates targets precisely those muscles. Every classical Pilates exercise begins with what instructors call "centering" — engaging the deep abdominal wall before any limb moves. This is the exact motor pattern you need on the bike: pelvis locked, spine neutral, legs free to generate power without the trunk collapsing or rocking.

## Pelvic Stability: Where Your Watts Go to Die

If you've ever watched slow-motion footage of yourself on a turbo trainer during a hard effort, you've probably noticed your hips rocking side to side. Maybe your saddle shifts beneath you. Maybe one hip drops as the opposite leg pushes through the pedal stroke. This is pelvic instability, and it's one of the biggest silent drains on cycling performance.

Every millimetre your pelvis moves laterally during a pedal stroke is energy that isn't going into forward motion. Dr Phil Burt — former head physiotherapist at British Cycling, the person who fitted Chris Froome and Laura Kenny — has spoken extensively about how pelvic control is the foundation of an efficient pedal stroke. His bike fit philosophy starts from the pelvis and works outward.

Pilates builds pelvic stability through two mechanisms. First, the deep core work teaches your body to hold the pelvis neutral under load. Second, the side-lying exercises — clams, side kicks, lateral leg lifts — directly strengthen the gluteus medius and the lateral hip stabilisers that prevent that side-to-side rocking.

You can spend a fortune on a bike fit, but if the muscles responsible for holding your pelvis steady aren't strong enough, you'll drift out of that perfect position within twenty minutes of a hard ride.

## Mat Pilates vs Reformer: What Cyclists Actually Need

The reformer looks impressive. It's that sliding bed with springs and straps that you see in studio photos, and a single session on one will cost you anywhere from $40 to $80 depending on where you live. Here's the honest truth: you don't need it.

Mat Pilates — a yoga mat on your living room floor, no equipment, no subscription — covers everything a cyclist needs. The exercises that address cycling-specific imbalances are almost entirely mat-based: bridges, clams, the hundred, single-leg stretches, spine stretches, swimming. These are bodyweight movements that use gravity and your own limb weight as resistance.

The reformer adds spring-loaded resistance and some variety. It's useful. Some people find it more engaging than mat work. If you enjoy it and can afford it, go for it. But the idea that you need a reformer to get value from Pilates is a myth driven by studio economics, not exercise science.

A 20-minute mat session, done consistently three times a week, will do more for your cycling than a single weekly reformer class. Consistency beats equipment every time.

## The Exercises That Actually Transfer to the Bike

Not every Pilates exercise matters equally for cyclists. These are the ones that directly address the imbalances cycling creates, with clear instructions so you can start today.

### The Hundred

This is the classic Pilates deep core activator. Lie on your back, knees bent at ninety degrees with shins parallel to the floor. Curl your head, neck, and shoulders off the mat — think about drawing your ribs towards your pelvis, not crunching your neck. Extend your arms alongside your body, hovering a few centimetres off the floor. Pump your arms up and down in small, controlled movements — five pumps on the inhale, five on the exhale. Work towards a hundred total pumps.

Why it matters for cycling: this forces sustained engagement of the transverse abdominis — the deep corset muscle that wraps around your midsection and holds your pelvis stable. It also teaches you to maintain core engagement while controlling your breathing, which is exactly what you need during hard efforts on the bike.

If straight legs in the air are too difficult initially, keep your knees bent. The core activation is the point, not the leg position.

### Single-Leg Stretch

Stay on your back with head and shoulders curled up. Pull one knee towards your chest, holding the shin with both hands. Extend the other leg out at roughly a forty-five-degree angle. Switch legs in a controlled, alternating pattern — pulling one knee in as the other extends. Keep your lower back pressed into the mat throughout.

Why it matters for cycling: this mirrors the alternating leg pattern of pedalling while demanding that your core holds stable. It also gently mobilises the hip flexors on the extending leg while the opposite hip moves through flexion. Ten to fifteen repetitions per side.

### Swimming

Lie face down with arms extended overhead, legs straight behind you. Lift your chest slightly off the mat and raise your right arm and left leg simultaneously, then switch — left arm, right leg — in a controlled flutter. Keep the movements small and precise. Think about lengthening through the fingertips and toes rather than lifting high.

Why it matters for cycling: this is one of the few exercises that specifically targets the posterior chain — the spinal extensors, glutes, and hamstrings — in a low-impact way. Cyclists spend hours in forward flexion. Swimming reverses that pattern and strengthens the muscles that keep you from collapsing onto the bars during long rides. Twenty to thirty seconds of continuous movement is a good starting point.

### Glute Bridge with Hold

Lie on your back, feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart, knees bent. Press through your heels to lift your hips until your body forms a straight line from shoulders to knees. Hold at the top for five seconds, squeezing your glutes deliberately. Lower with control. Repeat ten to fifteen times.

The progression: once this feels comfortable, try lifting one foot off the floor at the top of the bridge. Keep your hips level — absolutely level, no drop on the unsupported side. This single-leg variation exposes lateral pelvic instability instantly.

Why it matters for cycling: glute activation and pelvic stability in one movement. If you can't hold your pelvis level during a single-leg bridge, you can't hold it level during a hard pedal stroke. This exercise teaches your body the motor pattern it needs.

### Side-Lying Clam Series

Lie on your side with knees bent at roughly forty-five degrees, feet stacked. Keep your feet together and open your top knee towards the ceiling like a clamshell opening. Control the movement — three seconds up, three seconds down. Don't let your pelvis roll backwards. Fifteen repetitions.

Then extend the top leg straight and lift it to hip height. Small circles — ten forward, ten backward. Keep the movement controlled and the pelvis completely still.

Why it matters for cycling: the gluteus medius is the muscle that prevents your hip from dropping during single-leg loading — which is what every pedal stroke is. Weak glute med is one of the most common findings in cyclists with knee pain, ITB issues, or lower back problems. The clam series isolates it precisely.

### Spine Stretch Forward

Sit tall with legs extended in front of you, slightly wider than hip-width, feet flexed. Reach your arms forward at shoulder height. Exhale and round forward, starting from the head and peeling your spine into a C-curve — think about pulling your navel back towards your spine as your head drops forward. Inhale and stack back up, vertebra by vertebra, rebuilding the tall sitting position from the base.

Why it matters for cycling: this mobilises the thoracic spine — the section that gets locked into flexion on the bike. The controlled segmental movement teaches each vertebra to articulate independently rather than moving as a single stiff block. Five to eight repetitions with focus on the quality of the movement.

### Thread the Needle

Start on all fours — hands under shoulders, knees under hips. Reach your right arm underneath your body towards the left, letting your right shoulder and ear come towards the floor. Follow the hand with your eyes. Hold for two to three seconds, feeling the rotation through your thoracic spine. Return to the start and then reach the same arm towards the ceiling, opening your chest to the right. Alternate between the two positions. Eight to ten repetitions per side.

Why it matters for cycling: thoracic rotation is one of the first things to go in committed cyclists. You need it for looking over your shoulder in traffic, for comfortable group riding, and for preventing compensatory movement in the lower back. Thread the needle restores rotational range through the mid-back without stressing the lumbar spine.

## How Pilates Differs from Yoga for Cyclists

This comes up constantly, so let me be direct about it.

Yoga and Pilates overlap in some areas — both involve floor-based movement, both improve body awareness, both can be done at home. But the intent is fundamentally different, and for cyclists, that difference matters.

Yoga prioritises flexibility, breath work, and relaxation. A typical yoga class will take you through long holds in stretched positions, emphasise letting go of tension, and work towards greater range of motion. That's valuable, and if you enjoy yoga, keep doing it.

Pilates prioritises stability and control through range of motion. Instead of holding a passive stretch, Pilates asks you to move through a range while maintaining precise muscular control. The focus is on which muscles are working, how they're firing, and whether your alignment stays correct throughout.

For most cyclists, the primary deficit isn't flexibility — it's stability. Your hip flexors aren't just tight; they're tight because your glutes aren't strong enough to oppose them. Your lower back isn't just sore; it's sore because your deep core isn't stabilising your pelvis. Stretching alone doesn't fix motor control problems. You need to retrain the muscles to fire correctly, and that's what Pilates does.

The exception: if your hip flexors or hamstrings are so restricted that they're limiting your pedal stroke or forcing compensatory movement, targeted flexibility work — whether from yoga or simple stretching — is worth including alongside Pilates. But for the majority of riders I work with, stability is the bigger gap.

## Scheduling Pilates Around Your Training Week

This is simpler than most people make it. Pilates, done at the intensity level I've described here, creates almost no muscular fatigue. You're not tearing muscle fibres or creating significant metabolic stress. You're doing controlled, low-load movements that promote blood flow, restore range of motion, and reinforce motor patterns.

That means Pilates sits comfortably on easy days or rest days. A 20-minute mat session in the morning before an easy spin, or in the evening after a recovery ride, is ideal. Some riders prefer it as a warm-up before harder sessions — ten minutes of bridges, clams, and the hundred to activate the core and glutes before getting on the bike.

Here's a practical framework for a typical training week:

**Monday (rest day):** 20-25 minutes full mat Pilates session — all seven exercises listed above, with attention to form and breathing.

**Wednesday (easy ride day):** 15 minutes focused on hip and thoracic work — clams, bridges, spine stretch forward, thread the needle.

**Friday or Saturday (before a longer ride):** 10-minute activation routine — the hundred, single-leg stretch, bridges. This switches on the muscles you want firing during the ride.

That's three sessions, a total of about 45-50 minutes across the week. Nobody is too busy for that. If you're spending twelve hours a week on the bike, spending forty-five minutes maintaining the body that sits on it is the minimum.

## Starting Without Overthinking It

The biggest barrier to Pilates isn't difficulty — it's the perception that you need a class, an instructor, or a specific level of flexibility to begin. You don't.

Get a mat. Learn the seven exercises above. Do them three times in your first week, paying attention to the cues about alignment and breathing. By week two, you'll notice you can engage your core more deliberately. By week four, your bridges will feel stronger and your clams will actually fatigue the right muscles. By week six — the same timeframe as the Dello Iacono study — you'll feel the difference on the bike.

Your pelvis will sit quieter during hard efforts. Your lower back will complain less after long rides. Your ability to hold an aggressive position without your upper body collapsing will improve. These aren't vague promises — they're the direct, predictable outcomes of building the stability and mobility that cycling strips away.

The bike builds your engine. Pilates maintains the chassis that holds it together. Twenty minutes, three times a week, on your living room floor. That's the prescription.

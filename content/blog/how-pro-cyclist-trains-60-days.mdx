After hundreds of podcast conversations with World Tour coaches, sports scientists, and professional riders, I had all the knowledge in the world about how the pros train. But knowledge without application is just entertainment. So I decided to apply it — 60 days of training like a professional cyclist, adapted to my amateur schedule.

This is a first-person case study, not a universal prescription. For the complete periodisation framework, use the [cycling training-plan methodology guide](/topics/cycling-training-plans). ## The Setup

The plan was built around the principles I'd heard consistently from every coach and expert on the podcast:

**[Mostly easy, deliberately hard training](/blog/polarised-training-cycling-guide).** I used an 80/20-style distribution: most time at low intensity, a smaller amount at high intensity, and no habit of turning every easy ride into tempo. That is a practical shorthand, not a claim that moderate-intensity work has no place. In a nine-week randomised comparison of 48 trained endurance athletes, a polarised group improved several key variables more than the threshold and high-volume groups; the authors also cautioned that the result needed longer-term and competition-specific testing ([Stöggl and Sperlich, 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2014.00033/full)).

**Structured training blocks.** Three weeks of progressive loading followed by one week of recovery. Not the random "ride when I feel like it" approach I'd been defaulting to.

**Proper fuelling.** [Fuel for the work required](/blog/cycling-weight-loss-fuel-for-the-work-required). I matched carbohydrate to the session instead of forcing one target onto every ride. Published guidance places roughly 30-60g per hour in the 1-2.5-hour range and reserves intakes up to 90g per hour for exercise beyond about 2.5 hours, with the plan practised for tolerance ([Burke et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660838/)).

**Recovery as a training tool.** Sleep prioritised. Easy days easy. No junk miles.

**[Strength and conditioning](/blog/cycling-strength-training-guide).** Two gym sessions per week focusing on posterior chain.

## The Constraints

I'm not a pro. I have a podcast to produce, a business to run, and a life to live. My available training time was 8-10 hours per week — roughly half what a World Tour rider does. The question wasn't whether I could replicate their programme, but whether their principles applied at my scale.

## What Happened

The first two weeks were humbling. My easy rides felt embarrassingly slow. Zone 2 at my level meant riders were passing me on the bike path. But I held the discipline. When my ego screamed to chase someone, I let them go.

The hard sessions were hard. 4x4 minute VO2max intervals where every rep felt like the last thing I wanted to do. Threshold work where the final set was a psychological battle more than a physical one.

By week three, something shifted. The easy rides started feeling easier — not because I was riding harder, but because my body was adapting. The hard sessions felt more controlled. The numbers started creeping up.

By week six, the data was undeniable. FTP had moved 12 watts. Resting heart rate had dropped 4 beats. And perhaps most importantly, I was coming home from training feeling good instead of destroyed. The fuelling strategy was paying off — no bonking, no binge eating after rides, no energy crashes.

My 60-day results: FTP up 18 watts on the test protocol I used. Weight stable (which was deliberate — I wasn't trying to lose). Power-to-weight ratio improved by approximately 0.25 W/kg purely through that measured power change. And my enjoyment of cycling had increased because every session had a purpose. This is an N=1 observation, not an estimate of the gain another rider should expect.

## The Biggest Lessons

**1. A polarised-style approach worked in this amateur case.** You do not need 25 hours of training to separate easy riding from purposeful intensity. At 8-10 hours, that discipline produced a better result for me than my previous, less structured approach. It does not establish that the same distribution is best for every cyclist.

**2. Fuelling is the multiplier.** The single biggest change was eating properly around hard sessions. Everything improved — power, recovery, mood, consistency.

**3. Rest days are training days.** The adaptation doesn't happen during the session. It happens during recovery. Protecting rest was as important as hitting intervals.

**4. Structure beats motivation.** Having a plan meant I didn't have to decide what to do each day. The decision was already made. This removed the biggest barrier to consistency.

## Key Takeaways

- Professional training principles can be adapted to amateur scale; the dose and distribution still need to fit the rider
- An 80/20-style distribution at 8-10 hours/week produced a better result for me than my previous unstructured training
- Fuelling is the multiplier — proper nutrition amplified every training adaptation
- In this N=1 case, 60 days coincided with FTP +18W and resting HR -4 BPM; neither result is a forecast
- Structure beats motivation — having a plan removes the daily decision barrier

You already know how to ride a bike. You've got the miles in. You've done the solo loops, the headwind battles, the Saturday mornings where nobody else was awake and you were already 40km deep. And at some point during one of those rides, a club group swept past you — 15 riders in matching kit, chatting, drafting, barely looking like they were trying — and you thought: *I want that.*

Then you went home and didn't do anything about it.

That's not a criticism. It's just what happens. The gap between wanting to join a cycling club and actually showing up for the first ride is enormous, and it has almost nothing to do with fitness. It's the fear of not knowing the code. Of being the odd one out. Of getting dropped and having to ride home alone while the group disappears up the road without you.

Here's the good news: that fear is almost always worse than the reality. And the steps to get from solo rider to club member are simpler than you think.

## Why Most Amateur Cyclists Don't Join Clubs (and What They're Missing)

The reasons people give are predictable. I'm not fast enough. I don't know anyone. I don't have the right bike. I'll slow everyone down. I don't know the etiquette.

What most people do is keep riding solo and keep telling themselves they'll join next season. What actually works is accepting that every single person in that club felt exactly the same way before their first ride — and showing up anyway.

The thing about club cycling is that it makes you faster without trying harder. Riding in a group, even a slow one, teaches you to hold a wheel, read the road, anticipate surges, and manage your effort. The aerodynamic draft alone means you're doing 25-30% less work at the same speed compared to riding alone. Dr Andrew Coggan's research on drafting showed that at 40kph, a rider sitting second wheel saves roughly 35% of their power output. That's not a marginal gain. That's a different sport.

Beyond the physics, there's the accountability. When you know the club ride leaves at 8am on Saturday and people are expecting you, you go. When it's just you and your alarm clock, the sofa wins more often than it should.

And then there's the knowledge transfer. Club riders have collectively ridden every road within 80km of your town. They know which lanes have potholes, which cafes have decent coffee, which routes avoid the A-roads, and which hills are worth the suffering. Years of local knowledge, handed over for free, on a Tuesday evening ride.

Most solo riders are missing three things: the efficiency of the draft, the motivation of a group, and the craft of riding in close proximity to other people. A club gives you all three. The only cost is showing up.

## Types of Clubs — Racing Clubs, Social Clubs, Sportive-Focused Clubs, Women's Clubs

Not all clubs are the same, and picking the wrong type is the fastest way to have a bad experience. Understanding what each type offers — and what it expects — saves you a lot of wasted energy.

### Racing Clubs

These are affiliated with your national governing body (British Cycling in the UK, Cycling Ireland, USA Cycling) and their primary focus is competition. They'll run chaingangs, interval sessions, and structured training rides. The pace groups tend to be faster, and there's an expectation that you're working toward racing or at least riding with intent.

Racing clubs are brilliant if you want to compete, but they can be intimidating for newcomers. The culture often leans toward performance — watts per kilogram, race results, training zones. If that excites you, great. If it makes you anxious, look elsewhere first.

### Social and Touring Clubs

The backbone of amateur cycling. These clubs exist primarily for the group ride and the coffee stop. They'll have multiple pace groups, a calendar of sportives and events, and a culture that values showing up consistently over showing up fast.

Social clubs are where most people should start. The pace is manageable, the atmosphere is inclusive, and you'll learn group riding skills in a lower-pressure environment. Many social clubs also organise tours — week-long trips to Mallorca, the Pyrenees, or Girona — which are some of the best cycling experiences you can have.

### Sportive-Focused Clubs

A hybrid between racing and social. These clubs organise around specific events — the Marmotte, the Etape du Tour, the Dragon Ride, the Fred Whitton. Members train together toward shared goals, and the club ride often mirrors the demands of the target event.

If you've got a big sportive on your calendar and you want training partners who are working toward the same thing, this is your tribe. The shared goal creates a natural bond, and you'll push each other in ways that solo training rarely achieves.

### Women's Clubs and Women-Only Groups

The growth of women's cycling clubs over the past decade has been one of the best things to happen to the sport. Clubs like Breeze (British Cycling's women's programme), InternationElles, and hundreds of local women's groups provide spaces where riders can develop skills and confidence without some of the dynamics that can make mixed groups less welcoming.

If you're a woman who's been hesitant about joining a mixed club, a women's group is a superb way to build bunch skills and confidence first. Many riders start in women's groups and later join mixed clubs as well — or don't, because the women's group gives them everything they need.

### Virtual Clubs

A newer category, but a real one. Virtual clubs on Zwift, Wahoo SYSTM, and other platforms organise group rides, races, and training sessions entirely online. They're useful for winter training, for riders in remote areas, and for people whose schedules don't align with local club timetables. They won't teach you how to hold a wheel in a crosswind, but they'll give you structure, community, and friendly competition.

## How to Find the Right Club for You — What to Look For and What to Avoid

Finding clubs is easy. Finding the *right* club takes a bit more thought.

### Where to Search

Start with your national federation's club finder. British Cycling, Cycling Ireland, USA Cycling, and Cycling Australia all have searchable databases. Then check Strava — many clubs have Strava groups where you can see how active they are, what routes they ride, and what kind of pace they hold.

Social media is useful here. Search for cycling clubs in your area on Facebook and Instagram. Look at their recent posts. Are they welcoming? Do they post photos of riders across different ages and abilities? Or is every photo a race podium?

Word of mouth matters more than you'd think. If you know anyone who rides — a colleague, a neighbour, someone from the school run who's always in lycra — ask them. Personal recommendations filter out the clubs with toxic cultures faster than any website.

### What to Look For

**Multiple pace groups.** This is non-negotiable. A club with a single group ride that averages 32kph is not a beginner-friendly club, regardless of what their website says. You want a club that runs at least two, preferably three or four groups, with clearly defined paces. Something like: social (22-25kph), intermediate (26-30kph), fast (31-35kph), and race-pace (36kph+). The numbers will vary by region and terrain, but the principle holds — you need a group that matches your current fitness, not the fitness you hope to have in six months.

**A welcoming first contact.** Email or message the club before you show up. Tell them your experience level, your approximate average speed on a solo ride, and ask which group would suit you. The response tells you everything. A good club will reply quickly, give you a named person to look for on the morning, and tell you what to expect. A bad club won't reply at all.

**Structured rides with a ride leader.** The best clubs designate a ride leader for each group, someone who sets the pace, makes route decisions, and ensures nobody gets left behind. Ride leaders transform the experience from "trying to survive in a fast group" to "being guided through a well-organised morning on the bike."

**A no-drop policy on social rides.** This means the group waits at the top of climbs and at key junctions. Fast riders can push the hill, but they regroup at the top so nobody is left behind. No-drop policies are the single best indicator of a club's culture.

### What to Avoid

**Clubs where the slowest group is still fast.** If their "easy" ride averages 30kph, that's not an easy ride for most amateur cyclists. It means the club doesn't really cater for beginners, whatever their website claims.

**Clubs with no women members.** In 2026, a club with zero women members is telling you something about its culture. That's true whether you're a woman or a man — inclusive clubs are better clubs, full stop.

**Clubs where everyone is 25 and racing.** Unless you're 25 and racing. There's nothing wrong with these clubs — they're just not where a 42-year-old returning rider is going to feel at home.

**Any club that makes you feel like you need to prove yourself before they'll accept you.** You're a paying member, not a transfer target. If the vibe is gatekeepy during your first contact, it won't improve once you're riding with them.

## The First Ride — What to Expect, What to Wear, What to Bring

You've found a club. You've emailed them. They've told you which group to join and where to meet. Now you're lying awake the night before, wondering what you've agreed to. Normal.

### What to Bring

- Two full bidons. You'll drink more than you think because group riding is more variable than solo — surges, stops, and restarts all cost energy.
- A spare inner tube, tyre levers, and a mini pump or CO2 inflator. Puncturing on your first club ride is character-building. Puncturing and not having the tools to fix it is mortifying.
- Food. An energy bar or two in your jersey pocket. Club rides usually include a cafe stop, but bring food anyway — you don't want to bonk on the way there.
- Cash or a card for coffee. The cafe stop is not optional. It's where half the socialising happens, and skipping it to "save time" will mark you as someone who doesn't understand what club riding is about.
- Your phone, fully charged. For emergencies and for route tracking if you get separated from the group.

### What to Wear

Clean cycling kit. It doesn't need to be expensive, and it absolutely doesn't need to be the club jersey — nobody expects you to buy that before your first ride. Padded shorts (not boxers under shorts, actual cycling shorts), a jersey with rear pockets, gloves, helmet. If it's cold, arm warmers and a gilet. If it's wet, a rain jacket.

Nobody will judge your bike. I promise. The rider on the 15-year-old aluminium bike with Tiagra groupset who holds a steady line and signals hazards is infinitely more welcome than the rider on the brand-new Pinarello who half-wheels and forgets to call out potholes.

### What to Expect

You'll arrive early because you're nervous. Good. Use that time to introduce yourself to the ride leader. Tell them it's your first time. They've heard it a hundred times and they'll look after you.

The group will roll out slowly — neutralised for the first couple of kilometres to let everyone settle in and warm up. This is your chance to find a wheel and get comfortable. Don't go to the front. Sit in the middle or back of the group, find someone who looks like they know what they're doing, and follow their wheel.

The pace will feel easier than your solo rides at the same speed, because you're drafting. This is the magic of group riding — you'll wonder why you spent years fighting the wind alone.

There will be a cafe stop. Order a coffee and something to eat. Talk to people. This is where relationships form. Don't be the rider who sits on their phone the whole time.

The ride back will be slightly faster, because that's what always happens. But if you're in the right group, it'll be manageable.

When you get home, you'll think: *That was brilliant. Why didn't I do that months ago?* Then you'll set your alarm for the same time next week.

## Club Culture Decoded — The Pace Groups, The Coffee Stop, The Unwritten Rules

Every club has its own micro-culture, but certain patterns repeat everywhere. Knowing them in advance removes the guesswork.

### Pace Groups

Pace groups exist for a reason — to keep riders of similar ability together so everyone has a good ride. The system only works if people are honest about their level. What most people do is overestimate their fitness and join a group that's too fast. What actually works is starting one group down from where you think you belong. You can always move up. Moving down mid-ride after you've been dropped is much harder.

The social group (sometimes called the cafe ride, the easy ride, or the C group) is typically 22-26kph average. This is a conversational pace. You should be able to chat throughout. If you can't, you're either in the wrong group or you need more base miles.

The intermediate group (the B group) usually sits at 27-31kph. This is where most amateur cyclists land. It's quick enough to feel like proper training, slow enough that you can recover between surges. The riding here is more purposeful — you'll do through-and-off, the climbs will have some intent, and the cafe stop might be shorter.

The fast group (the A group) is 32kph and above. This is where club racers, ex-pros, and seriously fit amateurs ride. If you're here on your first club ride, you've either misjudged your level or you're already a strong cyclist.

### The Coffee Stop

The cafe stop is sacred. It's not a pitstop — it's the social hub of the ride. Thirty to forty-five minutes is typical. You order food and drink, you sit down, you talk about the ride, about cycling, about everything else.

Etiquette at the cafe: don't take forever ordering, don't spread your kit all over the table, and tip the staff. Cycling clubs are regular customers and the relationship with the cafe matters. Some of the best cafes in the country survive because of cycling clubs, and the clubs that treat them well get looked after in return.

### Unwritten Rules

**Signal hazards.** Point down at potholes. Wave your hand behind your back when moving out around a parked car. Call out "car back," "car front," "slowing," "stopping." You are the eyes and ears for every rider behind you.

**Don't half-wheel.** If you're riding two-abreast, ride level with the person next to you. Don't let your front wheel creep ahead. It silently drags the pace up and annoys everyone.

**Don't attack on the first ride.** Seriously. Even if you feel great and the pace feels easy, sit in. Watch. Learn how this particular group works before you start going to the front. Demonstrating that you're smooth and safe is worth far more than demonstrating that you're strong.

**Offer help with mechanicals.** If someone punctures, stop and ask if they need a hand. Even if you can't fix it yourself, the solidarity matters.

**Say thank you.** After the ride, thank the ride leader. Thank whoever organised the route. It's thankless work and a bit of appreciation goes a long way.

## Fitness Anxiety — "Am I Fast Enough?" (Spoiler: Yes)

This is the big one. The fear that stops more people from joining clubs than any other single factor. *Am I fast enough?*

Here's where it gets really interesting. You're almost certainly faster in a group than you think you are solo. The draft effect — sitting behind another rider and letting them break the wind — reduces your effort by 25-35% at moderate speeds. Dr Bert Blocken's wind tunnel research at TU Eindhoven showed that even the second rider in a two-person group saves around 27% of their aerodynamic drag. In a larger group, riders in the middle can save over 40%.

What that means in practical terms: if you average 25kph solo, you can comfortably sit in a group doing 28-30kph and use the same energy. The group makes you faster. Not a bit faster — meaningfully, measurably faster.

The other piece of this is that clubs are not selection squads. They're not looking for talent. They're looking for members who show up, ride safely, and contribute to the group's culture. The rider who averages 22kph but holds a steady line, points out hazards, and buys the ride leader a coffee is more valuable to a club than the rider who averages 35kph but rides unpredictably and never talks to anyone.

If you can ride for 90 minutes to two hours at a steady pace — and if you've been training solo, you almost certainly can — there is a club group for you. Not the fast group, probably. But a group where you'll fit, where you'll be challenged without being destroyed, and where you'll improve simply by riding with other people.

The fitness will come. Dr Stephen Seiler's research on training intensity distribution shows that the majority of endurance development comes from the kind of steady, moderate-intensity riding that social club groups do every weekend. You don't need to be fit to join. You join, and the fitness follows.

And if you're still worried, here's a practical step you can go and do this week: ride your usual loop, note your average speed over 90 minutes of steady riding (not including stops), and email the club. Tell them that number. They'll tell you exactly which group to join. No guessing, no anxiety.

## Getting the Most from Club Membership — Training Partners, Knowledge Sharing, Events

Joining is step one. Getting the full value from membership is a separate skill.

### Training Partners

The single biggest benefit of a club is finding people who ride at your pace and want to ride on the same days you do. One or two reliable training partners will transform your cycling more than any piece of equipment or any training plan.

After a few club rides, you'll naturally gravitate toward riders whose pace matches yours and whose company you enjoy. Suggest a midweek ride. Swap numbers. Build a small group that rides outside the official club schedule. This is how the best training relationships form — not through forced partnerships, but through shared miles.

### Knowledge Sharing

Clubs are repositories of practical knowledge. The experienced rider who's been cycling for 20 years can teach you more about bike fit, nutrition timing, pacing, and tactical riding in one cafe conversation than you'll learn from six months of watching YouTube videos.

Ask questions. Experienced riders love sharing what they know — it's one of the quiet pleasures of club life. Ask about their training, their bike setup, their favourite routes. You'll get more useful, specific, and locally relevant advice than any generic article can provide. Including this one.

Some clubs also run skills sessions — group riding drills, bike handling courses, basic maintenance workshops. These are gold dust. Sign up for everything.

### Events

Clubs organise events that you'd never do alone. Reliability trials. Time trials. Club championships. Touring weekends. Interclub races. Audax rides. Charity sportives. The calendar fills up fast, and suddenly your season has structure and purpose.

Time trials are worth a specific mention. The club "10" — a 10-mile time trial on a midweek evening — is one of cycling's perfect formats. Just you, the clock, and a local course you'll come to know better than your commute. It's honest, it's measurable, and it's the best way to track your fitness over a season. Most clubs run a series through the summer months, and the improvement from first to last is always bigger than people expect.

Enter things. Even if you think you're not ready. Events give your training meaning, and meaning makes training stick.

### Club Kit

Once you've been a member for a few months, buy the club jersey. It's not vanity. It's belonging. Wearing the club colours on a Sunday morning, rolling out with 30 other people in the same kit — there's a solidarity to it that sounds corny until you experience it.

## Online Communities as an Alternative — Skool, Strava Clubs, Facebook Groups

Not everyone wants a local club. Maybe your nearest club is 40km away. Maybe their ride schedule clashes with your family commitments. Maybe you're an introvert who loves cycling but dreads the idea of small talk at a cafe stop with strangers. All valid.

The good news is that the concept of a cycling community has expanded far beyond the traditional club structure. You can get connection, accountability, and knowledge without ever meeting anyone in a car park at 7:30am on a Saturday.

### Strava Clubs

Strava clubs are the lightest-touch version of community. You join, your rides appear in the club feed, you give and receive kudos. Some Strava clubs are active enough to organise group rides through the platform. Others are essentially leaderboard competitions. The quality depends entirely on the moderators and the culture they build.

What Strava clubs do well: passive accountability. Knowing that your training partners can see whether you rode or not is a surprisingly powerful motivator. What they don't do well: deep connection. Comments on Strava are shallow by design.

### Facebook Groups

Facebook groups can be brilliant or terrible, depending on moderation. The best cycling groups on Facebook are hyper-local — "cycling in [your county]" type groups where people share route recommendations, warn about road closures, and organise informal rides. The worst are echo chambers of argument and gear snobbery.

Look for groups with clear rules, active moderators, and a culture of helping rather than showing off. When they work, they're a fantastic supplement to club membership or a genuine alternative for riders who can't commit to scheduled group rides.

### Skool Communities

Here's where it gets really interesting for cyclists who want depth, not just surface-level interaction. Skool is a platform built for learning communities, and it's where some of the most engaged cycling groups now live.

The Roadman Cycling community on Skool is built for exactly the kind of rider who's reading this article. We run a free Clubhouse where you can connect with other riders, ask questions, and access basic resources. It's a genuine community — not a sales funnel with a comment section bolted on.

For riders who want more, the Not Done Yet (NDY) programme sits inside the same community at $195/month. NDY is structured coaching, training resources, accountability, and a peer group of riders who are all working toward getting better. It's the depth of a coaching relationship combined with the support of a club, delivered online so your geography and schedule don't matter.

What makes Skool different from Facebook groups or Strava clubs is the structure. Conversations are organised by topic rather than buried in an infinite feed. Resources stay findable. And because it's a dedicated platform rather than a corner of a social media app, the people there are there because they want to be, not because an algorithm put a post in front of them.

You can check it out at [https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling). The Clubhouse is free to join, so there's no barrier to seeing whether it's the kind of community that fits you.

### Discord and WhatsApp Groups

Worth mentioning because they're increasingly popular, especially among younger riders. Discord servers for cycling communities offer real-time chat, training discussion, and race analysis. WhatsApp groups are often used by local clubs as their primary communication channel — so even if you don't attend every ride, staying in the WhatsApp group keeps you connected.

### The Hybrid Approach

The best option for most riders isn't either/or. It's both. A local club for the weekend ride, the cafe stop, and the in-person connection. An online community for the deeper training discussions, the weekday accountability, and the knowledge that goes beyond what your local club can offer.

You don't have to choose one. Collect the communities that serve you and build a cycling life that fits your actual situation — not some idealised version where you have unlimited time and live next to the perfect club.

## Making the Decision

You've read this far, which means the curiosity is real. The question isn't whether club cycling would benefit you — it would. The question is whether you're going to keep thinking about it or actually do something about it.

Here's what you can go and do this week:

1. Search your national federation's website for clubs within 20km of your home.
2. Look those clubs up on Strava, Facebook, and Instagram. Check their recent activity.
3. Email or message two or three that look promising. Tell them your experience level and ask about their beginner or social group.
4. Pick the one that responds best and commit to turning up for their next ride.
5. Set a reminder. Put it in your calendar. Tell someone you're going so you can't back out.

That's it. Five steps. The longest part is waiting for the email reply.

Every club cyclist was a nervous newcomer once. Every single one. The riders who sweep past you in matching kit on Saturday mornings — they all had a first ride where they didn't know anyone, didn't know the route, and spent the whole time hoping they wouldn't do something embarrassing.

They showed up anyway. And now they can't imagine cycling without it.

Your turn.

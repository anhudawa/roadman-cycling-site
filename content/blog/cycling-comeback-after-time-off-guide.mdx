You know the moment. You walk past the garage and there it is, leaning against the wall behind a lawnmower and two boxes of stuff you were supposed to bring to the charity shop eight months ago. Tyres flat. Bar tape grey with dust. A thin layer of cobwebs connecting the right brake lever to the top tube like the bike has started growing roots.

And something in you flickers. Not the full flame — not yet — but a small, persistent flicker that says *I used to ride that thing. I used to be somebody on that thing.*

Here's the thing nobody tells you about coming back to cycling after a long break: the hardest part is not the fitness. The fitness comes back faster than you expect. The hardest part is the person you have to become in the process — the person who was once a cyclist, then wasn't, and now has to figure out what kind of cyclist they are this time around.

I've been through it. Not once. Twice. And I've spoken to thousands of riders on the [podcast](/podcast) who have been through it in their own way — a back injury that stole a year, a divorce that stole the will, a career change that stole the time, a pandemic that stole the routine. The story is always different. The feelings are remarkably the same.

This is the honest version. Not the "ten tips to bounce back stronger" version. The one that talks about what it actually feels like, what your body is actually doing, and how to get from the dusty bike in the garage to a version of riding that might be even better than what you had before.

## The Identity Problem Nobody Warns You About

Let me break this down, because it matters more than any training plan.

When you ride regularly — properly regularly, the kind of regular where your week has a shape because of the bike — cycling becomes part of your identity. You are a cyclist. You have the tan lines, the calves, the Rapha kit, the opinions about tyre pressure. You know which cafe does the best flat white at the halfway point. You have a group. You have a Strava profile that tells a story.

When you stop, that identity does not switch off cleanly. It fades. Slowly, then all at once. You stop checking Strava. You stop following the racing. Your mates stop texting about Sunday's ride because they know you'll say no. At some point — and you won't remember when — you start thinking of yourself as someone who *used to* ride.

That shift, from "I am" to "I was", is surprisingly painful. And coming back means reversing it, which requires you to sit in the most uncomfortable identity of all: the person who is trying to be something they used to be, without knowing if they can pull it off.

Here's where it gets really interesting. The riders who come back best are not the ones who try to pretend the gap never happened. They are the ones who accept that the person getting back on the bike is a different person to the one who got off it. [Joe Friel](/guests/joe-friel) told me something on the show that I think about constantly — he said the worst thing a comeback athlete can do is treat the return as a restoration. You are not restoring an old painting. You are starting a new one with better brushes.

Sit with that for a second, because it changes everything about how you approach the first ride, the first month, and the first moment you get dropped on a climb you used to fly up.

## The First Ride Is Going to Hurt Your Ego More Than Your Legs

You already know the lungs will burn. You already know the legs will be heavy. None of that is the real shock.

The real shock is the numbers. The speed that used to be your easy spin is now your limit. The climb that used to be a 12-minute warmup is now a 17-minute battle. The heart rate that used to sit at 140 on a flat road is now sitting at 155 and the flat road feels like a headwind.

What most people do: panic. Push harder. Tell themselves they just need to "get through" the first few rides and the fitness will come flooding back. Then they go out again the next day, harder again, and by the end of week two they have a sore knee, a sore back, or a sore ego that keeps them off the bike for another six months.

What actually works: ride the first ride. Accept what it tells you. Write it down — not the data, the feeling. Then ride again two days later at the same easy effort. And again. And again. The first two weeks are not training. They are reintroduction. You are saying hello to the bike, and the bike is saying hello back, and neither of you needs to shout.

## What Your Body Is Actually Doing (The Short Version)

I've written a [full physiological breakdown](/blog/cycling-comeback-after-break-complete-guide) of how detraining and retraining works — the muscle memory research, the capillary persistence, the mitochondrial rebuild timeline. If you want the deep science, read that.

Here is the version that actually matters when you are standing in the garage wondering whether this is a terrible idea.

**Your heart and lungs come back fast.** Within ten days of consistent riding, blood plasma volume increases, stroke volume improves, and your resting heart rate starts dropping. Two to three weeks in, you will notice that the same effort feels easier. This is real adaptation, and it happens first because the cardiovascular system is the fastest responder in the body.

**Your muscles remember.** This is the part that feels like good news because it is good news. When you trained before, your muscle fibres added extra nuclei — tiny control centres that direct protein synthesis. Kristian Gundersen's research at the University of Oslo showed those nuclei persist for years, possibly decades, even after the muscle itself has atrophied. You are not rebuilding from scratch. You are reactivating a system that was built once and mothballed. The same fitness that took 18 months to build originally can return in 10 to 14 weeks.

**Your connective tissue is the bottleneck.** Tendons, ligaments, the deep stabiliser muscles around your hips and pelvis — these adapt on a much slower timeline. Four to six weeks minimum. And this is where the danger lives: your cardiovascular system says go, your muscles say go, and your tendons say absolutely not yet. The riders who get hurt in a comeback are almost always riders who listened to the engine and ignored the chassis.

The practical takeaway is straightforward. Your body wants to come back. It has the infrastructure. But the infrastructure needs four to six weeks of gentle loading before you can build on it properly. Patience is not a personality trait here. It is a physical requirement.

## Being Dropped by Your Mates: The Social Nightmare Nobody Talks About

Let's talk about the thing that actually keeps comeback riders awake at night.

You used to sit mid-pack on the Saturday ride. Comfortable. Contributing turns on the front. Chatting in the bunch. Maybe you were the one who set the pace on the first climb. Maybe you were the one who always had a spare tube.

Now you are back, and you know — you *know* — that you cannot hold that wheel anymore. And the thought of rocking up to that same ride, clipping in next to the same riders, and being the one who drifts off the back on the first rise is genuinely mortifying.

This is not vanity. This is a real social cost. Cycling groups have hierarchies, and those hierarchies are built on performance. When your position in the hierarchy drops, it changes how you feel in the group. Even if nobody says a word — and most decent riding partners won't — you feel it. The gap between where you sit in the bunch and where you used to sit is a gap that runs straight through your self-worth.

Here's what I tell every comeback rider who asks about group rides: don't do them for the first four weeks. Minimum. Ride alone. Ride with one patient mate who understands the deal. But do not put yourself in a situation where social pressure overrides physiological common sense.

When you do go back to the group — and you should, because the social side of cycling is half the point — go back to a different group. A slower one. A social one. One where nobody knows your old numbers and nobody cares about your current ones. Build your way back up the groups rather than dropping down from the one you used to lead. The psychology of climbing up is completely different to the psychology of falling down, even if the destination is the same.

The riders in the [Roadman community](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) talk about this openly, which is one of the reasons it works. Comeback riders posting their first ride back, their first solo century, their first time holding a wheel they couldn't hold two months ago. When the whole room is rebuilding, nobody feels like the weak link.

## The Expectation Trap

The single most destructive sentence a comeback cyclist can say to themselves is: "I used to be able to..."

Every time you finish that sentence, you create a gap. Between now and then, between who you are and who you were, between the numbers on your screen and the numbers in your memory. That gap generates frustration, and frustration generates bad decisions — riding too hard, skipping rest days, chasing Strava segments you have no business chasing in week three.

What actually works is building a wall between past-you and present-you. Concrete, not glass. You do not need to see through it.

Practically, this means three things:

1. **Do not look at old training files.** Archive them. Move them to a different folder. Whatever it takes to stop yourself opening TrainingPeaks and scrolling back to the rides from two summers ago.
2. **Set a new FTP baseline.** Not from memory. From a test. The [FTP Test Calculator](/tools/ftp-test) converts your 20-minute effort into zones. Do it in week three or four, when the initial cardiovascular surge has settled. That number is your number. Not the old one.
3. **Measure rate of improvement, not absolute values.** An FTP that goes from 180 to 210 in eight weeks is a 17% improvement. That is remarkable progress regardless of the fact that you once held 275. Celebrate the trajectory. The destination takes care of itself.

[Prof. Stephen Seiler](/guests/stephen-seiler) has made the point repeatedly that the riders who sustain long careers are the ones who frame each training block on its own terms. A comeback is just a training block with a lower starting point. Nothing more.

## The 12-Week Rebuild: Week by Week

I have a [full 12-week training plan](/blog/comeback-cyclist-12-week-return-plan) with specific prescriptions. What follows is the week-by-week emotional and physical shape of the comeback — what to expect, what to feel, and what to do about it.

### Weeks 1-2: The Reintroduction

**On the bike:** Three to four rides per week. Forty-five to sixty minutes each. Zone 1-2 only — conversational pace, full stop. Cadence above 80 RPM to protect the tendons. No hills steeper than you can spin up seated.

**Off the bike:** Two bodyweight strength sessions per week, 20 minutes each. Single-leg glute bridges, step-ups, lateral band walks, calf raises, dead bugs for core stability. The goal is connective tissue protection, not performance.

**How you will feel:** Simultaneously encouraged and disappointed. The riding will feel easier by ride four, which is the cardiovascular system waking up. But the numbers will look nothing like what you remember, and the temptation to do more will be intense. Resist it.

**The danger:** Doing too much too soon because the lungs feel ready. They are. Your tendons are not.

### Weeks 3-4: The Quiet Wall

**On the bike:** Four rides per week. Sixty to seventy-five minutes each. Still Zone 1-2. Build one ride to 90 minutes by end of week four. Week four is a recovery week — drop volume by 30%.

**Off the bike:** Three strength sessions. Add single-leg hip hinges (bodyweight only), wall sits, and banded clamshells. Start a simple hip flexor stretching routine.

**How you will feel:** This is the motivational valley. The novelty is gone. The numbers haven't moved in a way that feels meaningful. The riders you follow on Strava are posting rides that make yours look like recovery spins. You will question whether any of this is working.

It is working. The adaptation is happening beneath the surface — tissue remodelling, mitochondrial proliferation, neural pathway reinforcement. None of it shows up on your head unit yet. All of it shows up in weeks five through eight.

**The danger:** Quitting. Not because you are in pain, not because you are injured, but because the return on effort feels invisible. More comeback riders stop in weeks three and four than at any other point. If you make it through these two weeks, the probability of a successful comeback rises dramatically.

### Weeks 5-6: The Turn

**On the bike:** Four to five rides. One ride reaches two hours. Introduce one tempo session per week — 2 x 10 minutes at 76-85% of your new FTP (from the test you did in week three or four), with five minutes easy between efforts.

**Off the bike:** Three strength sessions, progressing the exercises — slower tempos on the single-leg work, longer holds on the planks, adding light resistance bands to the glute bridges.

**How you will feel:** This is the week where the switch flips. Rides that felt desperate in week two start to feel manageable. Your heart rate at a given effort drops noticeably. You catch yourself enjoying a descent or holding a pace that would have buried you three weeks ago. The first genuine moment of "I can do this" arrives somewhere around ride 15 or 16.

**The danger:** Overconfidence. The engine is coming back and it feels brilliant, so the temptation is to skip ahead to threshold work or jump back into the group ride. Hold the line. Two more weeks of aerobic development here pays dividends for the next six months.

### Weeks 7-8: The Rebuild Clicks

**On the bike:** Five rides per week if you can manage it. Long ride reaches two and a half hours. Tempo session progresses to 2 x 15 minutes. Week eight is a recovery week and a retest — do a fresh 20-minute FTP test. The number will have moved. Possibly by a lot.

**Off the bike:** Maintain three strength sessions. You should be noticeably more stable on the bike by now — less lower back fatigue on longer rides, less knee discomfort on climbs.

**How you will feel:** Strong. Recognisably like a cyclist again. The tan lines are coming back. The legs have shape. The after-ride tiredness is the good kind — earned, not defeated. This is also when the social pull of group rides becomes hardest to resist. If you've been patient, you can rejoin a group now. Choose a social pace. Ride at the back. Contribute nothing on the front and feel zero guilt about it.

**The danger:** Ignoring the recovery week. Week eight is a down week, not an optional one. Your body has absorbed seven weeks of progressive loading and it needs consolidation time. Skip it and weeks nine through twelve will be flat at best, injured at worst.

### Weeks 9-10: Intensity Arrives

**On the bike:** Introduce one threshold session per week — 3 x 8 minutes at 95-100% FTP. Keep one tempo session. Keep the long ride. The rest stays Zone 2.

**Off the bike:** Strength drops to two sessions as riding volume is higher. Focus on maintenance.

**How you will feel:** Like yourself. Not the old yourself, but a new version that is starting to feel competent and familiar. You can hold a wheel on a group ride. You can push a climb without disintegrating. The gap between where you are and where you were is closing, and more importantly, you have started to care less about the gap and more about the riding itself.

### Weeks 11-12: The New Baseline

**On the bike:** Threshold work progresses to 2 x 15 minutes. If you feel strong and fully recovered, add one VO2max session in week 11 — 5 x 3 minutes at 110-115% FTP. If in doubt, skip it. There is no rush. Week 12 is a taper week — reduce volume by 40% and do a final FTP retest.

**Off the bike:** Two strength sessions. Light. Recovery-focused.

**How you will feel:** The retest at the end of week 12 is the moment that makes the previous 11 weeks worth it. Most riders who follow this structure reach 80-90% of their previous fitness. Some exceed it, particularly riders whose previous "peak" was undermined by overtraining, poor recovery, or an unsustainable schedule. The new version, built properly, often outperforms the old one because the foundation is sounder.

**What happens next:** Week 12 is not the end. It is the beginning of your next block, trained from a legitimate baseline, with connective tissue that can handle real work and an aerobic engine that has been rebuilt rather than patched. From here, the normal rules of structured training apply.

## The Part That Matters More Than Watts

Here's the thing nobody tells you about the comeback that turns out to be the most important part: many riders come back and discover they like the riding *more* than they did before.

Not the racing. Not the numbers. The riding.

The break did something that years of structured training never could — it recalibrated what the bike means. Before the break, riding might have become about average speed, FTP tests, Strava KOMs, Sunday morning hierarchy. After the break, riding becomes about being outside, turning the pedals, the coffee stop, the view from the top of the hill that you never looked at when you were staring at your stem.

I hear this from comeback riders constantly. "I enjoy it more now." "I don't care about the numbers the way I used to." "I ride for different reasons and the riding is better."

That does not mean performance stops mattering. It means performance takes its proper place — as one part of a larger thing, rather than the only thing. And paradoxically, riders who hold that perspective tend to train more consistently, recover better, and ultimately perform at a higher level than riders who treat every ride as an exam.

## The Strength Work Is Not Optional

I cannot say this clearly enough: bodyweight strength training from week one is the difference between a comeback that sticks and a comeback that ends in a physio's waiting room.

Two to three sessions per week. Twenty to thirty minutes. Nothing complicated. Here is the full list:

**Lower body**
- Single-leg glute bridges — 3 x 12 each side
- Step-ups onto a knee-height step — 3 x 10 each side
- Lateral band walks — 3 x 15 each direction
- Single-leg hip hinges (bodyweight) — 3 x 8 each side
- Wall sits — 3 x 30-45 seconds
- Calf raises — 3 x 15

**Core**
- Dead bugs — 3 x 10 each side
- Front plank — 3 x 30-60 seconds
- Pallof press with resistance band — 3 x 10 each side
- Bird dogs — 3 x 10 each side

**Mobility**
- Hip flexor stretch — 60 seconds each side
- Thoracic spine rotation — 10 each side
- Pigeon stretch — 60 seconds each side

This is the programme that protects the knees, the hips, the lower back, and the ankles. Every exercise is bodyweight or band-based. No heavy loading, no barbell work. The purpose is tissue tolerance, not muscle building. When [Dan Lorang](/guests/dan-lorang) designs return-to-training protocols for his World Tour riders, strength work goes in from day one. Not day thirty. Day one.

## What to Do If You Want to Quit in Week Three

You will want to quit in week three. Or week four. Almost everyone does. The novelty has gone, the progress is invisible, and the voice in your head is doing its best work: *This is pointless. You're too old. You've left it too long. You were never that good anyway.*

Three things to do when that voice gets loud:

**Shrink the commitment.** Don't think about the 12-week plan. Think about the next ride. Just the next one. Can you do 45 minutes tomorrow? That is the only question that matters.

**Change the context.** If you've been riding the same route, ride a different one. If you've been riding alone, call a mate. If you've been riding the turbo, go outside. Monotony kills more comebacks than injury does.

**Talk about it.** Not to someone who will say "just push through" — to someone who has been through it and remembers what it felt like. That is why communities like the [Roadman group on Skool](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) exist. Post the honest version. "Week three. Want to pack it in. Someone tell me this gets better." It gets better. And hearing it from someone who is six weeks ahead of you on the same path is worth more than any training plan.

## The Comeback Is Not a Straight Line

Some weeks you will feel like a different person. Stronger, faster, sharper. The next week you will feel flat, heavy, and irritable. This is normal. Adaptation is not linear. It comes in waves — load, fatigue, recovery, supercompensation — and the subjective experience of that process is messy.

Do not panic when you have a bad week. Check three things: sleep, stress, nutrition. If one of those is compromised, the training is not the problem. Fix the input before you change the programme.

And do not celebrate too loudly when you have a great week. A single good ride does not mean you are back. A sustained block of consistent training means you are back. Let the trend tell the story, not any single data point.

## What the Comeback Actually Gives You

I want to end on this, because it is the part that matters most and the part that training plans never cover.

Coming back to the bike after a long break gives you something that riders who never stopped do not have: perspective. You know what it feels like to not ride. You know what that absence costs. You know how the weeks blur together when there is no Saturday morning route, no Tuesday evening spin, no rhythm that the bike gives a week.

That knowledge changes the riding. It makes the bad days tolerable because you know what no days feel like. It makes the good days sweeter because you earned them twice — once to build the fitness, and once to come back for it.

The bike in the garage is not a reminder of who you used to be. It is a door. And the person on the other side of it is not the person who stopped riding. It is someone new, with all the old muscle memory and none of the old baggage.

Pump up the tyres. Wipe down the frame. Clip in.

You are not starting from zero. You are starting from experience. And that, it turns out, is the best place to start from.

You have done the work. Months of it. The early mornings, the intervals that made you question your life choices, the rides where the weather was terrible and you went anyway. All of that is banked. The question now is whether you will let it pay out.

Most amateur cyclists sabotage their target event in the final two weeks. Not by doing too little. By doing too much. Or by doing the wrong kind of less.

I covered the general principles of tapering — the science of supercompensation, volume reduction, intensity maintenance — in our [tapering guide](/blog/cycling-tapering-guide). This piece is different. This is about what your last 14 days actually look like based on the specific event you are targeting. Because a road race taper and a sportive taper are not the same thing. Neither is a time trial taper or a multi-day stage race taper. The demands are different. The protocols should be too.

## Why the Taper Is Where Amateur Events Are Won and Lost

Here is what happens in most club cyclists' final fortnight before a target event. They look at the calendar. They count the days. They panic.

The internal monologue goes something like this: *I haven't done enough. I should have done more long rides. My FTP hasn't moved in three weeks. Maybe one more hard session will make the difference.*

It will not make the difference. Or rather, it will — in the wrong direction.

Inigo Mujika, the Basque exercise physiologist who has spent three decades studying the taper in endurance athletes, puts it bluntly: the taper is the highest-return phase of the entire training cycle. His research across runners, swimmers, and cyclists consistently shows performance improvements of 2-3% from a well-executed taper. For a cyclist holding 250 watts on a 60-minute effort, that is 5-8 extra watts on race day. For free. No extra training required. Just the discipline to do less.

The problem is that doing less feels like losing. Every rest day in the final week feels like fitness draining out of your legs. Every short spin feels like a wasted opportunity. This is the central paradox of the taper: the thing that makes you faster is the thing that feels most like giving up.

It is not giving up. It is cashing in.

## The Mujika Framework: Exponential vs Linear Taper

Before we get into event-specific protocols, you need to understand the two models Mujika's research identified.

**The linear taper** reduces training load at a constant rate. You might drop 10% of your volume each day across a two-week period. Steady, predictable, easy to follow. Also suboptimal.

**The exponential taper** front-loads the reduction. You make larger cuts early and smaller cuts as race day approaches. Think of it as a steep drop in the first 4-5 days, then a levelling off where volume stays low but stable through race week.

Mujika's meta-analyses favour the exponential model. The reasoning is physiological: your body begins recovery processes as soon as the training stimulus drops, and the faster you remove the fatigue signal, the sooner supercompensation kicks in. A linear taper delays this. An exponential taper gets ahead of it.

In practical terms, this means your first two or three days of taper should feel dramatic. A sudden, noticeable drop. Not a gentle easing off. That uncomfortable feeling of doing far less than you are used to is the point.

The research also confirms three non-negotiables across all taper types:

1. **Volume must decrease** — by 40-60% for most events
2. **Intensity must be maintained** — short efforts at race-specific intensities
3. **Frequency should stay similar** — ride most days, just ride less

Those principles hold across every event type. What changes is the duration of the taper, the specific intensities you preserve, and how you structure race week itself.

## Event-Specific Taper Protocols

### Road Race Taper: Short, Sharp, Neuromuscular

A road race is an explosive, unpredictable event. Attacks. Surges. Corners taken at speed. A sprint finish you may not have planned for but suddenly find yourself in. The demands are anaerobic capacity, repeated high-power efforts, and the ability to respond instantly to changes in pace.

Your taper needs to protect that snap. Rest too long and your legs lose their punch.

**Taper duration:** 7-10 days

**Volume reduction:** 40-50% (less aggressive than a sportive taper)

**Key intensity to preserve:** Short, high-power efforts — 30-second to 2-minute accelerations at 120-150% FTP

**Days 10-8 (Phase 1 — the drop):**
- Reduce daily ride time by half immediately
- Keep one session of 6-8 x 30-second efforts at maximum sustainable power with full recovery between
- All other rides are Zone 2, 45-60 minutes maximum

**Days 7-4 (Phase 2 — maintain sharpness):**
- Volume stays at 50% of normal
- One session of race-simulation intervals: 3 x (2 minutes hard, 1 minute easy, 30 seconds maximum) — mimicking attack, recover, respond
- One easy spin of 40 minutes with 4 x 10-second sprints from a rolling start
- One rest day

**Days 3-1 (Phase 3 — openers and rest):**
- Day 3: 30-minute easy ride with 3 x 1-minute efforts at threshold, 2 x 10-second seated sprints
- Day 2: 20-minute easy spin or complete rest
- Day 1 (race day): Warm up 20 minutes with progressive effort, include two 30-second race-pace surges in the final 5 minutes before start

The critical difference here is that you are preserving neuromuscular recruitment patterns right up to 48 hours before the race. Road racing requires your fast-twitch fibres to fire on demand. Let them go quiet for three or four days and they take time to wake back up.

### Sportive and Gran Fondo Taper: Long, Deep, Fuel-Focused

A sportive is a different animal entirely. You are not sprinting for a finish line. You are managing effort over four, five, six hours or more. The limiters are glycogen depletion, accumulated muscular fatigue, and mental endurance.

Your taper needs to prioritise full recovery from training load and maximum fuel storage. Sharpness matters far less than depth.

**Taper duration:** 14 days (the full Mujika protocol)

**Volume reduction:** 50-60%

**Key intensity to preserve:** Threshold and tempo efforts (Zone 3-4) that mirror your target race pace

**Week 2 out (Days 14-8):**
- Drop volume to 60% immediately
- One quality session: 3 x 10 minutes at your target sportive power (likely 70-80% FTP), with 3-minute recoveries
- One endurance ride of 90 minutes at easy pace — this is your last long ride, and it should feel comfortable, not challenging
- Two easy spins of 45-60 minutes
- Two rest days

**Race week (Days 7-1):**
- Day 7: 60 minutes easy with 2 x 5 minutes at target sportive power
- Day 6: 45 minutes easy Zone 2
- Day 5: Rest
- Day 4: 40 minutes easy with 3 x 2 minutes at threshold — these are your openers, not a workout
- Day 3: 30 minutes very easy or rest
- Day 2: 20-minute spin, legs loose, nothing above Zone 2
- Day 1 (race day): Short warm-up, 15-20 minutes progressive, start conservative

Notice the difference from the road race taper. No sprints. No maximal efforts. The intensity you preserve is sustained, moderate-to-hard power — the exact effort you will hold for hours on race day. You are rehearsing the engine speed, not the acceleration.

The other critical element for sportive tapers is carbohydrate loading. From day 3 onwards, increase carbohydrate intake to 8-10 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 75kg rider, that is 600-750g of carbohydrates daily. Rice, pasta, bread, sports drinks, fruit. This is not the time for dietary restraint. Your muscles store approximately 500g of glycogen when fully loaded, and every gram is fuel you will need after hour three.

### Time Trial Taper: Precision Above Everything

The time trial is the most controlled event in cycling. No tactics. No drafting. No responding to attacks. Just you, the road, and a clock. The taper reflects that precision.

**Taper duration:** 10-12 days

**Volume reduction:** 40-50%

**Key intensity to preserve:** Sustained threshold power (your target race wattage) and position-specific efforts

**Days 12-8:**
- Drop volume sharply
- One session of 2 x 10 minutes at target race power in your TT position — this is about neuromuscular patterning in the aero position, not fitness
- One easy ride of 45-60 minutes
- Rest days as needed

**Days 7-4:**
- Day 7: Course familiarisation ride if possible — ride the course at 70% effort, noting corners, surface changes, wind exposure
- Day 6: 40 minutes easy with 1 x 5 minutes at target power in race position
- Day 5: Rest
- Day 4: Final aero check — 20 minutes in race position on the turbo, confirming everything feels right. This is not training. This is systems verification.

**Days 3-1:**
- Day 3: 20 minutes easy with 2 x 3 minutes at target race power — your final dress rehearsal
- Day 2: 15 minutes easy spin or rest
- Day 1 (race day): 20-minute warm-up on the turbo with progressive build, two 90-second efforts at race power in the final 5 minutes

Time trial tapers have two unique elements that other events do not require.

First, **course familiarisation**. If you can ride the course in the week before the event, do it. Not at race pace — at conversation pace. You are mapping corners, surface quality, gradient changes, and wind patterns into your brain so that on race day your pacing is reactive, not guesswork.

Second, **position time**. The aerodynamic position you hold in a TT is extreme. If you have not spent time in it recently, your body will fight you on race day — your back will seize, your hip flexors will cramp, your power will drop. Every opener session in the taper should be done in your race position on your race bike.

### Multi-Day Event Taper: Fresh but Resilient

Events like the Haute Route, Mallorca 312, or a multi-day tour require a different calculus. You need to be fresh for day one, but you also need the metabolic resilience to absorb repeated efforts across three, five, or seven days. Taper too aggressively and you will fly on day one and collapse on day three.

**Taper duration:** 10-14 days

**Volume reduction:** 30-40% (gentler than single-day events)

**Key intensity to preserve:** Tempo and threshold efforts, plus some muscular endurance work

**Week 2 out (Days 14-8):**
- Drop volume to 65-70% of your peak training week
- One session of 2 x 15 minutes at tempo (75-85% FTP) — this maintains the metabolic machinery you will need for sustained daily efforts
- One endurance ride of 2 hours at easy pace — your last properly long ride
- Easy spins and rest days to fill the week

**Race week (Days 7-1):**
- Day 7: 75 minutes easy with 2 x 8 minutes at tempo
- Day 6: 45 minutes easy
- Day 5: Rest
- Day 4: 50 minutes with 3 x 3 minutes at threshold — sharper than the sportive protocol because day one of a multi-day event often features a selective stage
- Day 3: 30 minutes easy
- Day 2: Travel day — if possible, a 20-minute spin on a turbo to keep legs moving
- Day 1 (event day 1): Conservative start, negative split the first stage

The psychological challenge with multi-day tapers is trust. You know that day four is going to hurt regardless of how well you taper. The temptation is to keep training volume higher "so I'm ready for the suffering." But Mujika's research is clear: the athletes who taper properly for multi-day events not only perform better on day one, they also recover faster between stages. Freshness compounds. Fatigue compounds faster.

Begin carbohydrate loading 72 hours before the event starts and maintain high carbohydrate intake throughout the event — 8-10g per kilogram daily, with particular attention to recovery nutrition within 30 minutes of finishing each stage.

## The Psychology of Tapering: Phantom Fatigue and the Urge to Test Yourself

This is the part nobody warns you about.

Three to five days into a taper, you will feel terrible. Heavy legs. Low energy. A creeping conviction that you have lost all your fitness and the event is going to be a disaster. You will look at your training log and see the drop in volume and interpret it as decline. You will think about going for a hard ride "just to check everything still works."

Do not do this.

What you are experiencing has a name in the research literature: phantom fatigue. Mujika and others have documented it extensively. When your body shifts from a high-stress training state into recovery, several things happen simultaneously:

- **Inflammation temporarily increases** as repair processes accelerate. Your immune system, held in check by training stress, ramps up activity. This creates systemic feelings of heaviness and lethargy.
- **Sleep patterns shift.** Without the physical exhaustion of hard training, you may sleep lighter, wake earlier, or feel unrested despite adequate hours. Your circadian rhythm is recalibrating.
- **Mood drops.** The endorphin hit from hard training disappears. Without that chemical reward, motivation wanes and anxiety fills the gap. You start catastrophising about fitness.
- **Perceived exertion disconnects from actual output.** A Zone 2 ride feels harder than it should. Your power numbers might even be slightly lower. This is temporary neuromuscular recalibration, not fitness loss.

Here is the good news: the athletes who report the worst phantom fatigue symptoms during taper consistently produce their best performances on race day. The depth of the recovery response is proportional to the quality of the taper. Feeling bad means it is working.

The hardest discipline in all of cycling is not the five-hour ride in the rain. It is sitting on the sofa on a Wednesday afternoon during taper week, watching other people's Strava rides, and trusting the process.

Two strategies that help:

**Remove the temptation.** Schedule non-cycling activities during taper week. Walk. Read. Do light mobility work. Fill the hours you would normally spend training with things that are actually restful, not things that are training by another name.

**Look at the data, not the feelings.** If you train with power, check your normalised power during opener efforts. It will be the same or higher than your pre-taper numbers. Your fitness has not gone anywhere. Your fatigue has. That is the entire point.

## Race Week Day-by-Day: The Universal Protocol

Regardless of event type, race week follows a similar rhythm. The specific intensities change (as outlined above for each event type), but the structure is consistent.

### Monday — 7 Days Out

Your last session with any real substance. This is not a hard session — it is a reminder session. 45-60 minutes total, with 10-15 minutes of effort at your event-specific intensity embedded in the middle. Afterwards, you should feel like you could easily do more. That restraint is the point.

### Tuesday — 6 Days Out

Easy spin. 30-45 minutes, purely Zone 1-2. No efforts. No Strava segments. No "accidentally" going hard on the climb near your house. This ride exists to keep blood flowing through your legs and nothing more.

### Wednesday — 5 Days Out

Rest. Complete rest. Not active recovery. Not yoga. Not a walk that turns into a hike. Rest.

If you cannot sit still, do 15 minutes of gentle mobility work — hip flexor stretches, thoracic spine rotations, ankle circles. Nothing that elevates your heart rate.

### Thursday — 4 Days Out

Openers. This is the most important session of the week. 30-40 minutes total, easy pace, with your event-specific opener efforts:

- **Road race:** 4 x 30 seconds at sprint power, full recovery
- **Sportive:** 2 x 5 minutes at target event power
- **Time trial:** 2 x 3 minutes at race power in race position
- **Multi-day:** 3 x 3 minutes at threshold

These openers prime the neuromuscular system. They remind your fast-twitch fibres that they exist. They give you the confidence that your legs still work. Total hard effort across all openers: 6-10 minutes maximum.

### Friday — 3 Days Out

Easy spin or rest. If you ride, 20-25 minutes maximum, entirely Zone 1. Some riders prefer complete rest. Both approaches work. Choose whichever leaves you feeling calmer.

Begin aggressive carbohydrate loading if your event is endurance-focused (sportive, gran fondo, multi-day). Increase carbohydrate intake to 8-10g per kilogram of body weight.

### Saturday — 2 Days Out (assuming Sunday race)

If you ride at all, it is 15-20 minutes with a single short effort: one 60-90 second surge at race intensity. That is it. The purpose is purely psychological — a final confirmation that your legs are ready.

Many experienced riders take this day as complete rest. Both approaches produce identical outcomes in the research. Do whatever makes you less anxious.

Eat well. Sleep well. Lay out your kit. Check your bike — tyre pressures, chain lube, gear cables, brakes. Do not leave mechanical checks to race morning.

### Sunday — Race Day

Wake 3-4 hours before your start time. Eat a familiar breakfast — whatever you have tested in training, typically 2-3g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight. Coffee if that is your routine.

Arrive at the venue with time to spare. Warm up 15-25 minutes, building from very easy to event-specific intensity in the final 5 minutes. Include two short surges at race power.

Then race the event you have trained for. The taper is complete. The work is done. Now you get to use it.

## What to Do If You Feel Flat During Taper

You have followed the protocol. It is Thursday of race week. You did your openers. Your legs felt like concrete. Your power was 15 watts below normal. You are now convinced the taper has ruined your form and you should go out for a hard ride tomorrow to "shake things off."

Stop.

This is the most common taper scenario, and it resolves itself. Here is why.

The power drop you see during mid-taper openers is real, but it is temporary. Your muscles are in the final phase of glycogen replenishment and tissue repair. Blood flow patterns have shifted. Your neuromuscular recruitment patterns are recalibrating after weeks of accumulated fatigue. Mujika's longitudinal data shows that time-trial performance in tapered athletes typically dips 1-2% between days 4 and 6 of the taper, then jumps 3-5% above baseline by race day.

Read that again. You get worse before you get dramatically better.

If you feel flat during taper:

1. **Do not add training.** This is the single most important instruction. Adding a hard session 48-72 hours before your event will not improve your fitness — fitness adaptations take 10-14 days to manifest. It will only add fatigue.

2. **Check your sleep.** Taper-related sleep disruption is common. If you are sleeping poorly, address it directly — magnesium before bed, no screens after 9pm, a cool dark room. Do not try to tire yourself out with extra riding.

3. **Eat more carbohydrates.** Flat feelings during taper are often a glycogen issue. Your muscles are trying to supercompensate and they need fuel to do it. Increase carbohydrate intake by 50-100g per day.

4. **Trust the timeline.** Flat feelings peak 3-5 days before the event and resolve 1-2 days out. By Saturday morning you will feel different. By race morning you will feel transformed.

5. **Remember the data.** Mujika's research across hundreds of athletes shows that subjective feelings during taper do not predict race performance. The riders who felt worst during taper performed statistically identically to those who felt great. Your feelings are noise. The physiology is signal.

## When NOT to Taper

Not every event deserves a taper. Tapering is a tool, and like any tool, using it at the wrong time costs more than it gains.

**B-races and training races.** If the event is a stepping stone, not a destination, do not taper for it. Ride it on your current training load. You might not produce your absolute best performance, but you will not lose two weeks of training in a period where those weeks matter for your A-race. Race on tired legs. Use it as a dress rehearsal for nutrition, pacing, and mental strategies.

**Events used for preparation.** If you are doing a 100km sportive as preparation for a 200km event eight weeks later, the sportive is training, not racing. Ride it hard. Suffer through it. Learn from it. But do not taper for it.

**Events within 4 weeks of each other.** If you have two target events in a month, you cannot taper fully for both. Choose one as your A-race and give it the full protocol. The other gets a 3-5 day mini-taper at most — a slight volume reduction and a set of openers. Trying to taper twice in four weeks creates a month-long hole in your training that you cannot recover from.

**Early-season races.** If you are still building fitness in February or March, tapering for an early-season event makes no sense. You would be peaking prematurely with an incomplete fitness base. Race these events as training efforts and save the taper for when it matters — for the event you have circled on the calendar since January.

The discipline is knowing the difference between an event that matters and an event that serves. Taper for the former. Train through the latter. Your season will be better for it.

---

The taper is the final exam you cannot cram for. The work was done weeks ago. All that remains is the courage to let it arrive. Reduce the volume. Keep the sharpness. Trust the science. Trust yourself.

And when you are sitting on the sofa three days before your event, legs twitching, brain screaming at you to go ride — remember that this discomfort is the last and most important part of your preparation. The riders who can tolerate doing less are the riders who perform more.

Race day is coming. You are ready. The taper is proof of that, not evidence against it.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about the Peak District 100: the climbing total looks moderate. 2,500 to 3,000 metres across 100 miles. That is less than an [Etape du Tour](/blog/etape-du-tour-training-plan) or a [Marmotte](/blog/la-marmotte-training-guide). On paper, it sits in the category of hard-but-manageable UK sportive. What the numbers fail to communicate is how those metres are distributed.

There is no single defining climb. No twenty-minute Alpine drag where you find a rhythm and grind it out. Instead there are thirty to forty short, steep efforts — many at 10 to 20 per cent gradient — that arrive every few minutes and never stop arriving. Up, down, up, down, for six to eight hours. The descents are too short to recover on. The flats barely exist. By mile 70, every lane that tilts upward feels like it was put there specifically to ruin you.

One hundred miles through the Peak District National Park. Start area around Bakewell or Matlock. Approximately 2,500-3,000m of climbing. Thirty-plus climbs, some reaching 20 per cent. Moorland roads at 300-600m altitude where the wind has nothing between it and your face. British weather at its most variable. Entry fee approximately £40-60. Typical finish time 6-8 hours for well-prepared riders, longer for those who underestimate the terrain.

The good news: it is fixable. The specific fitness this event demands is trainable in 12 weeks, even if you are starting from a base of moderate riding. The key is knowing what to train — and it is not what most people assume.

## Key Takeaways

- The Peak District 100 covers 100 miles (160km) with 2,500-3,000m of climbing across 30-40 short, steep efforts rather than sustained mountain passes
- Repeatability matters more than raw FTP — produce 3-5 minute efforts at 90-100 per cent FTP, recover briefly, repeat thirty times
- Target 2.5-3.0 W/kg for a comfortable finish, but five-minute power relative to FTP is a better success predictor than FTP alone
- British weather preparation is non-negotiable — exposed moorland at 300-600m altitude in conditions that change by the hour
- A 12-week build to 10-12 hours per week with at least one weekly session of repeated short climbs
- Fuel 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour — the constant up-and-down and cold conditions suppress appetite while burning more than flat miles
- Descending skill matters — narrow, wet, technical descents arrive dozens of times across the day

## Course Profile and Key Sections

The Peak District 100 routes out of the Bakewell or Matlock area and loops through the national park, taking in limestone dales, gritstone edges, and exposed moorland plateaux. The character is relentlessly undulating. Let me break this down by section.

### The Opening Miles: Out of Bakewell

The route rolls out through the Derbyshire dales on relatively kind roads. Relatively. You are in the Peak District, so the gradient changes from the first mile, but the early climbs are shorter and shallower than what waits ahead. This is the trap. The first twenty miles feel almost pleasant — rolling countryside, stone walls, sheep, the kind of riding that makes you wonder what everyone was warning you about.

Store that feeling somewhere safe. You will want to remember it at mile 80. This is the section to settle into your pacing, eat your first feed, and resist the urge to push the early climbs. Everyone around you will be riding too hard. Let them.

### The Moorland Sections

Here's where it gets really interesting. The route climbs onto the high moorland — the Dark Peak and the exposed gritstone edges — and the character shifts entirely. The temperature drops. The wind picks up. The views open out across empty moor and the road stretches ahead with nothing to hide behind.

Moorland riding in the Peak District is a specific discipline. The roads sit at 400-600m altitude on exposed plateaux. A south-westerly that was a gentle push in the Derwent valley becomes a crosswind across open moor that adds twenty watts to every pedal stroke. The road surface is rougher up here — cracks, standing water, patches where the tarmac has given up.

The climbs onto and across the moorland are not the steepest on the course, but they are the most relentless. Gradual drags of 3-5 per cent that feel trivial individually but accumulate across miles of exposed road. If there is rain, this is where you feel it. If there is wind, this is where you fight it.

### The Steep Valley Climbs: Winnats and Company

The signature efforts on the Peak District 100 are the valley climbs — short, steep, and violent. Winnats Pass is the headline act: barely a kilometre long but ramping to 20 per cent through a limestone gorge that funnels the wind and removes any illusion that you are on a gentle British sportive. Mam Nick climbs out of the Hope Valley at 10-15 per cent. Curbar Edge rises from the Derwent valley on exposed gritstone roads. Monsal Head kicks sharply out of the dale.

None of these climbs last longer than five minutes. Most are over in two or three. That is precisely the problem. They are short enough that you feel you should attack them. They are steep enough that attacking them costs more than you can afford thirty times across 100 miles.

The discipline is riding these climbs at a controlled effort — seated where possible, out of the saddle only when the gradient demands it. The rider who attacks Winnats at mile 30 and produces 120 per cent of FTP for three minutes pays for it across every climb from mile 60 onwards.

### The Exposed Hilltops

Between the valley climbs, the route crosses exposed hilltops and gritstone edges. The gradient is gentle but the wind and altitude make them deceptively hard. Riding across Curbar Edge or along Long Hill in a headwind after 70 miles of climbing is a different kind of effort. Not steep. Just unrelenting. The wind chill in rain can drop below 5 degrees even in summer — use the [Wind Chill Calculator](/tools/wind-chill) to see what exposed moorland at speed actually feels like.

### The Final 20 Miles: When Every Rise is a Wall

The final section threads back through the dales on roads that are objectively not hard. Nothing that would trouble fresh legs. But at mile 80, every short rise that tilts the road at 5 per cent feels like 15 per cent. Every false flat into a headwind feels like a climb. The psychological toll of the Peak District 100 lives here: in the final third, when the climbs are objectively small but subjectively enormous.

Riders who paced the first 60 miles conservatively and fuelled consistently ride this section hard but controlled. Riders who went into the red on every early climb are in survival mode.

Let me be really clear about this: the numbers that matter most for the Peak District are not raw FTP. A rider at 2.8 W/kg who can repeat three-minute efforts at 95 per cent FTP thirty-five times will ride this event better than a rider at 3.2 W/kg whose power drops 20 per cent after the twentieth effort. Train the repeatability, not just the peak number.

## 12-Week Training Plan Overview

### Phase 1: Aerobic Foundation (Weeks 1-3) — 7-9 hrs/wk

Four to five rides per week, 80 per cent in [Zone 2](/blog/zone-2-cycling-heart-rate-vs-power-vs-rpe). Easy stays easy. [Stephen Seiler](/guests/stephen-seiler)'s polarised principle is the framework: go easy enough on easy days that you can go properly hard on hard days.

**Anchor session:** Long ride building from 2.5 hours to 3.5 hours over the block, on the hilliest terrain you can find. **Mid-week session:** 60-90 minutes with 4-6 short hill efforts of 2 minutes at 85 per cent FTP. Not hard yet — just introducing the pattern.

**Core stability:** Twice per week, 20 minutes. Planks, dead bugs, side planks, single-leg glute bridges, hip mobility. Peak District descents on tired legs demand a stable torso. This is not optional.

### Phase 2: Punch and Repeatability (Weeks 4-7) — 9-11 hrs/wk

This is the phase that makes or breaks your Peak District 100. The volume increases, but the specific climbing-repeatability work begins.

**Key session 1: Short hill repetitions.** Find a hill at 10-15 per cent that takes 2-3 minutes to climb. Ride it 8 times in week 4, building to 12 times by week 7, at 90-100 per cent FTP. Descent recovery between each rep. The gradient matters — flat intervals at the same wattage do not prepare the neuromuscular system for what the Peak District does. If you only have a turbo, set the gradient to 12 per cent and ride seated.

**Key session 2: Sustained rolling tempo.** 90 minutes to 2 hours on the hilliest route available. Hold 75-85 per cent FTP across the entire ride. No surging on climbs, no coasting on descents. If your power spikes 30 per cent on every rise, you are doing what most people do, not what actually works.

**Long ride:** Building from 3.5 hours to 5 hours over the four weeks, always on hilly terrain. Practise eating on every ride.

Core stability continues twice per week. Add hip flexor stretches and single-leg balance work.

### Phase 3: Event Simulation (Weeks 8-10) — 10-12 hrs/wk

Peak volume and peak specificity.

**Key session 1: Extended hill repetitions.** Same steep hill, but now 10-15 reps of 2-3 minutes at 90-100 per cent FTP. By week 10 this session should feel hard but manageable. If it feels desperate, the volume in the rest of the week is too high.

**Key session 2: Course simulation.** A 3-4 hour ride on rolling terrain at your target Peak District pace. Include 8-10 short climbs at race-day effort. Full fuelling rehearsal.

**Long ride:** 5-6 hours with 1,200-1,500m of climbing. If you can travel to the Peak District, the Pennines, or the Yorkshire Dales and ride truly steep lanes, do it. One ride on that terrain teaches more about pacing than five rides on a smooth hill. If you can fit a back-to-back weekend — 5-hour Saturday, 2-hour easy Sunday — the Sunday teaches your legs what mile 80 feels like.

### Phase 4: Taper (Weeks 11-12)

Volume drops 40-50 per cent. The fitness is in. The work now is rest, sharpening, and logistics.

**Week 11:** Two quality sessions — short hill repetitions (6 reps, sharp and controlled) and a 90-minute rolling ride at target race pace. Total volume 6-7 hours. One core session.

**Week 12:** Two easy spins of 60-75 minutes with 2-3 short openers at 95 per cent FTP. Total volume 3-4 hours. Travel to the Peak District early if possible. Pre-ride 15-20 miles of local roads to feel the gradient character and road surface. Eat well. Sleep well. Lay out kit.

## Key Sessions

**The Punchy Repeats.** 15-minute warm-up. Then 10x2 minutes at 95-100 per cent FTP on a gradient of 10-15 per cent, descending between each rep. Recovery is whatever the descent gives you — typically 2-3 minutes. The gradient matters. Flat intervals at the same wattage train the engine but miss the muscular cost of steep climbing. Once per week from week 4 onwards. This single session, done consistently for eight weeks, is the most specific preparation you can do for the Peak District.

**Rolling Endurance.** 3-4 hours on the hilliest route available. Hold 70-80 per cent FTP throughout — no surging, no coasting. Eat 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour, practising exactly what you will use on race day. This session builds the durability that carries you from mile 60 to mile 100.

**The Wet-Weather Ride.** Pick a day when the forecast is cold, wet, or windy. Ride 2-3 hours in it. Deliberately. Wear the kit you plan to race in. Descend on wet roads at a pace that teaches you where the grip limit is. You need to know that your waterproof works, your gloves grip wet brake levers, and your mind does not switch off when the rain starts at mile 40.

## Nutrition Strategy

The Peak District 100 burns roughly 4,000-6,000 calories across 6-8 hours. Cold, wet conditions increase calorie burn while suppressing appetite. This combination is the biggest fuelling risk of the event.

The target: 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour from the first hour to the last. Use the [Fuel Planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to map your specific needs. A 2:1 glucose-fructose mix — backed by Asker Jeukendrup's research — maximises gut absorption.

**Practise eating on climbs.** The Peak District does not provide flat recovery sections. You are either climbing, descending, or fighting a crosswind. Train yourself to eat while riding at gradient. Awkward at first. Automatic by week 8.

**Cold-weather appetite suppression.** Here's the thing nobody tells you about fuelling in British sportives: when it is 8 degrees and raining, you do not feel hungry. Your body still needs the calories. Eat on a timer — every 20-25 minutes, regardless of appetite. By the time you feel hungry in cold conditions, you are already behind.

**Hydration.** 500-750ml per hour. Even in cold weather, you are sweating under the waterproof. Electrolytes in every bottle. The [Hydration Calculator](/tools/hydration) gives you a personalised starting point.

**Pre-event.** 8-10g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight across the 24 hours before. Breakfast 2-3 hours before the start: porridge with banana and honey, toast with jam, or whatever your gut handles at five in the morning. A caffeine gel at mile 60-70 sharpens focus for the final third — but only if you use caffeine regularly. The Peak District is not the place to experiment.

## Equipment and Gear

**Gearing.** Compact chainset (50/34) with an 11-32 cassette minimum. An 11-34 is better. Winnats Pass at 20 per cent after 30 miles of climbing will humble any gear that felt adequate on the turbo. Test your gearing on a 15 per cent gradient at the end of a long ride — not fresh. If you are grinding below 55 RPM, fit a bigger cassette. This is the most fixable mistake on this list.

**Tyres.** 28mm minimum, 30-32mm if your frame clears them. Peak District lanes include rough surfaces, cattle grids, loose gravel, and potholes that appear mid-descent behind dry stone walls. Wider tyres at lower pressure provide significantly more comfort and grip. Tubeless with sealant is ideal for puncture resilience.

**Waterproofs and layering.** A proper waterproof jacket in the jersey pocket regardless of the forecast. Arm warmers from the start. Full-finger gloves packed and accessible. A base layer that still insulates when wet. The moorland wind chill in rain can drop below 5 degrees Celsius in summer — use the [Wind Chill Calculator](/tools/wind-chill) to check. Riders who pack for sunshine and meet rain are the ones shivering at feed stations.

**Brakes.** Disc brakes are a genuine advantage. Dozens of short descents on wet, narrow roads means dozens of braking efforts. Rim brakes in the wet lose stopping power when you need it most.

**Repair kit.** Two inner tubes or plug kit, CO2 or mini pump, tyre levers, multi-tool, chain quick link. Peak District lanes are remote and mobile signal is patchy on the moorland.

## Race Day Pacing

The single most important sentence in this guide: do not attack Peak District hills.

What most people do is surge on every short climb because the gradient provokes a response and the efforts are short enough to feel recoverable. What actually works is riding every climb at a sustainable, repeatable effort from mile 1 to mile 100.

**First 30 miles.** Ride 5-10 per cent below your target effort. The legs feel too good. The climbs feel too short to worry about. That is the point. Every watt you save here, you spend in the final third. Use the first feed station. Eat. Drink. Do not ride through because you feel strong.

**Miles 30-70.** Settle into your target effort. The steep valley climbs live in this section. Ride them firmly but controlled. Seated where possible. Watch your heart rate — if you are spending more than 90 seconds above threshold on a two-minute climb, you are going too hard for what awaits.

**Miles 70-100.** Ride what you have. Every short rise will feel steeper than it is. That is fatigue lying to you. Hold your form, hold your cadence, keep eating. On descents: brake early, sit upright, respect the narrow lanes. A crash at mile 85 ruins a day that was going well.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Attacking every climb.** The first Peak District climb takes ninety seconds and feels trivial. The fifth feels easy. The fifteenth still feels manageable. The thirtieth does not. Riders who surge on every gradient produce a normalised power that looks impressive and a finishing time that does not. Ride within yourself from the gun.

**2. Wrong gears for the gradient.** A 34x28 bottom gear is not enough for Winnats Pass on tired legs. Fit a 32 or 34-tooth cassette. This costs less than fifty pounds and saves more energy than any supplement or piece of kit on the market.

**3. Packing for the forecast, not the moorland.** The forecast says 18 degrees and partly cloudy. The moorland at 500m altitude says 10 degrees and driving rain. A waterproof that stays in your pocket all day is insurance. A waterproof sitting at home when the rain starts at mile 40 is a mistake that defines the rest of your ride.

**4. Under-fuelling in cold weather.** Cold suppresses appetite. The effort is still there. The calorie burn is still there. Riders who eat based on hunger in cold Peak District weather arrive at mile 70 with a glycogen deficit that no amount of gels can fix in time. Eat on a timer. Every 20-25 minutes. No exceptions.

**5. Ignoring the descents.** Peak District descents are narrow, sometimes wet, sometimes loose, and they arrive dozens of times. Tired legs at mile 80 make errors that fresh legs would never make. Brake earlier than you think you need to. Scrub speed before the corner, not in it.

## Travel and Logistics

**Getting there.** The Peak District sits in the centre of England. From Manchester, 50 minutes. From Sheffield, 30 minutes. From Birmingham, 90 minutes. From London, 3-3.5 hours via the M1. Nearest train stations are Matlock and Buxton.

**Accommodation.** Bakewell is the most convenient base — a market town with pubs, B&Bs, and a few hotels. Book early; sportive weekends fill the town. Matlock (20 minutes south) and Buxton (20 minutes north) offer more options. Sheffield and Chesterfield are within 30-40 minutes if everything local is booked.

**Warm-up ride.** Arrive a day early and ride 15-20 miles of local lanes. Not the course — just local roads to feel the gradient character and calibrate your gearing expectations.

**Entry.** Approximately £40-60. Enter early — UK sportive places fill months ahead. Most Peak District sportives offer shorter route options at 50-70 miles.

**On the day.** Arrive early. Eat breakfast with time to spare. The start is usually rolling, not timed — but getting out in the first wave avoids congestion on narrow lanes.

## Recovery Plan

**Day 1 (race day evening).** Protein and carbohydrate within 30 minutes of finishing. Hydrate. Stretch gently. Sleep.

**Days 2-3.** Complete rest or a 30-minute easy spin. No climbing. No intensity. Expect the legs to feel worse on day 2 — delayed onset soreness from the repeated eccentric loading on all those descents.

**Days 4-5.** Easy riding, 45-60 minutes, flat terrain, Zone 1-2. Blood flow, not fitness.

**Days 6-7.** Normal easy riding resumes. Full structured training resumes in week 2. The specific fatigue from thirty-plus short steep efforts needs 7-10 days to clear fully.

## What Comes Next

The Peak District 100 builds exactly the fitness profile that transfers to bigger UK events. The [Dragon Ride](/blog/dragon-ride-training-guide) adds longer sustained climbs and more total distance. The [Tour of Wessex](/blog/tour-of-wessex-training-guide) extends the challenge across multiple days. If you finish the Peak District 100 well — controlled, fuelled, with something left in the tank — those events are realistic targets.

For riders with European ambitions, the repeatability you build for the Peak District is precisely what the [Nove Colli](/blog/nove-colli-training-guide) and the [Amstel Gold Sportive](/blog/amstel-gold-race-sportive-training-guide) demand. The terrain is different. The fitness transfers.

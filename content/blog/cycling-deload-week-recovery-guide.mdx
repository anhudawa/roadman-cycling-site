You have been doing everything right. Three weeks of structured intervals, progressive overload, sessions that left you staring at the ceiling wondering whether the fourth rep was actually necessary. Your CTL is climbing. Your training log looks like a monument to discipline. And now, right when the numbers say you should be getting stronger, you feel worse than you did at the start of the block.

Power at threshold has drifted down three to five watts. Sleep is restless despite being properly tired. The legs that felt springy in week one now feel like they belong to someone ten years older. Your mood is off — shorter temper, less patience on the bike and off it.

Most riders respond to this by doing more. One extra interval set. A longer weekend ride. Pushing through because the plan says so and quitting feels like weakness.

That response is precisely backwards. The training you have done is sitting in your body as accumulated stress. It has not become fitness yet. It will not become fitness until you give your system the time and resources to convert it. That conversion process has a name. It is called supercompensation. And it only happens when you back off.

## The Supercompensation Model — What Training Actually Does to You

Training does not make you fitter. Training makes you worse. That sounds like provocation but it is basic exercise physiology, and every coach worth talking to will tell you the same thing.

When you ride hard intervals, you create stress. That stress damages muscle fibres, depletes glycogen, elevates cortisol, and accumulates general systemic fatigue. Immediately after a hard session, you are measurably less fit than you were before it. Your body is in a deficit.

The adaptation — the actual fitness gain — happens during recovery. Your body overshoots the repair. It rebuilds the damaged systems slightly stronger than they were before the stress was applied. Mitochondria multiply. Capillary networks expand. Enzyme concentrations shift. Muscle fibres that were broken down come back with marginally more capacity to handle the same load next time.

This overshoot is supercompensation. And it is not optional. It is the entire mechanism by which training works.

Stephen Seiler, who has probably studied training intensity distribution more rigorously than anyone alive, frames it simply: stress plus recovery equals adaptation. Remove either side and the equation collapses. Most amateurs obsess over the stress side — more intervals, more TSS, more suffering. They starve the recovery side. And then they wonder why the numbers stall.

A single night of sleep handles the recovery from a single session. But block-level fatigue — the kind that accumulates over three or four weeks of progressive overload — requires block-level recovery. That is what a deload week is. Not a reward. Not a holiday. A planned, deliberate reduction in training load that allows the cumulative stress of an entire training block to convert into measurable adaptation.

## Proactive Versus Reactive Deloading

There are two ways to arrive at a deload week. One is intelligent. The other means you waited too long.

**Proactive deloading** is planned before you need it. You build your training calendar in blocks: three weeks of progressive loading followed by one week of reduced stress. The deload is scheduled on day one, baked into the structure, non-negotiable. You take it whether you feel like you need it or not — because by the time you feel like you need it, you are already deeper in the hole than necessary.

**Reactive deloading** is what happens when you skip the proactive version. Your body starts sending signals — elevated resting heart rate, poor sleep quality despite exhaustion, declining power output at intensities you handled easily two weeks ago, irritability, persistent muscle soreness that does not clear within 48 hours. These are not signs that you need to push harder. These are signs that accumulated fatigue has exceeded your body's capacity to absorb it at the current rate. You are no longer adapting. You are digging.

Reactive deloads work. They pull you back from the edge. But they cost more time than proactive ones because you are recovering from a deeper hole. A planned deload after three weeks of loading might need five to seven days. A reactive deload triggered by genuine overreaching might need ten to fourteen days before you are producing quality sessions again.

Dan Lorang, who has coached both Tadej Pogacar and Jonas Vingegaard, programmes recovery into every single block for his professional riders. If the best athletes on the planet — riders with youth, genetics, pharmacological support, and nothing to do but train and recover — require structured deloads, you and I absolutely require them. The question is not whether to deload. The question is whether you are doing it before or after the damage is done.

## Why Masters Cyclists Need Deloads More Than Younger Riders

This is the part that nobody wants to hear but needs to be said plainly: recovery capacity declines with age. It is measurable. It is documented. And it affects your training structure whether you acknowledge it or not.

After 40, several physiological shifts make recovery slower and less complete:

**Hormonal decline.** Testosterone and growth hormone — both critical drivers of tissue repair and adaptation — decrease progressively from your mid-thirties onward. The raw materials your body uses to rebuild after training stress are less abundant. Same damage, fewer repair crews.

**Increased inflammatory response.** Older athletes produce higher levels of systemic inflammation in response to the same training stimulus. That inflammation is part of the adaptation signal, but excessive or prolonged inflammation becomes a barrier rather than a trigger. Clearing it takes longer.

**Sleep quality changes.** Deep sleep — the phase where the majority of muscular repair and hormonal release occurs — becomes harder to sustain as you age. Even if you are getting eight hours, the architecture of that sleep may deliver less recovery than it did a decade ago.

**Cumulative life stress.** A 25-year-old professional cyclist's non-training stress is minimal. Sleep, eat, train, massage, nap. A 45-year-old amateur with a demanding career, children, a mortgage, and an ageing parent is carrying a systemic stress load before the first pedal stroke. That stress is not separate from training stress. The body does not distinguish between a hard interval session and a brutal day at work. It all lands on the same recovery system.

Joe Friel has written extensively about this. His recommendation for masters cyclists over 50 is blunt: monitor your morning heart rate and HRV daily, and be prepared to extend a deload beyond seven days if those markers have not returned to baseline. The plan does not dictate your recovery. Your body does. The plan adapts to the body, not the other way around.

For most riders between 35 and 45, a three-weeks-on, one-week-off rhythm still works. Over 45, a two-weeks-on, one-week-off pattern often produces better long-term results. Less total training volume per month, but higher quality training within each block — and the fitness gains actually stick because the body has time to absorb them.

## How Much to Reduce — The 40 to 60 Percent Guideline

The most common question about deload weeks is the simplest one: how much less should I ride?

The working guideline is a 40 to 60 percent reduction in TSS from your normal training week. If you have been averaging 500 TSS per week during your build block, your deload week should land between 200 and 300 TSS. Not zero. Not 400. Somewhere in that window.

The reduction comes primarily from volume, not intensity. This distinction matters. Cutting volume means shorter rides, fewer sessions, or both. You are spending less time on the bike. But within those reduced sessions, you maintain a small amount of quality work — 20 to 30 minutes of tempo, a handful of 30-second openers, maybe one short sweet-spot effort — to keep the neuromuscular system sharp.

Think of it this way: your aerobic engine does not decay in seven days. VO2max, lactate threshold, fat oxidation — these adaptations are built over months and they do not vanish in a week. What does decay quickly is neuromuscular coordination. The smooth, high-power pedal stroke that comes from regular hard efforts starts to feel slightly off after five to seven days of complete rest. A couple of short, sharp efforts during the deload week prevent that without adding meaningful fatigue.

Stephen Seiler's framing is useful: the deload is not about training less. It is about recovering more. The rides you do are there to maintain touch, not to add load. If you finish a deload ride and feel like you trained, you went too hard.

## The Three Mistakes That Ruin a Deload

I have watched riders make the same three errors for years. All of them come from the same place — an inability to tolerate the feeling of doing less.

### Mistake One: Complete Rest

The instinct when you are tired is to stop entirely. Feet up, bike in the garage, nothing for seven days. And for properly overtrained athletes — riders who have been grinding for months without a break — total rest is sometimes appropriate. But for a planned deload after a normal three-week build, complete rest is counterproductive.

Within five to seven days of zero activity, neuromuscular fitness drops measurably. The body starts to downregulate the systems it is not using. Your first ride back feels clunky, heavy, disconnected from the pedals in a way that has nothing to do with aerobic fitness. It takes another three to five days of riding to restore what you lost by stopping.

Stay on the bike. Ride less. Ride easier. But ride.

### Mistake Two: Guilt Riding

This is the opposite problem, and it is far more common among the riders I talk to. Wednesday of your deload week arrives. The legs feel fresh for the first time in a month because — surprise — you reduced the load and the body started recovering. The freshness triggers a thought: "I feel good. I should ride hard. I am wasting this."

So you hammer Wednesday's ride. Maybe you hit a Strava segment. Maybe the group ride pulls you in. By Thursday, the legs are heavy again and the deload is effectively cancelled. You have re-loaded before the adaptation was complete.

The freshness you feel mid-deload is the point. It is evidence that recovery is working. Spending that freshness on a hard ride is like withdrawing money from a savings account the day before the interest posts. You get the momentary satisfaction and lose the compounding benefit.

### Mistake Three: Deloading the Wrong Things

Some riders reduce volume but keep every session at threshold. Others keep the volume but drop the intensity to zero. Neither approach is right.

The correct formula: reduce volume substantially, keep one or two sessions with brief intensity, and make everything else properly easy. A deload ride should feel almost boring. You should finish and think "that was not enough." That thought is confirmation that you did it correctly.

## Signs You Need a Reactive Deload Right Now

Proactive deloads are better. But if you have been ignoring the calendar and training through, your body will eventually demand what it is owed. Watch for these markers:

**Elevated resting heart rate.** If your morning resting HR is five or more beats above your normal baseline for three or more consecutive days, your autonomic nervous system is under strain. That is not a fitness signal. That is a stress signal.

**Poor sleep despite fatigue.** You are physically exhausted but lying awake, or waking at three in the morning with a racing mind. Elevated cortisol from accumulated training stress disrupts sleep architecture. The result is a vicious cycle: bad sleep impairs recovery, impaired recovery increases fatigue, increased fatigue elevates cortisol, elevated cortisol disrupts sleep.

**Declining power at threshold.** You used to hold 260 watts for 20 minutes comfortably. Now 250 feels like a maximum effort. Your ceiling has dropped not because you are less fit but because fatigue is masking the fitness you have built. The adaptation is in there. It is buried under accumulated stress.

**Irritability and mood changes.** Overreaching affects the central nervous system, not just the muscles. Shortened temper, reduced motivation, a general sense that everything is harder than it should be — on and off the bike. If your partner has started giving you a wider berth, take that as diagnostic data.

**Persistent muscle soreness beyond 48 hours.** Normal DOMS from a hard session clears in 24 to 48 hours. If your legs are still sore three or four days after a session, clearance is impaired. The system is backed up. More training will not fix that. Only recovery will.

If three or more of these markers are present simultaneously, stop the current training plan and take a deload. Do not wait for the next scheduled one. The plan serves you, not the other way around.

## Sample Deload Week Structures

Training volume varies enormously between riders. A deload for someone riding six hours a week looks different from a deload for someone riding twelve. The principle is the same — reduce TSS by 40 to 60 percent, maintain brief intensity — but the application scales.

### The Six-Hour-Per-Week Rider (Typical: 300 TSS Normal Week)

**Monday:** Off  
**Tuesday:** 45 minutes easy spinning, truly conversational pace  
**Wednesday:** 60 minutes with 3 x 2-minute tempo efforts. Not threshold. Tempo. Recover fully between each.  
**Thursday:** Off  
**Friday:** 30 minutes easy spinning — active recovery, nothing more  
**Saturday:** 75 minutes steady endurance ride at the bottom of zone two  
**Sunday:** Off  

**Deload TSS target:** 130-180  
**Total ride time:** Approximately 3.5 hours

### The Eight-Hour-Per-Week Rider (Typical: 450 TSS Normal Week)

**Monday:** Off  
**Tuesday:** 60 minutes easy endurance  
**Wednesday:** 75 minutes with 4 x 30-second openers at race intensity, full recovery between each  
**Thursday:** 45 minutes easy spinning  
**Friday:** Off  
**Saturday:** 90 minutes steady endurance, cafe stop mandatory  
**Sunday:** 45 minutes easy recovery spin  

**Deload TSS target:** 180-270  
**Total ride time:** Approximately 5.25 hours

### The Twelve-Hour-Per-Week Rider (Typical: 650 TSS Normal Week)

**Monday:** Off  
**Tuesday:** 75 minutes easy endurance  
**Wednesday:** 90 minutes with 20 minutes of tempo in the middle, bookended by easy riding  
**Thursday:** 60 minutes easy spinning  
**Friday:** Off or 30 minutes very easy  
**Saturday:** 2 hours steady endurance at the bottom of zone two  
**Sunday:** 60 minutes easy recovery with 4 x 20-second sprints for neuromuscular touch  

**Deload TSS target:** 260-390  
**Total ride time:** Approximately 7-7.5 hours

Notice the pattern across all three: two days fully off the bike, one session with brief intensity, everything else at genuine conversational pace. The higher-volume rider keeps more total time but the reduction in stress is proportionally the same.

## Deloads and Racing — Two Different Situations

Deloading interacts with your race calendar in two distinct ways, and confusing them will cost you performance.

### Deloading Into a Target Event (The Taper)

A pre-race deload is technically a taper, but the underlying physiology is identical: remove accumulated fatigue so that the fitness you have built is fully expressed on race day. The fatigue has been masking your form. Strip it away and the numbers you could not hit last Tuesday suddenly reappear on Saturday.

A race taper typically lasts seven to fourteen days depending on the importance of the event and the depth of the preceding training block. Volume drops by 40 to 60 percent. Intensity stays — short, race-specific efforts that keep the neuromuscular system primed. Two or three sessions of openers in the final week: 5 x 1-minute at race power with full recovery, or similar. Everything else is easy.

The performance management chart should show TSB (Training Stress Balance) rising into positive territory during the taper. That positive TSB is the point. It represents freshness. The higher your CTL was before the taper, the more fitness is available to be revealed once the fatigue clears.

### Deloading After a Block of Racing

The opposite situation: you have just finished three consecutive weekends of racing, or a stage race, or a particularly brutal run of events. The body is carrying both training fatigue and race fatigue, which is a deeper hole because racing adds neurological and psychological stress on top of the physical load.

Post-race deloads should be longer and easier than standard training deloads. Seven to ten days minimum after a heavy racing block. Very little intensity — your nervous system has been operating at maximum output and needs genuine quiet. Easy spins, social rides, maybe one light tempo session toward the end of the deload to test readiness before resuming structured training.

## Reading the Performance Management Chart During a Deload

If you track your training with any power-based platform — TrainingPeaks, Intervals.icu, WKO, or similar — you are looking at a performance management chart (PMC) that shows three lines: CTL, ATL, and TSB.

**CTL (Chronic Training Load)** is your fitness. It is a rolling average of your daily TSS over approximately 42 days. During a deload, CTL will drop. It has to. You are applying less stress than the rolling average, so the average declines. This is where the anxiety hits. You watch the blue line drip downward and every instinct says you are losing fitness.

You are not. CTL is a proxy for training load, not a direct measure of physiological capacity. Your mitochondria do not disappear because a number on a screen dropped three points. What is actually happening during the deload is that your body is consolidating the training you did during the build. The CTL drop reflects less incoming stress. The adaptation is happening underneath, invisible on the chart.

**ATL (Acute Training Load)** is your fatigue. It is a rolling average over approximately seven days. During a deload, ATL drops sharply — much faster than CTL — because you are applying very little new stress. This is the mechanism that creates freshness.

**TSB (Training Stress Balance)** is the difference between CTL and ATL. When ATL drops faster than CTL, TSB rises. Positive TSB means you are fresh relative to your fitness level. During a deload, TSB should climb from negative territory (fatigued) into positive territory (fresh). A TSB of +10 to +25 at the end of a deload week is typical for most riders and indicates readiness to begin the next training block.

The mental shift you need to make: stop watching CTL during a deload. Watch TSB. The rising TSB line is the deload working. When you start the next build block, CTL will climb again — and it will climb from a higher effective baseline because the previous block's adaptations have been absorbed.

## The Psychological Battle

I am not going to pretend this is easy. Sitting on the sofa while your mates are posting Strava rides takes a kind of discipline that is entirely different from grinding out a fifth interval set. It is quiet discipline. Nobody sees it. Nobody congratulates you for it. There is no segment PR for a well-executed rest day.

The fear of losing fitness is the most powerful force working against you. You have spent weeks building CTL and now you are watching it decline. The club WhatsApp group is full of weekend rides and you are doing 45 minutes of easy spinning. Some part of your brain is absolutely convinced that everyone else is getting faster while you get softer.

That conviction is wrong. While you are recovering, they are accumulating the same fatigue that was dragging your numbers down. Their turn to deload will come — or, if they skip it, their plateau will arrive instead.

Joe Friel addresses this directly in his work with masters athletes. His position is that the hardest coaching conversation he has is not about training harder. It is about training less. The athletes who progress year on year are the ones who can tolerate the discomfort of backing off. The ones who cannot tolerate it oscillate: build, burn out, take a forced break, build again from a lower baseline. Repeat for years. Net improvement: close to zero.

Stephen Seiler puts it differently but arrives at the same place. His research shows that the athletes who distribute their intensity correctly — mostly easy, occasionally very hard, with structured recovery periods — outperform athletes with higher training volumes but poor intensity management. More is not better. Better is better. And better requires rest.

Dan Lorang, when I spoke to him about how he structures training for World Tour riders, was unequivocal: the recovery week is not a suggestion. It is a load-bearing wall in the training structure. Remove it and the entire block fails to produce the intended adaptation. He does not let his riders negotiate on deload timing. The plan says recover, they recover. No exceptions, regardless of how the legs feel.

## Coming Out of a Deload — What to Expect

If you executed the deload correctly — reduced volume, maintained brief intensity, slept well, ate well — the first hard session of the next block should feel noticeably different.

Power that felt heavy in week three of the build should feel manageable. Heart rate at the same wattage should be lower. RPE at threshold should drop by one to two points. The connection between brain and legs should feel sharper, more responsive. Accelerations that felt sluggish should feel snappy.

This is supercompensation expressed. The training stress you applied during the build has been converted into actual physiological adaptation. Mitochondrial density is higher. Glycogen stores are full. The neuromuscular system has been recalibrated. You are, measurably and noticeably, fitter than you were before the deload.

If you do not feel that shift, one of two things has happened. Either the deload was not long enough — in which case extend it by two to three more easy days before testing again — or the preceding build was not sufficiently stimulating and the stress was not enough to drive adaptation. Both are correctable. Neither is cause for panic.

## Building Deloads Into Your Annual Plan

The riders who progress consistently are the ones who plan their deloads at the same time they plan their training blocks. Not as an afterthought. Not as something they will get to if they feel tired. As a fixed, immovable part of the structure.

A simple annual framework:

**Base phase (winter):** Three weeks build, one week deload. Moderate loading, emphasis on volume and aerobic development. Deloads reduce volume by 40 percent.

**Build phase (spring):** Three weeks build, one week deload for riders under 45. Two weeks build, one week deload for riders over 45. Higher intensity, more race-specific work. Deloads reduce volume by 50 percent but maintain openers.

**Race phase (summer):** Deloads timed around target events. Taper into key races. Recovery weeks after blocks of racing. Flexibility required — the race calendar dictates the structure.

**Transition (autumn):** Extended deload of two to four weeks. Unstructured riding, cross-training, mental break from numbers and plans. This is the annual reset that prevents season-on-season burnout.

Within each block, track your resting heart rate and HRV daily. If those markers start drifting before the scheduled deload, bring it forward. The calendar is a guide. Your body is the authority.

## The Deload Is Where Fitness Is Built

I know how this sounds. You trained hard for three weeks and I am telling you the fitness was built during the week you barely rode. It sounds like a con. But it is how the physiology works, and every serious coach — from Friel to Seiler to Lorang — will tell you exactly the same thing.

The training block provides the stimulus. The deload allows the adaptation. Without the deload, you are doing all the work and collecting none of the reward. You are paying into an account you never let compound.

The riders who get faster year on year are not the ones who train the most. They are the ones who recover the best. They are the ones who can watch their CTL drop for seven days without panicking. They are the ones who understand that the purpose of rest is not the absence of training — it is the completion of it.

You are not being lazy. You are not losing fitness. You are doing the part that matters most, the part that most riders refuse to do because it requires a kind of patience that does not come naturally to competitive people.

Do less. Absorb more. Come back sharper.

That is the deload. That is the skill.

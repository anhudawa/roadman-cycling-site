You have spent three months building form for a Mallorca cycling holiday. The flights are booked, the hotel has a bike storage room, and you have mapped every climb on the east coast. You have thought about gearing, tyres, nutrition, sun cream, even the coffee stops.

You have not read your travel insurance policy. Almost nobody does.

And when you do — properly, past the summary page and into the exclusions — there is a reasonable chance you will find that the thing you are flying abroad to do is the thing your policy does not cover.

## The Standard Policy Problem

Most travel insurance is designed for people who lie on beaches, visit museums, and eat dinner. It covers lost luggage, cancelled flights, and a trip to a local hospital if you pick up a stomach bug. It is not designed for someone descending a mountain pass at 70kph on a $12,000 carbon bike with 40 other riders.

Standard policies work on exclusion lists. Activities the insurer considers higher risk than a normal holiday are classified as hazardous, adventurous, or sporting — and excluded from cover unless you pay an upgrade or buy a specialist policy. The trouble is that cycling sits in a grey area. Walking along a beachfront promenade on a rented bike? Covered. Riding a road bike on open roads, in traffic, on mountain descents? That is where the exclusions start.

The exact wording varies by insurer, but the pattern is consistent. Road cycling on public roads is frequently classified as a hazardous activity. Group riding amplifies the classification. Descending at speed, riding at altitude, riding in organised events — each of these can trigger a separate exclusion.

What most riders assume: my travel insurance covers me if I get hurt on holiday. What actually happens: the insurer reads the claim, checks the activity, finds the exclusion, and declines. You are left with a hospital bill in a foreign country and no recourse.

## What Counts as Hazardous Activity

Every insurer defines hazardous activity slightly differently, and this is where the detail matters. Some policies exclude cycling entirely unless it is "leisure cycling on paved, designated cycle paths." That language is specific and it is deliberate. A cycle path is not a public road. A public road is not a mountain pass. If you are riding your road bike on the Ma-10 in Mallorca — a public road shared with cars, motorbikes, and tourist coaches — you are not on a designated cycle path.

Other policies take a speed-based approach: cycling above a certain speed, or cycling that involves descending, or cycling above a certain altitude. Some use the vague catch-all of "extreme sports" and leave the definition to the claims assessor.

The fixable version of this problem is reading the policy document before you buy. Not the marketing page. Not the comparison site summary. The actual policy wording, specifically the section titled "Exclusions" or "What is not covered." If cycling on public roads is not explicitly listed as a covered activity, it is safer to assume it is excluded.

## Sportives, Gran Fondos, and the Competition Clause

This catches more riders than any other exclusion.

You have signed up for a gran fondo in Italy. It is not a race — there are no prizes, no ranking that matters, and you are riding it at your own pace with 3,000 other amateurs. It is a long day out on beautiful roads with feed stations and a medal at the finish. You are not competing. You are participating.

Your insurer disagrees. The moment an event has a start line, a finish line, timing chips, and an entry fee, most standard travel policies classify it as competitive sport. Competitive sport is excluded. It does not matter that you finished 2,400th out of 3,000. It does not matter that your goal was to survive, not to win. The policy wording does not distinguish between Tadej Pogacar and a 47-year-old accountant from Surrey on a bucket-list ride. Both are participating in an organised sporting event. Both are excluded.

Charity rides with timed segments fall into the same trap. Sportives with age-group categories. Time trials. Hill climbs. Anything with a number pinned to your jersey.

This is the scenario that plays out badly. You crash 80km into the Mallorca 312 sportive. Collarbone, road rash, maybe a concussion. The ambulance takes you to a hospital in Palma. You call your insurer from the hospital bed, and the first question they ask is: what were you doing when the incident occurred? You explain that you were riding in an organised event. The claim is declined.

The hospital bill arrives. Treatment, X-rays, overnight stay, follow-up consultation. Depending on the injury and the hospital, you are looking at $3,000 to $15,000. That is your bill. Uninsured.

Cycling-specific policies cover sportive and event participation as standard. That is the entire point of their existence.

## Your Bike Is Probably Not Covered

Standard travel insurance typically includes a "personal belongings" section with a single-item limit. That limit is often $500 to $1,000. Your bike is worth considerably more than that.

Picture this: you are staying at a hotel in Rimini for a week of riding in Emilia-Romagna. The hotel has a bike storage room — a shared space on the ground floor with a basic lock. You come down one morning and the bike is gone. Stolen overnight. Your Cervelo or Canyon or Pinarello, the one you spent $8,000 on and another $2,000 upgrading, is now in the back of someone's van heading to a market where it will be stripped and sold for parts.

Your travel insurance personal belongings cover pays out $500 against a $10,000 loss. That is not a rounding error. That is a catastrophic gap.

Dedicated bike insurance — from providers like Yellow Jersey, Bikmo, or Pedalsure — covers the bike at its full declared value, including overseas trips. Typical annual premiums for a bike valued between $5,000 and $15,000 run from $200 to $500 per year, depending on the cover level and the excess you choose. Some policies include race and event cover, accidental damage, crash replacement, and theft from vehicles (with security requirements).

A cycling-specific travel insurance policy bundles bike cover with medical and trip cover into a single product. Either approach works. The mistake is travelling with a valuable bike and assuming it is protected by a policy that was designed to cover a lost suitcase.

## Medical Evacuation — The Number Nobody Checks

Your travel policy might show a headline medical cover figure of $5 million or $10 million. That looks reassuring. But the number that matters for cyclists is buried in the sub-limits: evacuation and repatriation.

Consider the Col du Galibier. You are 15km from the summit on a quiet Tuesday in July. The road is narrow, the gradient is steep, and the nearest town is a long way below. You clip a pothole on a descent, go over the bars, and land badly. Broken hip. You cannot move.

Someone calls the emergency services. A helicopter is dispatched from the nearest hospital with mountain rescue capability. That helicopter costs between $15,000 and $50,000 depending on the country, the distance, and the hospital. In Switzerland, helicopter evacuation costs can exceed $80,000. In France, the PGHM (mountain gendarmerie) performs the rescue, but the medical transport and hospital treatment are billed separately.

Standard travel policies may cover evacuation — but frequently cap it at a figure well below what a mountain helicopter rescue actually costs. Some policies exclude helicopter transport entirely unless it is deemed "medically necessary and no alternative exists," which gives the insurer room to dispute the claim after the fact.

Check the evacuation sub-limit. Not the headline medical figure. The evacuation sub-limit. If it is capped at $10,000 and the helicopter costs $40,000, you are covering the difference.

## EHIC and GHIC — Useful but Not Insurance

If you hold a European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) or the UK's Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC), you have access to state healthcare in EU and EEA countries at the same cost as a local resident. In countries with universal healthcare, that means treatment may be free or heavily subsidised at the point of delivery.

This is useful. It is not insurance.

The EHIC does not cover repatriation to your home country. It does not cover helicopter evacuation. It does not cover private hospital treatment — and in some countries, the nearest hospital with the right orthopaedic capability may be a private facility. It does not cover dental treatment beyond emergency care. It does not cover your bike, your kit, or your cancelled flights.

It also does not work outside the EU and EEA. Turkey, Morocco, and other popular cycling destinations are not covered.

Think of the EHIC as a supplement. It reduces the cost of state treatment in EU countries, which means your insurer pays less and your claim is processed faster. But it replaces nothing. You still need a travel insurance policy that covers cycling, and you still need bike cover.

## Providers Who Cover Cyclists Properly

The UK market has several insurers who build policies specifically for cyclists. They understand the sport, they understand the equipment, and — critically — they understand what a claim looks like when a rider crashes in a foreign country.

**Yellow Jersey** offers cycling-specific travel insurance and standalone bike insurance. Their policies cover sportives, races, and organised events as standard. Bike cover is included in their travel policies and available separately for year-round protection.

**Bikmo** started as a bike insurance provider and expanded into travel cover. Their policies cover the bike at agreed value, which means the payout matches what you declared rather than a depreciated figure the insurer decides after the fact.

**Pedalsure** offers combined bike and cycling travel insurance, covering racing, sportives, and worldwide travel with bike cover included.

**SportsCover Direct** provides sports-specific travel insurance with cycling as a named covered activity, including competition and event participation.

These are UK-based providers, but riders in other markets should look for the same features: cycling explicitly listed as a covered activity (not a hazardous exclusion with an optional upgrade), event and sportive participation included as standard, bike cover at declared value, and evacuation cover with realistic limits.

The difference between a cycling-specific insurer and a standard insurer with a sports add-on becomes obvious at the claims stage. When you call to report a cracked carbon frame, a cycling insurer knows what you are talking about. They know that a rear derailleur hanger is not the same as a new bike. They know that a crack in a carbon seatstay is a write-off, not a repair. That understanding saves weeks of back-and-forth that you do not want when you are injured in another country.

## Single-Trip vs Annual Multi-Trip

If you take one cycling holiday per year, a single-trip cycling travel policy is the simple option. Typical cost for a European trip of one to two weeks with bike cover: $150 to $300 depending on the provider and the cover level.

If you ride abroad more than once — a spring training camp, a summer sportive, an autumn gran fondo — annual multi-trip cover almost always works out cheaper. Typical cost: $300 to $600 per year for worldwide cycling travel cover with bike protection.

The maths is not complicated. Two single-trip policies at $200 each cost $400. An annual policy at $350 covers both trips and every other trip you decide to take that year. The annual policy pays for itself before the second trip starts.

Annual multi-trip policies also eliminate the risk of forgetting to buy cover for a last-minute trip. The invite to a long weekend in Girona arrives on a Wednesday. You fly on Friday. With an annual policy, you are already covered. Without one, you are scrambling to buy single-trip cover in 48 hours — or, more realistically, you travel uninsured and hope for the best.

## The Claims Process — Documentation Wins Everything

Insurance exists to pay claims. But insurers only pay claims that are properly documented, and the documentation you need starts before anything goes wrong.

**Before you leave:**

Photograph your bike from multiple angles — drive side, non-drive side, front, rear. Photograph the serial number on the bottom bracket. Photograph the components: groupset, wheels, saddle, handlebars, computer mount. If you have made upgrades, photograph those individually with the receipts visible.

Keep purchase receipts for the frame, wheels, and groupset. If you bought second-hand, keep the transaction record. Insurers want proof of value, and "I paid $8,000 for it three years ago" without a receipt is a contested claim.

Photograph the bike packed in the bike bag before you leave. This establishes condition at departure and makes it harder for anyone — including the airline — to claim the damage was pre-existing.

**If something goes wrong:**

Photograph the scene immediately. The road, the bike, the damage, any vehicles involved, any witnesses. Get names and contact details of witnesses if possible.

For theft: report it to the local police within 24 hours and obtain a police report number. Most insurers will not process a theft claim without a police report. In Italy, this means visiting the Carabinieri or Polizia and filing a denuncia — a formal report. It takes time. It is bureaucratic. It is non-negotiable.

For medical treatment: keep every document the hospital gives you. Admission records, treatment records, discharge summaries, prescriptions, invoices. Request copies in English where available. Photograph everything.

For bike damage: do not repair the bike before the insurer has assessed the claim, or at minimum before you have photographed the damage thoroughly. A repaired bike is a disputed claim.

Call your insurer as soon as possible. Most policies require notification within 24 to 48 hours of an incident. Late notification is a common reason for rejected claims, and it is entirely avoidable.

## A Pre-Trip Checklist

Before every cycling trip abroad, run through this list:

- **Policy wording reviewed** — is cycling on public roads explicitly covered, not just "leisure cycling"?
- **Event cover confirmed** — if you are riding a sportive, gran fondo, or any organised event, is competitive and organised sport covered?
- **Bike cover in place** — is the bike covered at its declared value, either through the travel policy or a separate bike insurance policy?
- **Evacuation sub-limit checked** — is helicopter and mountain evacuation covered with a realistic limit, not a $10,000 cap?
- **EHIC or GHIC packed** — as a supplement to insurance, not a replacement
- **Bike photographed** — serial number, components, overall condition, packed in the bike bag
- **Receipts accessible** — frame, wheels, groupset, upgrades, stored digitally where you can retrieve them from a hospital bed
- **Insurer contact details saved** — emergency number stored in your phone, not buried in an email you cannot find without wifi
- **Emergency contacts listed** — someone at home who knows your itinerary, your insurer details, and your policy number

## The Cost of Getting It Wrong

A week-long cycling trip to Mallorca costs roughly $1,500 to $3,000 for flights, hotel, and bike transport. The bike you are taking is worth $5,000 to $15,000. The medical bills from a serious crash in a foreign hospital can run from $5,000 to $50,000. Helicopter evacuation from a mountain pass can add another $15,000 to $80,000.

Proper cycling travel insurance for that trip costs $150 to $300. Annual cover for all your trips costs $300 to $600.

The arithmetic is not subtle. The risk is real and the cost of cover is small relative to the cost of riding uninsured. Every year, riders fly to southern Europe with expensive bikes and inadequate insurance, and a small percentage of them find out exactly how expensive that gap is.

This is fixable. Read the policy. Check the exclusions. Buy cover that actually matches what you are going to do. Then go and ride every climb on your list without a financial disaster sitting in your back pocket alongside the energy gels.

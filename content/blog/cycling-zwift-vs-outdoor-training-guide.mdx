Your Zwift FTP says 280. Your outdoor FTP says 260. Both numbers came from legitimate tests. Both are accurate measurements of what you produced on that day, in that environment, with those conditions. But only one of them is going to show up when you pin a number on and line up at a real event on a real road with real wind.

This is the conversation nobody in the Zwift community particularly wants to have. Not because the platform is bad — it is brilliant at what it does — but because the gap between indoor performance and outdoor performance is wider than most riders realise, and the consequences of ignoring it range from blown pacing strategies to entire seasons of misallocated training.

I have had over 1,400 conversations on the podcast with coaches, sports scientists, and athletes who race at every level from Saturday morning club runs to Grand Tours. The indoor-versus-outdoor question comes up constantly. And the answer is never as simple as "both are good" or "one is better." The answer depends entirely on what you are preparing for, what phase of training you are in, and whether you are a 25-year-old with elastic recovery or a 45-year-old who pays for every hard session with an extra day of fatigue.

This post is specifically for the second group. If you are a masters cyclist — roughly 35 to 55 — and you race or ride events outdoors, this is how to think about Zwift, the indoor trainer, and the road.

## The Zwift FTP Problem

Let me start with the number, because the number drives everything.

Indoor FTP is almost always higher than outdoor FTP. The magnitude varies — 5 per cent for well-cooled riders with optimal fan setups, 10-15 per cent for riders testing in warm rooms with inadequate cooling — but the direction is consistent. Your indoor number will be higher than your outdoor number.

Three things drive this.

**Cooling.** Outdoors, the air moving over your body at 30-40 km/h provides substantial convective cooling. Your core temperature stays lower, your cardiovascular system doesn't have to divert as much blood to the skin for thermoregulation, and more of your cardiac output goes to the working muscles. Indoors, even with a good fan, you are fighting thermodynamics. The air speed from a floor fan is a fraction of what you get at riding speed. Your core temperature rises faster, heart rate drifts higher at the same power, and your ability to sustain threshold and above drops earlier. The paradox: you feel like you are working harder indoors at the same wattage, and you are. But in the structured test environment with ERG mode holding the power, you can push through it for 20 minutes in a way you probably would not sustain over 60 minutes of outdoor riding.

**Body position variability.** On the road, you shift your weight constantly. You stand on a climb, tuck on a descent, move your hands between the hoods and the drops, rock the bike in a sprint. Each of those movements recruits different muscle groups briefly and gives others micro-rest. On the trainer, you sit in essentially the same position for the entire session. The same muscle fibres do the same work throughout, and localised fatigue accumulates faster. But during a 20-minute test, this actually works in your favour — the efficiency of a fixed position produces a marginally higher number than the same effort outdoors where position changes introduce tiny inefficiencies.

**Environmental consistency.** Indoors, there is no wind. No gradient changes. No traffic lights forcing you to stop and restart. Every watt you produce gets captured. Outdoors, the same effort includes micro-coasts, braking events, wind gusts, and gradient transitions that the power meter records but that dilute your average power. A 20-minute effort on a rolling outdoor road is physiologically different from a 20-minute effort on a flat virtual road at fixed resistance.

The practical consequence is this: if you train to your indoor FTP outdoors, you will over-cook every effort. Your threshold sessions will be above threshold. Your sweet spot will be at threshold. Your endurance rides will be in the grey zone. Everything shifts up, and the result is either blown efforts or chronic under-recovery. Check where your numbers actually sit with the [Masters FTP Benchmark](/tools/masters-ftp-benchmark) — it will tell you whether your expectations match reality for your age and training history.

The fix is simple. Test in both environments. Use your indoor FTP for indoor sessions. Use your outdoor FTP for outdoor sessions. They are two different numbers for two different contexts, and treating them as interchangeable is where things go wrong.

## What Zwift Cannot Simulate

The virtual world is getting better every year. The graphics are prettier, the physics engine is more sophisticated, the group dynamics are more realistic. But there remain fundamental things about outdoor cycling that no screen can replicate, and these are the things that decide races.

### Wind

This is the big one. Zwift has a draft mechanic, but it is a simplified version of what happens on a real road. In the real world, wind changes direction mid-ride. A headwind on the outbound leg becomes a crosswind on the return. Echelons form not because someone pressed a button but because riders read the wind angle, positioned themselves correctly, and the riders who did not read it got gapped. The ability to sense wind direction, choose position in the bunch accordingly, and adjust effort in real time is a skill that only develops through outdoor riding. It does not exist on Zwift.

### Cornering under load

You can corner on Zwift by steering or by doing nothing at all. On the road, cornering is a full-body skill that integrates speed judgment, lean angle, tyre grip assessment, road surface reading, braking control, and confidence. In a crit or a fast sportive, the riders who corner well gain five to ten seconds per corner without increasing their power. Over a 60-minute crit with 40 corners, that is minutes of free time. You cannot practise this indoors.

### Road surface and terrain reading

Outdoors, the road tells you things before they happen. The surface changes from smooth tarmac to chipseal and your rolling resistance shifts. The gradient kicks up slightly and you need to respond before your speed drops. A pothole appears and you adjust your line. These are low-level cognitive skills that run in the background during every outdoor ride, and they keep your pattern-recognition sharp for racing. Three months of exclusively indoor training blunts them.

### The chaos of group riding

A Zwift group ride is orderly. A real bunch is not. Someone brakes unexpectedly. A rider overlaps your wheel. The pace surges on a rise and then stalls as the bunch concertinas coming into a roundabout. Managing your position, your effort, and your attention simultaneously — while also eating, drinking, and checking over your shoulder — is the actual skill of road racing. It cannot be trained on a screen.

## When Zwift Actually Earns Its Place

I am not writing this to tell you to cancel your subscription. Zwift has legitimate, defensible uses for the rider who races outdoors, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

### Weekday precision intervals

You get home from work at 6pm. It is dark. It is raining. You have 75 minutes before dinner. The turbo is in the garage. This is where Zwift is unbeatable. You warm up for 10 minutes, execute your prescribed intervals — 4x8 minutes at threshold, say, or 5x4 minutes at VO2max — cool down, and you are in the shower by 7:15. The session quality is high because the environment is controlled. The time investment is minimal because there is no kit-change, no route-finding, no traffic. For the time-crunched masters rider with a job and a family, this is often the difference between training four times a week and training twice.

### Specific interval execution

Some sessions are simply better indoors. Short VO2max intervals — 30/30s, 40/20s, Tabata-style efforts — are cleaner on ERG mode than they are on the road, where you spend the first 10 seconds of each effort getting up to speed and the recovery intervals are contaminated by braking, cornering, or gradient changes. Sweet spot 2x20s, where the goal is to hold 88-93 per cent of FTP for exactly 20 minutes, are more consistent indoors because you remove the pacing variability of outdoor terrain. Use the [FTP Zones calculator](/tools/ftp-zones) to make sure your indoor targets are set from your indoor test, not your outdoor one.

### Bad weather blocks

There are stretches of winter in the UK and Ireland — and in much of the northern US and Canada — where outdoor riding is not just unpleasant but properly dangerous. Ice, standing water, gale-force winds, four hours of daylight. During those blocks, Zwift keeps you on the bike. The base fitness you build through a consistent indoor winter is worth more than the sporadic outdoor rides you would manage in the same conditions. This is not controversial. The trainer saves seasons.

### Social accountability

Group rides on Zwift, done properly, provide social pressure to show up. For the rider who struggles with motivation during winter base phase, knowing that their mates are on at 7am Saturday morning creates accountability that a solo turbo session does not. The key phrase is "done properly" — meaning a genuine zone 2 group ride where the pace stays low, not a group ride that descends into a race within the first five minutes.

## The Engagement Trap

Here is where I get honest.

Zwift is designed to keep you on the platform. That is not a criticism — it is a business model, and it works. The leaderboard segments, the race results, the XP points, the jersey unlocks, the route badges — all of it exists to make you come back tomorrow. And it does. Zwift's retention numbers are extraordinary precisely because the dopamine feedback loop is so well-tuned.

The problem is that the dopamine feedback loop and the training adaptation loop point in different directions.

The dopamine loop says: race tonight. The training plan says: easy spin. The dopamine loop says: chase that KOM segment. The training plan says: stay in zone 2. The dopamine loop says: ride with the fast group. The training plan says: ride alone at your own pace.

Every time the dopamine wins, the training plan loses. And the dopamine wins more often than most riders will admit, because the feeling of racing hard on Zwift is indistinguishable from the feeling of training hard. The sweat is real. The effort is real. The elevated heart rate is real. It feels productive. But if tonight's session was supposed to be easy endurance work and you have just spent 45 minutes at threshold chasing someone's wheel through Watopia, you have replaced an adaptation-building session with an adaptation-neutral one. One night of that does not matter. Three months of that costs you a season.

The riders who use Zwift well are the riders who log in with a plan, execute the plan, and log off. They treat it as a tool, not an experience. The riders who get caught in the engagement trap are the riders who open the app and see what is starting in five minutes.

## The Masters-Specific Angle

This is where the conversation gets particular to the audience reading this site.

### Recovery cost

For a 25-year-old, a hard Zwift race on Tuesday and a hard outdoor ride on Thursday is fine. The recovery window is short enough that both sessions produce adaptation and neither compromises the next.

For a 45-year-old, the same week looks different. The indoor session creates more fatigue per unit of time than the equivalent outdoor session because of the sustained static loading, the higher core temperature, and the elevated heart rate at equivalent power. The Thursday outdoor ride arrives before the Tuesday session has been properly absorbed. By Saturday, the long ride is compromised. By the following Tuesday, the cycle starts again with an accumulated deficit.

I have seen this pattern in dozens of riders who come through the Not Done Yet community. Their training load looks reasonable — 7-8 hours a week, four to five sessions — but they are persistently flat because every session carries a slightly higher recovery cost than the plan anticipated. The fix is usually not fewer hours. It is fewer indoor hours. Swapping one indoor session for an outdoor endurance ride at true zone 2, where the position changes, the cooling is better, and the intensity stays properly low, often produces an immediate improvement in freshness. The [Masters Recovery Score](/tools/masters-recovery-score) will help you track whether your current split is working or whether you are carrying more fatigue than you realise.

### Joint impact

The static position on an indoor trainer is harder on the lower back, the hip flexors, and the knees than outdoor riding. Outdoors, the constant micro-adjustments — standing, sitting, shifting weight, adjusting hand position — distribute the load across more tissues. Indoors, the same tissues bear the load continuously. For the masters rider with a desk job who already sits for eight hours before training, adding 60-90 minutes of fixed-position pedalling amplifies existing postural issues. If your lower back is consistently tight after indoor sessions but not after outdoor rides of the same duration, the trainer position is the likely cause.

### Mental fatigue

This is under-discussed. Outdoor riding provides genuine mental recovery even during hard sessions. The scenery changes. The environment demands attention but not the kind of grinding focus that indoor intervals require. The sensory experience — wind, temperature shifts, sounds, smells — is restorative in a way that a screen in a garage is not.

For the masters rider managing work stress, family logistics, and the general cognitive load of adult life, outdoor rides provide a mental break that indoor sessions do not. This matters for long-term consistency. The rider who dreads every session because they are all on the turbo will eventually stop showing up. The rider who looks forward to the Saturday morning group ride will be there in October when the dark mornings arrive.

## Blending Both Across a Training Week

Here is a practical framework. This is not a training plan — it is a structure that you can adapt to your own schedule, your own goals, and your own recovery capacity. It assumes a masters rider training five to six days per week, preparing for outdoor road events.

### Monday
Rest or very light spin. No Zwift races. No structured work. If you ride at all, it is 30 minutes at recovery pace, outdoors if possible.

### Tuesday
Indoor structured session. Prescribed intervals — the hard stuff belongs here because the environment is controlled and the session is time-efficient. Threshold work, VO2max intervals, over-under efforts. This is where Zwift or your structured platform earns its subscription. Use workout mode or ERG mode. Do not free-ride.

### Wednesday
Outdoor endurance ride if schedule allows. One to two hours at genuine zone 2. If time or weather prevents outdoor riding, a light indoor spin is acceptable, but the goal is easy — not a group ride that turns into a race. Monitor your [Training Load](/tools/training-load) to ensure this session stays where it belongs.

### Thursday
Indoor structured session. The second key session of the week. Different stimulus from Tuesday — if Tuesday was threshold, Thursday is VO2max or vice versa. Same rules: prescribed work, controlled execution, no chasing.

### Friday
Rest or very easy spin. Recovery before the weekend.

### Saturday
Outdoor long ride. This is non-negotiable on the road. Group ride, sportive route, whatever gets you three to five hours of outdoor riding with real terrain, real wind, real bunch dynamics. Practise pacing, practise cornering, practise eating on the bike. This is the session Zwift cannot replace.

### Sunday
Outdoor ride — moderate intensity, one to two hours. This can be a coffee ride, a skill-focused session, or a steady tempo effort on terrain similar to your target events. The second outdoor ride of the weekend cements the outdoor skills that the weekday indoor sessions cannot develop.

This split gives you two indoor sessions (precision work), two outdoor rides (specificity work), and flexibility on the fifth and sixth days. Adjust as needed. The principles are more important than the specific days.

## Blending Both Across a Season

The weekly split should change across the year, and this is where most riders get it wrong. They keep the same indoor-outdoor ratio from January to July and wonder why their outdoor performance plateaus in May.

### November to February (base phase)

Indoor sessions can dominate. Three indoor sessions, one to two outdoor rides. The indoor work is predominantly sweet spot and tempo — controlled, progressive, consistent. The outdoor rides are for maintaining bike handling and keeping the feel of the road. This is where Zwift's social features actually help: group rides and accountability keep the base phase from becoming a motivation desert.

### March to April (build phase)

Shift towards two indoor, two to three outdoor. The indoor sessions stay structured but move towards race-specific intensities — harder threshold work, longer VO2max intervals. The outdoor rides start including group riding, hills, and race-pace efforts. The transition matters because the rider needs to recalibrate their power perception from the indoor environment to the outdoor one.

### May to September (race season)

One indoor session per week maximum, used for the midweek interval session that is too time-consuming to do on the road. Everything else is outdoors. The long ride, the group ride, the recovery ride, the race itself — all on real roads. This is where the outdoor-specific skills you have been maintaining all winter pay off. If your indoor FTP has been your reference all winter, test outdoors in March and recalibrate everything.

### September to October (transition)

Ease back. Reduce structure. Ride for enjoyment. If you want to Zwift race for fun, this is the time to do it without worrying about the training implications. Let the body recover from the season. Let the mind reset from the structure.

## The Bottom Line

Zwift is a brilliant piece of technology that solved a real problem — it made indoor training bearable, and for millions of riders, it made consistent training possible for the first time. That matters.

But for the masters cyclist who races outdoors, Zwift is a supporting actor. It fills the gaps that weather, daylight, and schedule create. It provides precision when precision is needed. It keeps you honest on dark Tuesday evenings when the alternative is the sofa.

What it does not do is prepare you for the wind in your face on a February sportive. It does not teach you to read a bunch. It does not develop the cornering confidence that wins crits. It does not build the pacing instincts that come from hundreds of hours on real roads with real gradients and real fatigue.

Use it for what it is good at. Stop using it for what it is not. Test in both environments. Train to the right numbers. Respect the recovery cost. And when the weather breaks and the roads are dry, get outside. The road is where the results happen, and the road does not have a leaderboard.

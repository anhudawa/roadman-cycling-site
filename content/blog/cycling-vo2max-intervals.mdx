VO2max intervals for cycling are repeated hard efforts intended to accumulate work at a high fraction of maximal oxygen uptake. They are not one fixed duration, one FTP percentage or one “Norwegian” workout.

The practical answer is: **choose a format that matches the rider and goal, pace it so the work remains repeatable, and progress one variable only after normal recovery**.

## The short answer: which session should you do?

| Rider and job                          | Starting session        | Why choose it                                | Main caution                                            |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| New to severe-intensity intervals      | 4 × 3 min, 3 min easy   | Simple pacing and modest work volume         | Do not turn rep one into a test                         |
| Wants a familiar long format           | 4 × 4 min, 3–4 min easy | Easy to remember and scale                   | It is not a proven cycling gold standard                |
| Trained rider needing sustained work   | 4 × 8 min, 2–4 min easy | More accumulated continuous work             | Requires lower power and a larger dose                  |
| Rider who tolerates broken work better | 2–3 sets of 30/15s      | Short recoveries keep oxygen demand elevated | Research protocols can be too large for a first attempt |

The table is a decision aid, not a progression ladder. You do not have to “graduate” from 4×4 to 4×8 or from long intervals to 30/15s.

## What VO2max is—and is not

VO2max is the highest rate at which the body can take in, transport and use oxygen during an incremental test. A laboratory measures it from respiratory gases. A watch, smart trainer or field test estimates it from other signals.

It matters, but “VO2max is the ceiling and FTP is the floor” is a metaphor, not a complete performance model. The classic [Joyner and Coyle review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17901124/) describes the interaction between VO2max, the fraction of it that can be sustained and efficiency. Cycling performance also depends on anaerobic contribution, durability, aerodynamics, tactics and the event.

A [2025 systematic review in trained cyclists](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39788807/) found that training improved VO2max and time-trial performance across compared distributions without one training-distribution model clearly winning. Raising VO2max can be useful; it does not guarantee an equal change in FTP or race result.

## Why intervals are used

Continuous exercise at a severe intensity cannot be sustained for long. Recovery periods allow a rider to accumulate more work near that domain across a session.

The aim is not necessarily to touch the highest possible power. It is to create enough repeatable aerobic demand for the intended adaptation without allowing the first effort, glycolytic contribution or total fatigue to destroy the rest of the set.

Only gas analysis can show whether oxygen consumption reached a chosen fraction of VO2max. In everyday coaching, power, heart rate, breathing, RPE and repeatability are proxies with different strengths and delays.

## The four main VO2max interval formats

### 1. Introductory 4 × 3 minutes

This is a Roadman coaching template, not a claim that one trial proved the best beginner workout.

- Warm up progressively for 10–15 minutes.
- Ride 4 × 3 minutes at RPE 8–9/10.
- Pedal very easily for 3 minutes after each effort.
- Keep the opening controlled and the four efforts comparable.

If the rider has a current, credible FTP, the lower part of the conventional 106–120% FTP band can be a starting estimate. It is not a target to defend when the session response says otherwise.

### 2. The 4 × 4 format

A practical cycling version is four 4-minute efforts with 3–4 minutes of easy recovery. It is popular because it is simple and creates 16 minutes of work.

The evidence is frequently misrepresented. The famous [Helgerud et al. 2007 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17414804/) compared uphill treadmill-running programmes in 40 moderately trained men. The 4×4 group ran at 90–95% maximum heart rate with 3-minute active recoveries, three times per week for eight weeks. It supports that protocol in that population; it is not direct proof that 4×4 is the best cycling workout or that 106–120% FTP is its required dose.

For cycling, pace the first effort as the start of a set rather than a four-minute power test. A hard but reproducible RPE and power profile is more useful than reaching an arbitrary heart rate early.

### 3. The 4 × 8 format

In a [small randomised cycling study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21812820/), trained recreational cyclists completed two interval sessions per week for seven weeks. Groups used 4×4, 4×8 or 4×16 minutes at maximal tolerable session intensity, alongside low-intensity training. The 4×8 group produced the largest average improvement across the reported measures.

That result makes 4×8 a credible option, not a universal winner. Each interval group contained only nine riders, the accumulated work differed, and the 4×8 group rode at lower relative intensity than the 4×4 group. It is especially relevant when the job is sustained high aerobic work and the rider can tolerate 32 minutes of work without turning it into threshold survival.

### 4. The 30/15 format

Here, 30 seconds of hard work alternates with 15 seconds of easier pedalling, usually in sets. Oxygen consumption may remain elevated because the recovery is short.

A [2015 effort-matched trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24382021/) assigned 16 cyclists to short or long intervals twice weekly for ten weeks. The nine-rider short-interval group improved VO2max and several performance measures more than the seven-rider long-interval group under those study conditions.

That does not make 30/15 universally superior. In a [2025 crossover involving 12 well-trained cyclists and triathletes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40328438/), 3 × 13 × 30/15 produced more time above 90% VO2max than 6 × 5 minutes when intensity was fixed, but not when both were self-paced maximally. How intensity is controlled changes the comparison.

The full research format is a large first dose. A rider new to short intervals can use fewer repetitions or sets, then build only if power, technique and recovery remain appropriate.

## 4×4 vs 4×8 vs 30/15

| Question                 | 4×4                      | 4×8                                            | 30/15                                                |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Continuous work          | Moderate                 | High                                           | Broken into short bouts                              |
| Pacing demand            | Straightforward          | Requires restraint                             | Requires repeated changes                            |
| Typical use              | General hard aerobic set | Sustained severe/high aerobic work             | Repeatability and high oxygen demand                 |
| Evidence caveat          | Famous trial was running | Small cycling groups and more accumulated work | Small trials; outcome changes with intensity control |
| Best reason to choose it | Simple and scalable      | Event or limiter rewards longer work           | Rider executes short broken work better              |

There is no trophy for choosing the most scientific-looking session. The best format is the one that answers the current training question and can be placed into the week without degrading everything around it.

## Is time above 90% VO2max the goal?

It is one useful acute measure, especially in a laboratory. It is not a direct scoreboard for long-term fitness.

A [2026 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42237396/) evaluated 239 HIIT protocols from 86 articles. Longer work intervals generally produced more time near VO2max than short or moderate intervals, and variable-intensity work also performed well. The authors highlighted substantial variability and said the dose-response relationship between time near VO2max and long-term adaptation still needs investigation.

This helps explain why individual studies can appear to conflict. A short protocol may outperform a long one under one intensity prescription; pooled acute data may favour longer work in another comparison. Session design, athlete, intensity control and outcome are not interchangeable.

Do not extend a session solely to chase estimated minutes near VO2max after power, cadence, technique or symptoms have deteriorated.

## How hard should VO2max intervals be?

### Power

Power gives immediate external output. For efforts lasting roughly two to five minutes, **106–120% FTP is a common starting band**, not a physiological definition.

The rider’s power-duration curve matters. Two cyclists with the same FTP can have very different four-minute power and anaerobic contribution. An inflated or stale FTP makes every percentage misleading. If laboratory maximal aerobic power, critical power or a well-modelled power-duration profile is available, those can provide a more individual anchor.

### Heart rate

Heart rate shows a delayed internal response. It may take several minutes and repetitions to rise, and maximum heart rate itself may be estimated inaccurately. Heat, hydration, altitude, caffeine, medication and fatigue also affect the signal.

Do not sprint above the intended power because heart rate has not reached 90% in the first minute. Review the peak and shape after each repetition. [HIIT monitoring guidance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30685470/) recommends combining objective and subjective measures rather than relying on heart rate alone.

### RPE and breathing

Long VO2max intervals commonly reach RPE 8–9/10: very hard, with only short speech possible. The first repetition should not feel like an all-out finish. RPE should rise across a well-paced set even if power stays similar.

The useful combination is:

- power to pace the work now;
- RPE and breathing to judge whole-body cost;
- heart rate to review the delayed response; and
- repeatability to decide whether the dose was realistic.

Research comparing traditional intensity anchors with physiological thresholds found that the anchor changes average training response ([Meyler et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39538060/)). The percentage is not the athlete.

## How much recovery between intervals?

Recovery serves the next work bout. Equal work and recovery is easy to remember, not a universal law.

- A first 3- or 4-minute session can use 3–4 minutes easy.
- Long 8-minute efforts may use 2–4 minutes depending on the intended density and rider.
- A 30/15 session deliberately uses incomplete 15-second recovery inside a set, then longer recovery between sets.

The 2026 acute systematic review found no overall difference between active and passive recovery for time near VO2max. That does not make recovery irrelevant; it means no single mode won that pooled outcome. Use easy pedalling when it is comfortable and safe, and enough recovery to perform the next bout as intended.

## How often should cyclists do VO2max intervals?

One session per week is a conservative starting point. Some trials use two. That does not establish two as the optimum for every cyclist or a third as automatically harmful.

A [2025 FITT review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40247924/) describes how frequency, intensity, duration and type interact and notes that adaptations may occur with frequencies as low as once or twice weekly. The rest of the plan determines whether the rider can absorb the dose.

Count all hard work:

- races and fast group rides;
- running or rowing intervals;
- demanding strength sessions;
- hard climbs hidden inside endurance rides; and
- work, sleep and life stress that affect recovery.

Add a second VO2max session only when the first remains repeatable, easy riding stays easy, motivation and sleep are stable, and the second session has a deliberate role.

## How long should a VO2max block last?

There is no biological switch that flips at four or six weeks. Studies choose intervention windows so they can test a question. Their calendar is not automatically an individual prescription.

Use a block long enough to learn and progress the session, but stop or change it when:

- the event demands a more specific focus;
- repeated sessions deteriorate despite appropriate recovery;
- easy days no longer restore normal function;
- pain, illness or concerning symptoms appear; or
- the rider no longer needs this limiter prioritised.

A useful review point is every three to four weeks. Decide from execution, recovery, event timing and the wider plan rather than a claim that the adaptation “expires”.

## A four-step progression

1. **Repeat the session:** improve pacing before adding load.
2. **Add one repetition:** increase accumulated work without changing intensity.
3. **Lengthen the work slightly:** for example, move 4 × 3 to 4 × 4 only when that still serves the job.
4. **Change format or frequency last:** 30/15s, 4×8s or a second day change more than one part of the weekly stress.

Do not add repetitions, raise power, shorten recovery and add another day in the same week. The resulting fatigue cannot tell you which change worked.

Progress can appear as more even power, the same work at lower RPE, better repeatability after a hard race phase or normal recovery from a dose that previously disrupted the week. It does not have to be a guaranteed percentage gain in VO2max or FTP.

## Indoor, outdoor and hill intervals

Indoor training makes timing and resistance repeatable, but cooling is part of the setup. Use a strong fan, stable bike, accessible drink and a gear or resistance mode that does not trap the rider when cadence falls.

Outdoors, choose a steady climb or uninterrupted road with safe sight lines. Turn around or descend during recovery only when it can be done without rushing or traffic risk. Do not stare at power through junctions and do not perform maximal efforts in traffic.

[Hill repeats](/blog/cycling-hill-repeats-training) can serve the same high-aerobic job when the gradient and route allow controlled work. Gravity does not guarantee the correct intensity; pacing still matters.

## VO2max intervals for cyclists over 40

Age does not create one different protocol at midnight on a birthday. Training history, health, power profile, current load and recovery matter more than a single age cutoff.

Masters riders may choose fewer repetitions, one weekly session or more recovery because that produces better repeatability—not because every rider over 40 needs a fixed 72-hour rule. The [VO2max workouts for cyclists over 40](/blog/vo2-max-workouts-cyclists-over-40) page owns that narrower decision.

## When to stop and seek help

End the hard work for chest pain, faintness, unusual breathlessness, new sharp pain, loss of bike control or a rapidly worsening movement pattern. Severe, unexplained or persistent symptoms need appropriate medical assessment.

Hard breathing and leg discomfort are normal in severe-intensity work; the internet cannot diagnose the boundary for an individual. A rider with known cardiovascular, respiratory or metabolic disease, or returning after a long inactive period, may need individual clinical advice before unsupervised vigorous training.

If the problem is simply an ambitious target, lower power, extend recovery or end the set. Finishing a bad number is not evidence of a better stimulus.

## Claims this guide does not make

Roadman does not claim that:

- 4×4 is the most validated cycling workout;
- every VO2max effort must sit at exactly 106–120% FTP;
- heart rate must cross 90% for the session to count;
- 30/15 is always better than long intervals;
- two weekly sessions are universally optimal;
- a block must stop after four to six weeks; or
- a particular session guarantees a 5–8% gain.

The evidence supports multiple workable formats and meaningful average improvements. It also shows small samples, protocol dependence and individual variation. A trusted cycling knowledge layer should preserve both halves of that sentence.

You trained for this one. Sixteen weeks of structured intervals, long Sunday rides in the rain, early mornings on the turbo when it was still dark outside. You tapered. You carb-loaded. You pinned your number on straight. And then somewhere around hour three, the wheels fell off — not because your fitness failed, but because your fuelling did.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about race day. The gap between a good finish and a terrible one is almost never fitness. Not at the amateur level. It is almost always nutrition. Specifically, it is a small collection of predictable, preventable mistakes that riders make over and over again, often without realising what went wrong until they are sat in the car park afterwards, staring at a result that does not match the training they put in.

I have seen every one of these mistakes play out across our community, across 1,400-plus podcast episodes, and in conversations with sports nutritionists like Asker Jeukendrup and Sam Impey, who has fuelled World Tour riders through three-week Grand Tours. The pattern is remarkably consistent. The mistakes are the same. And the good news is that every single one of them is fixable.

This is not a general [cycling nutrition](/topics/cycling-nutrition) guide. We have covered [training nutrition mistakes](/blog/cycling-nutrition-mistakes-kill-performance) and [race-day fuelling strategy](/blog/race-day-fuelling-strategy-cycling-guide) elsewhere. This is specifically about what goes wrong on race day, why it goes wrong, and what to do instead.

## Mistake 1: Starting to Eat Too Late

This is the most common race-day fuelling error, and it is the one that causes the most damage.

The logic seems reasonable. You feel fine at the start. Your legs are fresh, you have energy, your glycogen stores are full from the carb-loading you did yesterday. So you ride. You get into the rhythm, find your group, settle into the pace. Eating feels unnecessary. An hour passes. Maybe ninety minutes. Then you remember you should probably have a gel. You have one. Then another thirty minutes later. By hour three, the bonk arrives and you cannot understand why — you were eating, weren't you?

Here's where it gets really interesting. Glycogen depletion does not work like a fuel gauge that drops gradually from full to empty. It follows a curve with a cliff at the end. You feel completely normal at 40 per cent glycogen. You feel slightly heavy at 25 per cent. And then at around 15-20 per cent, the lights go out. By the time you feel hungry on a bike — properly hungry, not just peckish — your stores are already significantly drawn down. And because your gut can only absorb 60-90g of carbohydrate per hour (even with a trained gut and a glucose-fructose mix), you cannot eat your way out of a deep glycogen hole mid-race. The maths simply does not work.

Jeukendrup's work at Birmingham and later at the Gatorade Sports Science Institute established this clearly: fuelling must begin early and continue at regular intervals. Not when hunger signals arrive. Not when you feel like you need it. From the first 15-20 minutes of the event.

**The fix:** Set a repeating timer on your bike computer for every 20 minutes. When it beeps, eat something. A gel, a few swallows of drink mix, a bite of rice cake — whatever you have practised. Do not wait for signals from your body. By the time those signals arrive, you are already behind. Front-load your fuelling in the first two hours and you will still have legs in the fifth.

## Mistake 2: Testing New Products on Race Day

You know this one. Everyone knows this one. And yet, on every race morning in every car park in the country, someone is unwrapping a gel they have never tasted before because a mate handed it to them, or the expo had free samples, or the brand looked professional on Instagram.

Your gut is not a passive tube that processes whatever you drop into it. It is an adaptive organ with specific transporters — SGLT1 for glucose, GLUT5 for fructose — that upregulate in response to repeated exposure. Jeukendrup's research showed that the gut requires 5-10 exposures to a specific carbohydrate type and concentration before transport capacity is fully adapted. That adaptation is product-specific. A gel with a 2:1 glucose-to-fructose ratio at 40g per serving is a different stimulus to a gel with a 1:0.8 ratio at 30g per serving.

Sam Impey, who manages fuelling for professional teams, makes the point that even at the World Tour level — where riders have sports science support, personal dietitians, and access to every product on the market — nobody introduces a new product on a race day. The protocol is absolute: test in training, confirm at race intensity, then deploy. No exceptions.

Under race-day stress, with adrenaline constricting blood flow to the gut, with intensity higher than most training rides, and with three hours of effort ahead of you, an untested product is not a small risk. It is the kind of variable that turns a target finish into a survival march.

**The fix:** Build your race nutrition kit at least six weeks before your event. Use those exact products on every hard training ride during that block. Test them during threshold sessions and long rides — not on easy coffee spins where your gut is barely working. Know how your stomach responds to each product at intensity, at the volumes you will use, in the conditions you will face. Race day is execution. Nothing new.

## Mistake 3: Under-Fuelling by 30-40 Per Cent

Let me break this down with real numbers, because this is the mistake that quietly wrecks more race days than any dramatic error.

At moderate-to-high race intensity — say 70-85 per cent of your FTP — your muscles are burning somewhere between 60 and 90 grams of carbohydrate per hour. Your liver and muscle glycogen stores hold roughly 400-500g in total, depending on your size and how well you loaded. Without eating, that is empty between hour three and hour four.

Now look at what most amateur cyclists actually consume during a race. One gel every 40-45 minutes. Maybe half a banana from a feed station. Perhaps some energy drink, sipped sporadically. Add it up and you are looking at 25-35g of carbohydrate per hour. Against a burn rate of 60-90g per hour.

That is a deficit of 30-60g every single hour. Across a four-hour sportive, you have under-fuelled by 120-240g of carbohydrate. That is the equivalent of an entire day's glycogen stores. And then you wonder why your legs stopped working on the last climb.

Jeukendrup's research established the 60-90g per hour target for events lasting longer than 2.5 hours, using a multiple transportable carbohydrate approach (glucose plus fructose). For events over four hours, some current research supports pushing towards 90-120g per hour. These are not theoretical numbers for professionals. These are the quantities your muscles need at the intensities you are racing at.

**The fix:** Calculate your hourly target before race day. For most riders in events over 2.5 hours, that is 60-90g per hour of mixed glucose and fructose. Map that onto specific products — two gels plus 500ml of drink mix per hour, for example, gives you roughly 70-80g depending on the brand. Write it on your top tube. Practise it. Hit it. The [Fuel Planner](/tools/fuel-planner) will calculate your personalised numbers based on weight, duration, and intensity. Use it.

## Mistake 4: The Wrong Pre-Race Breakfast

Race morning is where nerves and nutrition collide, and the collision is rarely pretty.

Some riders skip breakfast entirely because they are too anxious to eat. Others eat too close to the start — wolfing down porridge 45 minutes before the gun and then spending the first hour with a sloshing, heavy stomach. And a surprising number eat the wrong things altogether — a full fry-up because "I need the energy," or a high-fibre muesli because "it's healthy."

The pre-race meal has one job: top off liver glycogen, which depletes overnight while you sleep. Your muscle glycogen is largely unaffected by the overnight fast (it was loaded in the 48 hours prior), but liver stores drop by 30-50 per cent. That liver glycogen is what maintains blood glucose during the early stages of the race. Skip that top-up and you start with a partially empty tank.

The timing matters precisely. Eat 2.5-3 hours before the start. This allows gastric emptying to complete and blood glucose to stabilise after the insulin response to the meal. Eating within 60 minutes of the start can actually cause a transient dip in blood glucose as insulin peaks right when you begin exercising — the so-called reactive hypoglycaemia effect. You feel sluggish, light-headed, and weak in the opening kilometres. Not because you are unfit, but because your blood sugar is in a temporary trough.

**The fix:** Eat a carbohydrate-rich, low-fibre, low-fat breakfast 2.5-3 hours before the start. Target 1-2g of carbohydrate per kilogram of bodyweight. Porridge made with water and honey, white toast with jam, or plain white rice with a drizzle of maple syrup all work. Then, in the 15-20 minutes before the start, have a small top-up: half a gel, a few sips of drink mix, or a couple of jelly sweets. This last-minute carbohydrate bypasses the reactive hypoglycaemia window because the exercise itself blunts the insulin response. This is the protocol — and it should be one you have rehearsed before every hard training ride in your build-up block.

## Mistake 5: Ignoring Your Sweat Rate

Most cyclists have a rough sense that they should drink during a race. Very few know how much they should drink. And almost none of them have actually measured their sweat rate.

Here is where most riders get caught out: sweat rate varies enormously between individuals. Two riders of the same weight, riding the same course in the same conditions, can lose anywhere from 500ml to 2 litres of sweat per hour. Genetics, acclimatisation, fitness level, and body mass all play a role. So does the humidity, which affects how efficiently your sweat evaporates.

A 2 per cent loss in body mass from dehydration — that is 1.5kg for a 75kg rider — reduces endurance performance measurably. Cardiac output drops because blood volume decreases. Core temperature rises because there is less fluid for thermoregulation. Perceived exertion climbs. By 3-4 per cent body mass loss, you are in trouble — cognitive function declines, reaction time slows, and the risk of heat illness rises.

But the fix is not "drink as much as possible." Over-drinking carries its own risks, which we will get to in mistake eight. The fix is to know your number.

**The fix:** Measure your sweat rate in training. Weigh yourself nude before a ride. Ride for 60-90 minutes at race intensity. Weigh yourself nude after, and add back the weight of any fluid you consumed during the ride. The difference, adjusted for ride duration, is your sweat rate per hour. Do this in different conditions — cool, warm, hot — and you will have a personal hydration target that replaces guesswork with data. For most riders in temperate conditions, 500-750ml per hour is a reasonable starting range. In the heat, 750-1000ml. Match your bottles and your drinking schedule to your number, not to how you feel.

## Mistake 6: Relying on Feed Stations Alone

Feed stations are a safety net. They are not a fuelling plan.

What most people do: rock up to the start with two bottles and a single gel, assuming the organisers will provide everything they need at the feed zones. What actually works: carrying your entire fuelling plan on your person, with feed stations as a bonus.

The problems with feed stations are practical, not theoretical. You do not know what products they will stock — and whatever they stock, you almost certainly have not trained with it (see mistake two). The queues can cost you minutes you did not plan for. The spacing is designed for the average rider, not for your specific fuelling schedule. And in mass-participation events, the first riders through often strip the tables, leaving later riders with whatever is left.

Sam Impey has spoken about how World Tour teams carry their own nutrition in musettes and on the team car, despite having dedicated soigneurs at the roadside. If the best-supported riders in the world do not rely on external provision, neither should you.

**The fix:** Carry enough fuel for the entire event in your jersey pockets and on your frame. For a four-hour sportive at 70g per hour, that is 280g of carbohydrate — roughly eight gels or the equivalent in drink mix and bars. It sounds like a lot. It fits in three jersey pockets with room for a phone and a mini pump. Use feed stations for water only — refill your bottles, top up your bidon, and keep moving. Your fuel is yours. Do not outsource it to a folding table at kilometre 65.

## Mistake 7: Too Much Fibre, Fat, or Protein on Race Morning

This mistake usually comes from good intentions. You eat "healthy." Whole grains, seeds, nuts, eggs, avocado, Greek yoghurt with granola. On any normal day, this is excellent nutrition. On race morning, it is a recipe for gastrointestinal disaster.

Here's why. Fibre, fat, and protein all slow gastric emptying — the rate at which food leaves your stomach and enters the small intestine. That is a useful property when you want sustained energy release across a normal day. It is a terrible property when you need rapid carbohydrate absorption during a race.

During high-intensity exercise, blood flow to the gut drops by 60-80 per cent as the body redirects circulation to working muscles. Whatever is in your stomach when you start racing is being processed by a digestive system running on a fraction of its normal capacity. A high-fibre, high-fat breakfast sits there, half-digested, creating bloating, cramping, nausea, and the urgent need to find the nearest portaloo at the worst possible moment.

Jeukendrup's lab consistently identified pre-race meals high in fibre and fat as the strongest predictors of GI distress during endurance events. Not the gels. Not the drink mix. The breakfast.

**The fix:** Race-morning nutrition should be boring, predictable, and almost entirely carbohydrate. White bread, not wholemeal. Porridge made with water, not with full-fat milk and a mountain of nuts and seeds. Jam, honey, or maple syrup for flavour. No eggs, no bacon, no avocado, no high-fibre cereals. Save those for the recovery meal. The best race breakfasts are the ones nobody photographs for Instagram — bland, white, sugary, and effective.

## Mistake 8: Drinking Too Much Plain Water

Dehydration gets all the attention. Over-hydration gets almost none. And it can be considerably more dangerous.

When you drink large volumes of plain water during a long race without replacing the sodium you are losing through sweat, the sodium concentration in your blood drops. This is called hyponatraemia, and it is not a theoretical risk. A landmark 2005 study in the New England Journal of Medicine found that 13 per cent of Boston Marathon finishers had clinically significant hyponatraemia. The primary risk factor was not heat or distance — it was drinking more fluid than the body could process, without adequate sodium.

Timothy Noakes has written extensively about this — the old advice to "drink before you are thirsty" and "drink as much as you can tolerate" came largely from sports drink industry marketing, not from physiology. Drinking to thirst is a better baseline for most riders. But even drinking to thirst can cause problems if you are only drinking plain water.

What most people do: fill their bottles with plain water, drink constantly, and assume hydration is handled. What actually works: drinking a sodium-containing solution, at a rate matched to your sweat losses, with electrolytes replacing what you are sweating out.

Your sweat contains 500-1,500mg of sodium per litre. If you are sweating at a litre per hour and replacing that with plain water, you are diluting your blood sodium by a measurable amount every hour. Over four or five hours, that dilution can produce confusion, nausea, swelling, headache, and in extreme cases, seizures.

**The fix:** Never race with plain water. Every bottle should contain 500-1,000mg of sodium per litre, delivered via electrolyte tabs, drink mix, or a pinch of salt. Consider sodium loading in the 24-48 hours before a hot-weather event — an extra 1,500-2,000mg spread across the day before the race, with food and additional electrolyte drinks. During the race, match your fluid intake to your measured sweat rate (see mistake five) and include sodium in every sip. ## Mistake 9: Forgetting Caffeine Timing

Caffeine is the most evidence-backed legal ergogenic aid in sport. The performance benefit is well-established — a meta-analysis by Southward et al. found an average improvement of 3-4 per cent in endurance performance. For a rider finishing a sportive in four hours, that is seven to ten minutes. Free speed.

And most cyclists either ignore it, use it wrong, or time it so badly that they get the side effects without the benefit.

Here's how caffeine works in performance terms. It blocks adenosine receptors in the brain, reducing perceived exertion — the effort feels easier at the same power output. It increases free fatty acid mobilisation, potentially sparing glycogen early in a ride. And it has a direct effect on muscle contractility at the neuromuscular junction. Peak blood concentration occurs 45-60 minutes after ingestion. That is the ergogenic window — the period when you get maximum benefit.

What most people do: slam an espresso in the car park ten minutes before the start, then take a caffeinated gel at random points during the race. What actually works: dosing by body weight, timing to peak concentration, and matching your race-day protocol to what you have practised in training.

The effective dose, based on the literature, is 3-6mg per kilogram of body weight. For a 75kg rider, that is 225-450mg — roughly two to four espressos, or three to five caffeinated gels (depending on the brand). But you do not take it all at once. You time it.

**The fix:** Take your primary caffeine dose 45-60 minutes before the start — this can be strong coffee, caffeine tablets, or a caffeinated gel. That puts peak blood concentration right at the gun. Then top up with smaller doses (50-100mg) every two hours during the race to maintain the effect without overshooting into jitteriness, gut distress, or cardiac stress. If you are caffeine-sensitive, start with 3mg per kilogram and assess your response in training. If you drink coffee daily, you will need the higher end of the range. And critically — test your race-day caffeine protocol in training. The last thing you want is to discover at kilometre 40 that 400mg on a nervous stomach makes you feel like your heart is trying to escape your chest.

## Mistake 10: Ignoring the Post-Race Window

You crossed the line. You stopped your Garmin. You are stood in the finish area, hands on knees, trying to remember what normal breathing feels like. The last thing on your mind is eating.

This is a mistake, and it matters more than most riders realise — particularly if you are racing a multi-day event, have another race within the week, or simply want to train well in the days that follow.

The post-exercise glycogen resynthesis window is not a myth. It is a well-documented physiological reality. The enzyme glycogen synthase, which drives glycogen storage in muscle, is most active in the first 30-60 minutes after exercise. Ivy et al. demonstrated back in 1988 that glycogen resynthesis rates are roughly 50 per cent higher in this window compared to two hours later. Miss it and you are starting your next effort with a partially depleted tank that takes significantly longer to refill.

This is especially relevant for stage races, multi-day sportives, and any weekend where you are racing Saturday and riding Sunday. The difference between recovering 80 per cent of your glycogen and recovering 50 per cent is the difference between functioning legs on day two and legs that quit on the first climb.

**The fix:** Have your recovery nutrition packed and ready in your transition bag or car. Not a protein shake. Not a recovery drink alone. Real food, eaten within 30 minutes of finishing. Target 1-1.2g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight and 20-30g of protein. A sandwich with lean meat, a rice bowl with chicken, or even chocolate milk and a banana will do the job. Prepare it the night before so there is zero friction. Your body is primed to absorb and store — do not waste that window standing around chatting while your glycogen synthase activity drops by the minute.

## Your Race-Day Fuelling Checklist

Ten mistakes. All of them fixable. Here is the summary you can screenshot and pin to the fridge before your next event.

1. **Start eating within 20 minutes** — set a timer, do not wait for hunger
2. **Nothing new on race day** — every product tested at race intensity in training
3. **Hit 60-90g carbs per hour** — calculate it, map it to specific products, write it on your top tube
4. **Eat breakfast 2.5-3 hours before the start** — carb-rich, low-fibre, low-fat, familiar
5. **Know your sweat rate** — measure it, match your fluid intake to it
6. **Carry your own fuel** — feed stations are a backup, not a plan
7. **Keep race morning boring** — white carbs, no fibre, no fat, no protein-heavy meals
8. **Add sodium to every bottle** — 500-1,000mg per litre, never plain water during a race
9. **Time your caffeine** — primary dose 45-60 minutes pre-start, top-ups every two hours
10. **Eat within 30 minutes of finishing** — 1-1.2g/kg carbs plus 20-30g protein, prepared in advance

Not one of these requires a coaching qualification, a sports science degree, or expensive equipment. They require planning, testing, and the discipline to execute what you have practised. The training is the hard part. The fuelling should be the easy part — as long as you have done the homework.

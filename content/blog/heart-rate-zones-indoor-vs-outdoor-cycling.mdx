You know the feeling. Saturday morning, out on the road, 135 bpm, nose-breathing, legs turning over, proper Zone 2. Sunday, you get on the turbo, ride at the same 135 bpm, and twenty minutes in you're breathing through your mouth, legs are filling up, and the session feels nothing like easy.

Or the opposite. You try to hit your outdoor threshold numbers on the trainer and you can't get your heart rate there no matter how hard you push.

The numbers don't lie. But they don't mean the same thing in both places.

## The 5–10 beat gap

For most riders, heart rate runs 5–10 bpm lower on a stationary trainer than it does outdoors at the same power output. Professor Stephen Seiler has talked about this on the podcast — the heart rate response is context-dependent, not just effort-dependent. Your body doesn't produce the same cardiovascular demand in a static indoor environment as it does on the road.

This isn't a flaw in your fitness. It's physiology doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

## Why it happens

There are four main reasons your heart rate behaves differently on the trainer, and knowing them means you stop second-guessing every indoor session.

**Less muscle recruitment.** On the road, you're steering, braking, shifting your weight through corners, standing over rough surfaces, gripping the bars through crosswinds. Your upper body is working constantly. On the trainer, the bike doesn't move. You sit and pedal. Fewer muscles working means less oxygen demand, which means the heart doesn't need to pump as hard to sustain the same wattage at the pedals.

**No micro-surges.** Outdoor riding is full of tiny accelerations you don't even notice — responding to a gust, closing a gap, punching over a rise, adjusting speed for a corner. Each one spikes heart rate briefly and keeps the average elevated. On the trainer, effort is metronomically steady. No surges, no spikes, no accumulated cardiac cost from constant small adjustments.

**Lower adrenaline.** Traffic, descents, group dynamics, dogs, potholes — the road keeps your sympathetic nervous system slightly activated. That baseline adrenaline response elevates heart rate by a few beats even before effort enters the equation. Your garage doesn't trigger the same response. Hopefully.

**Vibration and proprioception.** Road surface vibration travels through the bike and into your body. Your muscles constantly make micro-corrections to absorb it. It's a small energy cost, but it's real, and it contributes to the total cardiovascular load that doesn't exist on a locked-in trainer.

## The heat wildcard

Here's where it gets interesting. Everything above assumes decent cooling. If your indoor setup is a back bedroom with one small fan and the door closed, the script flips completely.

In a hot, poorly ventilated room, your heart rate will be *higher* indoors than outdoors at the same power. Your body diverts blood to the skin for cooling, which reduces the volume available for your working muscles, and the heart compensates by beating faster. This is thermal cardiac drift, and over the course of a session it can add 10–15 bpm on top of your normal response — sometimes more if you're doing longer efforts in a badly ventilated room.

One of the things we've talked about a lot on the podcast is how much riders underestimate cooling. A proper fan setup — a strong floor fan aimed at your torso, not a desk fan across the room — closes the gap significantly. If your indoor heart rate is consistently higher than outdoor at the same power, fix the cooling before you change your zones.

## What this means for your training

If you're training by heart rate and using a single set of zones for both environments, one of your two training contexts is wrong.

Using your outdoor zones on the trainer means your easy rides are too hard and your hard rides are too easy. Your Zone 2 is actually tempo. Your threshold intervals are sitting at sweetspot. The sessions don't hit what they're designed to hit, and over time you end up in exactly the grey zone that Seiler's research says gets you the least return for the hours you put in.

Using your indoor zones outside means the opposite problem — your easy rides are genuinely easy (fine), but your intervals don't reach the intensity that stimulates the adaptation you're after.

I see this all the time in the community. A rider will post asking why their winter trainer block didn't produce results when they got back outside — they'd been doing three months of "Zone 2" indoors using outdoor numbers. The effort felt about right, so they didn't question it. But they were sitting at tempo the whole time, never building that aerobic foundation, and wondering why the spring races felt harder than they should.

The fix is straightforward. Test in both environments. Set two zone tables.

## How to set indoor and outdoor zones

You don't need a lab. You need a threshold test in each environment, done within the same week, with similar rest and fuelling.

**Outdoor:** Find a steady 20–30 minute effort — a climb or a flat stretch you can ride uninterrupted. Go as hard as you can sustain for 20 minutes. Take the average heart rate for that effort and multiply by 0.95 — that gives you your approximate lactate threshold heart rate outdoors. The 5% discount accounts for the fact that you can push higher over 20 minutes than you could hold for a full hour. Build your zones from that number.

**Indoor:** Same test on the trainer within a couple of days. Same warm-up. Same 20-minute max effort. Apply the same 0.95 multiplier. The number will almost certainly be lower. That's your indoor threshold. Build a separate set of zones from it.

TrainingPeaks and Garmin Connect both let you set separate heart rate zones for indoor and outdoor activities. It takes five minutes to configure and it means every session you do from that point on is actually hitting the right stimulus.

If you re-test every 8–12 weeks (which you should be doing anyway), update both.

## When the gap narrows

The 5–10 beat gap isn't constant. It shrinks at higher intensities. At VO2max effort, your cardiovascular system is working so hard that environmental differences get overwhelmed by sheer demand. The heart rate ceiling is the heart rate ceiling whether you're on the turbo or halfway up a mountain.

This means the zone discrepancy matters most at the intensities where most of your training happens — Zone 2 and tempo. Exactly where precision matters most. Riding Zone 2 at the wrong heart rate isn't a small error. Over months of base training, it's the difference between building a real aerobic base and just accumulating fatigue at an intensity that doesn't produce the adaptation you're after.

## The bigger question

Here's the thing nobody tells you about this indoor-outdoor gap. It's actually a symptom of a bigger issue — are you training with the right intensity targets in the first place?

Most riders set their zones once, maybe from a ramp test they did two years ago, and never revisit them. They wonder why training feels off but never question whether the numbers they're chasing still reflect their current fitness.

Your zones should be a living document. They change as you get fitter, as you age, as your weight shifts, as you stack more years on the bike. An indoor-outdoor mismatch is one version of this problem, but it's rarely the only one.

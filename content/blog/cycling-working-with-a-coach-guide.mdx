You hired a coach. Brilliant. You compared credentials, checked references, asked the right questions, signed up, and felt that wave of commitment — the sense that now things are going to be different. The plan landed in your TrainingPeaks calendar. The structure was there. The accountability was there. Everything was going to click.

Then six weeks later, something felt off. Maybe the plan didn't quite fit your schedule. Maybe you weren't sure whether your coach really understood what was going on in your life. Maybe the responses to your session notes felt generic. Maybe you started doing sessions differently than prescribed and didn't say anything about it, because you didn't want to seem difficult. The coaching relationship drifted from partnership toward polite obligation, and the money kept going out.

I've watched this happen hundreds of times. Not because the coaches were bad, and not because the athletes were lazy. It happened because nobody teaches you how to be coached. There's plenty of advice on finding a coach — we've covered that in the [choosing a cycling coach guide](/blog/best-online-cycling-coach-how-to-choose). But finding a coach is the easy part. Working with one effectively is the skill that determines whether the investment pays off, and almost nobody talks about it.

## Hiring a coach is not the hard part — being coached is

Here's what nobody tells you about coaching. The quality of your results depends more on your behaviour as an athlete than on the quality of the plan you receive.

That sounds wrong. You're paying someone for their expertise, so surely their expertise is the primary variable. But think about what a coach actually works with. They get your power files. They get your session notes. They get whatever context you volunteer about sleep, stress, nutrition, life. From that information — and only that information — they make decisions about your training.

If the information is thin, the decisions are thin. A coach looking at a power file with no session notes is reading half the story. A coach who doesn't know you slept four hours last night because your kid was sick, or that your work project has you running on cortisol, or that your left knee has been aching since Tuesday — that coach is prescribing training for a version of you that doesn't exist this week.

The athletes who get the most from coaching are not the most talented. They're the most communicative. They treat the relationship like a genuine collaboration: they share information, they ask questions, they flag problems early, they push back when something doesn't feel right. The athletes who get the least from coaching are the ones who receive the plan, execute it in silence, and expect results to appear.

This is a skill. It can be learned. And it matters more than whether your coach has a PhD or a Level 3 certification.

## The first 8-12 weeks: calibration, not magic

New athletes almost always expect too much, too fast. You sign up, you get a plan, and you expect the plan to be dialled. It won't be. It can't be. And understanding why saves the relationship.

Your new coach doesn't know you yet. They know what you told them in the onboarding questionnaire. They know your FTP from whatever test you last did. They might have a few weeks of historical data from your Garmin or Wahoo. But they don't know how you actually respond to training.

They don't know whether you absorb VO2max work easily or whether it buries you for three days. They don't know if you underreport fatigue or overreport it. They don't know that you always feel terrible on Wednesdays because that's your long day at work, or that your Saturday long ride realistically starts at 6am because the family needs you by 10. They don't know your fuelling habits, your sleep patterns under stress, or how you behave when motivation drops.

All of that gets learned across the first 8-12 weeks. The coach prescribes, you execute and report, they adjust, you execute and report again. Each cycle gives them more data about you — not just your physiology, but your patterns. By week 12, a good coach has a working model of you that's dramatically more accurate than what they started with.

This means the first two months are not the time to judge results. If your FTP hasn't moved at week 8, that is completely normal. The coach is still calibrating. The plan you're doing in week 10 is better than the plan you got in week 2, even if the numbers haven't shifted yet.

What you should be evaluating in this phase is the process. Is your coach asking good questions? Are they adjusting based on what you report? Do they explain why the plan looks the way it does? If those things are happening, the results will follow. If the plan arrives each week unchanged regardless of what you told them — that's a different conversation, and we'll get to it.

## Communication that actually helps your coach

Most athletes dramatically underestimate how much their coach needs to hear from them. You think the power file tells the story. It doesn't. It tells part of the story — the mechanical part. The subjective part is where coaching decisions actually get made.

### Post-session feedback

After every key session — intervals, threshold work, anything structured — your coach needs four things from you:

**How it felt on a 1-10 scale.** Not how it went in watts. How it felt. A session where you hit every number but felt like you were dying is different information than a session where you hit every number and had more in the tank. The power file says the same thing; the subjective rating says different things entirely.

**Sleep quality the night before.** One sentence. "Slept well, 7 hours" or "Terrible night, maybe 5 hours broken." Your coach can't see this in the data, and it changes the interpretation of everything.

**Life context.** A brief note on anything relevant. Work stress. Travel. Family stuff. Illness. Even positive stress counts — a house move, a new job, a holiday. Your body doesn't distinguish between good stress and bad stress; your coach needs to know both.

**Whether you could have gone harder or were holding on.** This tells your coach whether the prescription was right. If every session is a death march, the intensity is too high. If every session feels comfortable, there's room to push. Neither is visible from the power file alone.

This takes 60 seconds per session. Maybe 90. And it changes what your coach can do with your data. The athletes in our [Not Done Yet programme](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) who give this kind of feedback consistently get better plans, faster adjustments, and more targeted progression than the ones who upload the file and move on.

### Weekly check-ins

Beyond session notes, a weekly message at the start of the week makes an enormous difference. Something like: "This week is heavy at work, Tuesday is probably off, I'm feeling a bit flat from last week's intensity." That gives your coach time to restructure the week before it starts, rather than reacting after you've already missed sessions or pushed through fatigue you should have flagged.

The exact channel doesn't matter — TrainingPeaks comments, WhatsApp, email, whatever you and your coach agreed on. The consistency matters. When the communication goes silent, coaches fill the gaps with assumptions. Assumptions are sometimes right, but more often they're just the coach's best guess, and guessing is not what you're paying for.

### The conversation you should have on day one

Before you do a single session with a new coach, agree on three things:

**Response time expectations.** How quickly will your coach typically respond? Within a few hours? Within 24 hours? Only on certain days? Knowing this prevents the frustration of sending a message on Tuesday and stewing until Thursday because you didn't know Thursday was their review day.

**Preferred communication channel.** Some coaches live in TrainingPeaks. Others prefer WhatsApp. Others use email. Pick one primary channel and stick with it. Scattered communication across three platforms means things get missed.

**Check-in cadence.** Weekly is the floor for effective coaching. Some athletes prefer a structured weekly call. Others prefer async messages. The format matters less than the commitment to it happening reliably.

Get this right on day one and you skip the single most common reason coaching relationships fail: mismatched expectations about communication that slowly breed resentment on both sides.

## What good coaching feedback looks like from both sides

Coaching is a two-way feedback loop. Most people think about what the coach delivers — the plan, the adjustments, the advice. Fewer think about what they should be receiving back from their coach about their own performance and trajectory.

### What your coach should be giving you

**Rationale, not just prescription.** If the plan says "4x8 minutes at 92-95% FTP," your coach should be willing and able to explain why. Not a sports science lecture — a sentence or two. "We're building sustained power at a level just below your threshold to extend time at that intensity without the recovery cost of full threshold work." If a coach can't explain why you're doing something in plain language, they either don't know or they don't respect you enough to explain. Both are problems.

**Pattern recognition.** A good coach notices things across weeks that you don't see in the moment. "Your power numbers drop every third week — we might need to restructure your recovery timing." Or: "You always report feeling bad on Mondays, but your Monday sessions are consistently your best. Your subjective read and your actual output don't match." That kind of observation is what you can't do for yourself, and it's where coaching earns its money.

**Honest assessment of progress.** Not cheerleading. Not "great session!" every week. Honest assessment. Where are you relative to your goal? What's working? What isn't? What needs to change? If your coach only tells you things are going well, they're either not paying attention or not comfortable delivering honest feedback. Neither is useful.

### What you should be giving your coach

**Honesty about compliance.** If you didn't do the session, say so. If you changed the workout mid-ride because you felt terrible, say so. If you added a hard group ride on Sunday that wasn't in the plan, say so. Coaches can work with any information. They can't work with false information. The athlete who pretends to follow the plan while doing something different is paying for coaching they're not actually receiving.

**Willingness to ask why.** You're not being difficult when you ask why a session is prescribed. You're being engaged. Good coaches welcome this because it means you're thinking about your training rather than just executing instructions. The question "Why am I doing sweet spot this week instead of threshold?" often leads to a conversation that makes you a smarter athlete.

**Early flags, not late explosions.** If something has been bothering you for three weeks — a session that doesn't fit your schedule, an intensity that consistently feels wrong, a communication gap — raise it now. Don't wait until the frustration has compounded into a conversation about whether to quit. Small adjustments made early prevent big problems later.

## Red flags that the relationship is not working

Not every coaching relationship works. Sometimes the fit is wrong. Sometimes the coaching quality is wrong. Here's how to tell the difference between normal friction and genuine problems.

### Normal friction (not a red flag)

The plan doesn't feel perfect in the first 8 weeks. Sessions occasionally feel too hard or too easy. You disagree with a specific prescription. There's a week where communication is slower than usual. Your coach pushes back on something you want to do.

All of this is normal and often productive. Friction means two people are engaging with the process. Absence of friction often means someone isn't paying attention.

### Actual red flags

**The plan never changes.** You report fatigue, stress, illness, a family crisis — and next week's plan arrives looking identical to last week's. This is the clearest signal that your feedback is not being used. A coach who doesn't adjust based on information is delivering a template, not coaching.

**No rationale, ever.** You ask why and get "trust the process" or no response at all. A coach who cannot or will not explain their reasoning is either operating on intuition alone (risky) or doesn't have a methodology to explain (worse).

**Every missed session is treated as failure.** Life happens. Kids get sick. Work goes sideways. Travel disrupts routines. A good coach treats a missed session as information — something to factor in, not something to punish. The coach who makes you feel guilty for missing a Tuesday ride because your boiler broke is confusing rigidity with discipline.

**Dismissal of your concerns.** You say something doesn't feel right. The response is "just follow the plan" with no further engagement. Your subjective experience is data. A coach who dismisses it consistently is coaching the power file, not the person.

**No progress after six months with no explanation.** Coaching isn't magic, and not every month will show gains. But after six months, your coach should be able to articulate what has improved, what hasn't, and what the plan is to address the gaps. If they can't — if it's just "keep going, it'll come" — that's a flag.

**Radio silence.** If you have to chase your coach for responses repeatedly, the relationship has already broken down. You're paying for attention and expertise. If neither is arriving consistently, the contract isn't being fulfilled.

## Self-coaching vs coached: an honest comparison

I'm not going to tell you everyone needs a coach. That's not true, and it serves coaches more than athletes. Here's the honest breakdown.

### Where self-coaching works

**Disciplined, analytically minded riders** who actually enjoy learning about training science can self-coach effectively. If you read Friel, you listen to podcasts like this one, you understand periodisation, and you have the discipline to follow a structured plan through the boring phases — you can produce results that rival coached athletes.

**Experienced riders** who've been coached before and absorbed enough knowledge to apply the principles themselves. This is actually what good coaching should produce — a rider who understands the why well enough to continue independently.

**Riders with stable, predictable lives.** If your schedule doesn't change much, your stress is manageable, and your training environment is consistent, the need for real-time adjustment is lower. A well-designed annual plan can run with minimal modification.

### Where self-coaching systematically fails

**Objectivity.** You cannot see yourself clearly. This is not a character flaw — it's a cognitive reality. Self-coached riders tend to overtrain their strengths and undertrain their weaknesses because the things you're good at feel productive and the things you're bad at feel pointless. A coach sees the imbalance.

**Recovery decisions.** Self-coached riders almost universally under-recover. The decision to take an easy week when you feel okay but the data says otherwise requires a kind of detachment from your own training that most people can't maintain. Stephen Seiler's research on intensity distribution shows that even experienced athletes drift toward too much moderate intensity when left to self-regulate. A coach holds the line.

**Accountability during hard phases.** Build phases are hard. The sessions hurt, the fatigue accumulates, and the results are weeks away. This is where motivation dips, sessions get skipped, and the structure starts to fragment. External accountability — someone who notices when you're drifting — is really valuable here.

**Crisis response.** When life goes sideways — injury, illness, family emergency, career change — the self-coached rider usually does one of two things: stops completely or carries on regardless. A coach provides the third option: intelligent adaptation that keeps you in the game without making things worse.

### The middle ground

If full-time coaching isn't right for you — financially, temperamentally, or practically — structured community programmes fill a lot of the gap. The weekly coaching calls and accountability structures in communities like [Not Done Yet](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) provide the external perspective and accountability that self-coaching lacks, without the cost of individual coaching. It's not the same as having a coach in your corner full-time, but for a lot of riders it's the better fit.

## How to be a better athlete to coach

Coaches talk about this amongst themselves more than you'd think. The athletes who are a joy to coach share specific traits, and none of them are about talent or fitness.

**Be honest, especially when it's uncomfortable.** Tell your coach when you didn't follow the plan. Tell them when you went out for beers on Friday night. Tell them when you're struggling with motivation. False compliance — pretending to follow the plan while doing your own thing — wastes everyone's time and money. Your coach can only help with what they know about.

**Give context, not just data.** Your coach can see that your power was 8% down on Thursday. What they can't see is that you had a terrible argument with your partner on Wednesday night and slept three hours. That context changes their response from "we need to adjust your threshold" to "let's recover and get back at it next week." Without it, the wrong adjustment gets made.

**Do the boring work.** The athletes who only execute the exciting sessions and skip the easy rides and recovery days are self-selecting the parts of the plan that feel productive while ignoring the parts that make the productive sessions possible. If your coach prescribes an easy spin, do an easy spin. Not a "mostly easy" spin that somehow includes 20 minutes at tempo because you felt good.

**Ask questions, but respect the answer.** "Why am I doing this?" is a great question. "Why am I doing this, and also I disagree and I'm going to do something different" is not a question — it's a power struggle. Ask, listen to the reasoning, and if you still disagree after hearing the rationale, have a proper conversation about it. Don't just quietly override the plan.

**Flag schedule conflicts early.** If you know Thursday is going to be impossible next week, tell your coach on Sunday. That gives them time to restructure. Telling them on Thursday afternoon that you can't train today — and doing that repeatedly — makes their job nearly impossible and your plan chronically reactive.

**Understand that your coach has other athletes.** This sounds obvious, but the athlete who sends five WhatsApp messages on a Saturday morning expecting immediate detailed responses is not being realistic. Respect the agreed communication cadence. If you need more interaction than your current arrangement provides, have the conversation about upgrading, not the passive-aggressive message about slow responses.

## When to move on (and how to do it professionally)

Not every coaching relationship should last forever. Some of the best coaching outcomes I've seen involved an athlete working with one coach for two years, then moving to another who had a different strength that matched the athlete's evolving needs. Changing coaches is not failure. Staying with a coach who isn't working because you feel obligated — that's closer to failure.

### Signs it's time to leave

You've addressed the red flags directly and nothing changed. Communication has broken down and neither side is fixing it. Your goals have shifted to an area your coach doesn't specialise in. You've been properly stuck for six months or more and the plan forward isn't clear to either of you. You've outgrown the service level.

### How to leave well

The cycling coaching world is small. Everyone knows everyone, or knows someone who does. Leaving a coach badly creates problems you don't need.

**Be direct and honest.** "I've decided to make a change" is enough. You don't owe a detailed critique, but if you can offer specific feedback — "I needed more communication than we had" or "My goals have shifted toward ultra-endurance and I need a specialist" — most coaches appreciate hearing it. It helps them improve.

**Give reasonable notice.** Most coaching agreements have a notice period. Honour it. Even if they don't, giving two to four weeks allows for a clean transition.

**Don't ghost.** Disappearing is the worst option. The coach is left wondering what happened, potentially worrying about you, and unable to learn from the situation. A brief, honest message is all it takes.

**Take your data with you.** Download your training history before the transition. Your next coach benefits from seeing your historical data, and your current coach should support that. If they won't share your data, that tells you something about the relationship you're leaving.

## The goal: a coach should make you smarter, not dependent

Here's the test for whether coaching is working at the deepest level. After a year with your coach, do you understand your training better than you did before? Can you look at your plan and understand why it's structured the way it is? Can you make an intelligent decision about an individual session when your coach isn't available?

If the answer is yes, you're in a good coaching relationship. Your coach is teaching you while training you. You're becoming a more informed athlete, not just a more compliant one.

If the answer is no — if you understand less about your training after a year of coaching than you did going in, if every decision requires the coach's input, if you couldn't function for a week without them — something has gone wrong. You've become dependent on the coach rather than developed by them.

The best coaches I've had on the podcast — people like [Dan Lorang](/guests/dan-lorang), [Joe Friel](/guests/joe-friel), coaches who've worked at the World Tour level and with amateur athletes — all express the same philosophy in different words. The goal of coaching is to make the athlete better at the whole process, not just fitter. That includes understanding why the training works, recognising the signals from their own body, and developing judgment about the daily decisions that no coach can make for them.

A rider who leaves coaching after two years and can self-coach effectively using what they learned — that's a coaching success story. A rider who stays coached for ten years and still can't explain why they're doing threshold intervals on a Tuesday — that's not coaching. That's plan delivery with a human interface.

Your coach should be making you smarter. You should understand more about training, recovery, fuelling, and your own physiology after every month of coaching. That accumulated understanding is the real return on your investment — not the watts, not the FTP number, not the race result. Those are downstream products of the understanding.

The athletes who get the most from coaching are the ones who bring curiosity, honesty, and engagement to the relationship. They treat their coach as a collaborator, not an authority. They communicate consistently, flag problems early, ask why, and do the boring work between the exciting sessions. They understand that the first few months are calibration, that communication is more important than compliance, and that the goal is to become a better, more self-aware athlete — not to outsource their thinking to someone else.

That's what working with a coach actually looks like. Not a plan in your inbox and a charge on your card. A genuine partnership between two people trying to make you faster, smarter, and more durable on the bike. If you can build that, the investment pays for itself several times over.

Your last event was three weeks ago. You know it was three weeks ago because you have been counting. And in those three weeks, you have not missed a single training day.

You are not following a plan, exactly. There is no specific goal attached to what you are doing. But you are riding. You are on the turbo when it rains. You are watching your CTL on TrainingPeaks and telling yourself that if you just hold this number through October, you will start January ahead of everyone else.

Here is the thing nobody tells you: the riders who train through October do not arrive at January ahead. They arrive tired, stale, and wondering why they feel flat when the real work begins. The riders who stopped — properly stopped, for two to four weeks — are the ones who come back hungry, fresh, and capable of absorbing the training that actually matters.

I have been the rider who could not stop. I have had every conversation with myself about maintaining fitness, about getting a head start, about how I would regret taking time off. And I have spoken to enough coaches, sports scientists, and experienced riders on the podcast to know that the evidence is not even close. The off-season is not a gap in your training. It is training. And skipping it has a cost that most riders do not recognise until it is too late.

## Why You Cannot Stop

Let me break this down, because this is not a willpower problem. There are specific reasons riders struggle with rest, and understanding them is the first step toward doing something about it.

### Your Identity Is Tied to the Bike

This is the big one. For a lot of us — and I am including myself here — cycling is not just a hobby. It is a core part of who we are. It shapes our social lives, our daily routines, our sense of self-worth. When you strip that away, even for a couple of weeks, it feels like losing something fundamental.

The morning alarm goes off and there is no session to do. The group chat is quiet. The structure that organised your week has disappeared. And in that gap, a small voice starts asking uncomfortable questions about what you are if you are not the person who rides.

That discomfort is real. It is also completely normal. And it passes — usually within about five days, once you stop reaching for the routine and start noticing all the space that has opened up.

### Strava Is Watching

Strava is brilliant for motivation during the season. It is terrible for your head during the off-season. Every ride your mates post is a reminder that you are not riding. Every weekly summary that shows a zero feels like falling behind. The leaderboard does not have an off-season column, so it looks — to anyone scrolling past — like you just stopped.

The fix is simple and uncomfortable: log out. Delete the app for two weeks if you have to. You are not going to miss anything that matters. Nobody remembers who topped the local KOM table in November. What they remember is who showed up sharp in March.

### Fear of Fitness Loss

This is the one that gets the most rational-sounding justification. You have numbers. You have watched your FTP rise over six months. You know — because your training platform tells you in alarming red graphics — that your fitness score is declining. Every day off feels like it is costing you something you earned.

I am going to give you the actual numbers in a moment, and they are going to make you feel better. But first, understand what is happening psychologically. You are confusing the number with the thing it represents. Your CTL is a rolling average of training stress. It is supposed to drop when you stop training. That is what it is designed to do. It is not measuring your fitness. It is measuring your recent workload. Those are very different things.

### FOMO — Everyone Else Is Still Going

Your club is still riding on Saturdays. Your training partner is posting intervals on Instagram. The forum you follow is full of people talking about their winter plans. It feels like the rest of the world is pushing forward while you sit still.

Here is what they are not posting: the flat first six weeks of their January training. The illness that wiped February. The niggling injury that turns up in March because they never let their body properly recover. You are comparing your rest to their highlight reel.

## What Actually Happens to Your Fitness

Right. Let us talk numbers, because this is where the fear meets the evidence and the evidence wins.

### The First Two Weeks

VO2max — your maximum aerobic capacity, the ceiling of your endurance engine — drops roughly 3 to 5 per cent after two weeks of complete inactivity. Not reduced training. Complete inactivity. Lying on the sofa, doing nothing at all.

Your FTP holds remarkably well for the first 10 to 14 days. The neuromuscular pathways that let you produce power do not disappear in a fortnight. They are deeply encoded from months of training.

Plasma volume drops within the first few days, which is why you might feel sluggish on that first ride back. This is not fitness loss. It is a fluid shift. It comes back within three to five days of riding.

### Weeks Two to Four

VO2max decline accelerates slightly, reaching roughly 5 to 7 per cent after four weeks. Your FTP starts to soften — typically a decline of 5 to 10 watts depending on where you started.

Mitochondrial density — the engine room of your aerobic system — is remarkably sticky. It takes months of complete inactivity to significantly reduce the mitochondrial adaptations you have built over years of training. Four weeks barely touches them.

### The Rebuild

And here is where it gets really interesting. Previously trained athletes do not rebuild fitness from scratch. The research consistently shows that returning to your pre-break level takes roughly half the time of the break itself, often less. Two weeks off? You are back within seven to ten days of structured riding. Four weeks off? Two to three weeks gets you to where you were.

Compare that to the cost of skipping the off-season: accumulated fatigue that you carry into your first training block, suppressed immune function that means you catch every cold going around, reduced motivation that turns intervals into a chore, and a body that simply cannot absorb the training load you are asking it to handle.

The maths is straightforward. A short-term dip that rebuilds in days versus a season-long drag that never fully clears. The off-season is not a cost. It is a trade — and it is the best one you will make all year.

## Signs You Skipped the Off-Season Last Year

Burnout does not announce itself. It does not arrive as a single dramatic moment where you throw your bike in the garage and declare yourself finished with the sport. It is slower and quieter than that. It is a gradual erosion that you can mistake for bad form, poor luck, or just not being good enough.

Here are the markers. Be honest with yourself about whether any of them sound familiar.

**Your power is lower than twelve months ago despite consistent training.** You have been putting in the hours. You have been following a plan. And yet your numbers at the end of a training block are below where they were at the same point last year. This is the signature of accumulated fatigue — your body is spending so much energy managing the backlog of stress that there is nothing left for adaptation.

**You dread the sessions you used to look forward to.** Interval day used to feel like a challenge. Now it feels like a punishment. You find yourself checking the weather, looking for excuses to push it to tomorrow, or cutting sessions short. This is not laziness. This is your central nervous system telling you it does not have the resources to cope with what you are asking.

**You catch everything going around.** You are the first person in the office to get the cold. Every stomach bug finds you. You cannot shake infections the way you used to. Training is an immune stressor. When fatigue accumulates beyond a certain point, your immune system runs at a deficit. This is not coincidence. It is physiology.

**Your motivation comes and goes in waves.** One week you are fired up. The next you cannot be bothered. This inconsistency — the inability to maintain steady motivation over a training block — is different from the natural ebb and flow of a well-rested athlete. It is a sign that your psychological reserves are depleted.

**Rest days do not feel like enough.** You take a day off, or even two, and come back feeling roughly the same. The freshness does not arrive. You need more recovery than you used to, and even then it is not quite sufficient. This is because the fatigue is not from last week's training. It is from last year's.

If you recognise three or more of those, you are carrying a debt that daily or weekly recovery cannot clear. Only a proper off-season — real rest, genuine time away from structured training — resets the balance.

## Reframing the Off-Season: Investment, Not Loss

Here is a different way to think about it. Every season you train, you are drawing from two accounts. The first is your physical capacity — aerobic fitness, muscular endurance, power output. This account is visible. You can measure it. It has numbers attached. You watch it rise and fall on a graph.

The second account is less visible but just as important: motivation, hormonal balance, immune function, tendon resilience, joint health, mental freshness. This account does not appear on TrainingPeaks. Nobody tracks it on Strava. But it determines how much training you can absorb, how consistently you can show up, and whether you are still enjoying the sport in five years.

The off-season is when you top up the second account. You cannot do it during the season because the demands of training keep drawing it down. You cannot do it with a single rest week because the balance is too deep in the red. You need a proper block of time — two to four weeks — where the demands drop to near zero and the account starts rebuilding.

The riders who view this as losing time are looking at the wrong account. The riders who view it as an investment are the ones who arrive at January with a full battery and the ability to actually do something with it.

## What to Actually Do During the Off-Season

The off-season is not structured. That is the whole point. But "do whatever you want" is not particularly helpful advice when your default setting is to train. So here are some guidelines that give you enough framework to feel like you are doing something without falling back into the habit of structured training.

### Ride If You Want To — But Differently

The instruction is not "stop riding." It is "stop training." The difference matters.

Ride to the cafe with your mates. Go explore a road you have never been down. Take your mountain bike somewhere muddy. Do the gravel loop you have been meaning to try all year. Ride with your partner or your kids at whatever pace they set.

No power meter. No intervals. No Strava segments. No prescribed duration. Ride for as long as it is fun and stop when it stops being fun. If that is forty-five minutes, that is forty-five minutes. If that is three hours because you found an incredible road and kept going, brilliant.

The only rule: if you find yourself thinking about training zones, you are not in off-season mode yet.

### Do Things That Are Not Cycling

This sounds obvious, and yet most of us are appalling at it. We define ourselves so completely through the sport that when the structured riding stops, we do not know what to do with the time.

Go for a walk. A long one, with no fitness goal attached. Go swimming — not pool intervals, just moving through water because it feels good. Play a sport you have not played in years. Do some yoga. Cook something that takes three hours. Read a book that has nothing to do with performance or nutrition or watts per kilo.

The point is not cross-training. The point is reminding your brain that you are a whole person who happens to ride a bike, not a cyclist who occasionally does other things.

### Reconnect Socially — On and Off the Bike

Training is solitary, even in a group. You are following your own plan, your own intervals, your own targets. The off-season is the window to rebuild the social side of riding that structured training squeezes out.

Organise a coffee ride with no agenda. Ride with someone slower than you and enjoy the conversation. Go to a cycling event as a spectator. Meet your club at the pub instead of the start line. Reconnect with the people in the sport who remind you why you started riding in the first place.

### Explore New Routes

Structured training calcifies your route choices. You ride the same loops because they fit the time window, the terrain matches the session, and you know the surface. The off-season is the chance to blow that open.

Pick a direction you have never gone. Follow a road until it gets interesting. Get lost on purpose. Use a map instead of a GPS route. The novelty is not just recreational — it rebuilds the sense of adventure and discovery that gets buried under months of prescribed intervals on familiar roads.

## The Strength and Conditioning Window

I have [a separate piece on off-season structure](/blog/off-season-training-cycling-what-to-do-guide) that covers the transition roadmap in detail, but let me make one argument here because the timing matters.

During the season, the gym is always fighting the bike for recovery. You do a hard leg session on Monday, and your Tuesday ride suffers. You have a race on Saturday, so you skip the gym on Thursday. The bike always wins because the bike always has the more immediate demand — a session to complete, an event to prepare for, a training load to maintain.

The off-season is the only time this dynamic flips. There is no hard riding competing for your recovery. There are no events on the horizon. The gym can take priority — genuinely take priority, not the half-hearted "I should really do some core work" priority it gets during the season.

Two to three sessions per week. Single-leg exercises that transfer directly to pedalling — Bulgarian split squats, step-ups, single-leg press, glute bridges, hip thrusts. Core stability work that actually translates to time in the saddle — planks, pallof presses, loaded carries. Progressive overload, proper rest between sets, consistent attendance for four to six weeks.

This is the window where you build the structural resilience that protects you during the season. The tendons, the ligaments, the stabilising muscles around the knee and hip that cycling neglects. You cannot retrofit this work once serious training begins. It has to happen now, when the body has the capacity to absorb it.

I am not going to prescribe a programme here — that is a separate conversation and depends on your individual history and limitations. But the argument for doing it now, during the off-season, is not debatable. There is no better window. If you have been saying "I should start going to the gym" for three years, this is the time.

## Setting One Goal for Next Season

Before you write a training plan — before you think about volume or intensity or race calendars — answer one question: what do you actually want from next season?

Not a list. One thing.

Maybe it is a specific event. Maybe it is a power number. Maybe it is riding consistently four times a week without injury. Maybe it is completing something you have never done before. Maybe it is just enjoying the sport again after a year where it felt like work.

One goal. Written down. Clear enough that you can measure whether you achieved it.

The reason this matters now — during the off-season, before the training begins — is that it gives your winter base phase a direction. Aimless base training is one of the biggest wastes of time in amateur cycling. Riders spend twelve weeks in Zone 2 with no idea what they are building toward, and then wonder why they feel unprepared when the season arrives.

A goal anchors everything. It determines which limiters you need to address. It shapes the structure of your first training block. It tells you whether the sportive in June is a target event or a training ride. It makes every session purposeful, even when the session itself is nothing more exciting than a two-hour endurance ride in the rain.

Write it down now. Stick it somewhere you will see it in January when the alarm goes off at six and it is dark and cold and you cannot remember why you are doing this. That is when you will need it.

## When the Off-Season Starts and Ends

This varies more than most guides acknowledge, because the amateur cycling calendar is not uniform. Here are the rough benchmarks.

**Road season finishes September to October.** If your last target event is in September or early October, your off-season starts when that event is done. Not when you feel like stopping. When the event is done. Two to four weeks of unstructured activity, which puts you in a position to begin a base phase in late October or November.

**Sportive-heavy calendar finishing in late summer.** If your season peaks around August — the Etape, Marmotte, or a UK sportive series — your off-season could start as early as September. This gives you the luxury of a longer off-season and an earlier base phase.

**Racing into late October or November.** Cyclo-cross riders or those doing late-season gravel events may not start their off-season until November. This is fine. The principles are the same. But if your season runs that long, lean toward a longer off-season — three to four weeks minimum — because you have accumulated more fatigue across a longer competitive window.

**Indoor-only riders.** If your training is year-round on the turbo with no defined race season, you still need an off-season. Pick a point in the year — many riders use September or October — and take two to three weeks where the turbo stays off and the gym takes over.

The end of the off-season is not a date on the calendar. It is a feeling. You will know it when it arrives: the genuine desire to ride with purpose again. Not obligation. Not fear of falling behind. Actual desire. When that turns up, the off-season has done its work and you are ready to start building again.

## The Year Starts Here

The off-season is not a gap in your year. It is the foundation of the year that follows. The riders who understand this — who can tolerate the discomfort of stopping, who trust the evidence on fitness loss and rebuild, who use the time to recover properly and set a clear direction — are the ones who arrive at January with the freshness and motivation to absorb real training.

The riders who cannot stop are not ahead. They are carrying a bill they do not know they owe, and it comes due in February when the training load increases and the body has nothing left to give.

Stop. Rest. Let the numbers drop. Trust the process. The fitness comes back faster than you think. The freshness does not come back at all unless you create the space for it.

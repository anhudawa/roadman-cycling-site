Here's the thing nobody tells you about hydration articles. You can read twenty of them, memorise the recommended ranges, buy the right electrolyte brand, and still get it catastrophically wrong — because you never actually measured what *your* body does when it sweats.

I know this because I did it for years. I followed the standard advice: 500-750 ml per hour, one electrolyte tab per bottle, drink before you're thirsty. And it worked fine until it didn't. A gran fondo in 30-degree heat, mid-July, and I fell apart at kilometre 110. Not a bonk — I was eating plenty. My legs just emptied. Heart rate drifting up, power drifting down, a creeping headache that no amount of water seemed to fix.

What I didn't know — and what most cyclists don't know — is that my sweat sodium concentration sits at around 1,400 mg per litre. That's well above average. One electrolyte tab per bottle was covering about a third of my actual losses. I was essentially drinking flavoured water and wondering why my body was shutting down.

The fix wasn't complicated. It was measurable. And it started with two things: a bathroom scale and a 20-minute appointment with Andy Blow at Precision Hydration.

## Why You Need Your Own Numbers

Let me be really clear about this. Generic hydration advice — "drink 500-750 ml per hour" — is a starting point, not a plan. It's like telling every cyclist to ride at 200 watts because that's the average. Your body is not average, and personalising this part of your [cycling nutrition](/topics/cycling-nutrition) is not optional. Your sweat rate, your sodium concentration, your fluid absorption capacity — all of these are individual, and some of them are genetically fixed.

Two riders can sit side by side on the same group ride, hold the same power, and lose wildly different amounts of fluid and sodium. One might lose 600 ml of sweat per hour with 400 mg of sodium per litre — total sodium loss of 240 mg per hour. The other might lose 1.2 litres per hour with 1,500 mg of sodium per litre — total sodium loss of 1,800 mg per hour. That is a sevenfold difference. One electrolyte tab is adequate for the first rider and dangerously insufficient for the second.

Andy Blow, who founded Precision Fuel & Hydration and has tested thousands of endurance athletes, puts it this way: the variation in sweat sodium concentration between individuals is the single biggest variable in hydration that most athletes never measure. And unlike sweat rate — which changes with temperature, intensity, and fitness — sodium concentration is largely determined by your genetics. You are born a salty sweater or you are not, and no amount of heat acclimatisation or training will change the concentration significantly.

That's actually the good news. It means you only need to measure sodium concentration once. The number stays broadly stable across your entire athletic career. Sweat rate is the moving target, and you can measure that yourself for free.

## Part One: The DIY Sweat Rate Protocol

This is the foundation of your hydration plan, and it costs nothing beyond a set of scales and one hour on the bike. Here is the protocol, step by step.

### Equipment

- A bathroom or kitchen scale accurate to at least 0.1 kg (digital scales are best)
- A towel
- Your normal training kit and bike
- A notepad or phone to record numbers
- A head unit or watch to track ride duration and temperature
- Optional: a pre-weighed water bottle if you cannot ride for an hour without drinking

### Before the Ride

**Step 1.** Go to the bathroom and empty your bladder completely.

**Step 2.** Strip down — completely naked — and weigh yourself on the scale. Write the number down to one decimal place. This is your pre-ride weight.

**Step 3.** If you plan to drink during the test, weigh your full water bottle on the same scale and record that number separately.

**Step 4.** Note the temperature, humidity if available, and whether conditions are calm or windy. You will need these later when you categorise your results.

### The Ride

**Step 5.** Ride for exactly 60 minutes at your typical training intensity. Zone 2 to Zone 3 is ideal — the effort you'd hold on a normal weekday ride. Do not ride at race intensity unless you are specifically testing for race conditions.

**Step 6.** Ideally, drink nothing during the test. If you cannot manage a full hour without fluid — and some riders simply cannot in hot conditions — drink from your pre-weighed bottle and record exactly how much you consumed.

**Step 7.** Do not eat anything solid during the test. Food weight will skew your result.

**Step 8.** Do not stop for a bathroom break during the ride. If you absolutely must, add a note — this introduces error.

### After the Ride

**Step 9.** When you get home, towel off thoroughly. Hair, face, arms, legs, back — everywhere sweat has collected. You need to remove surface moisture before weighing.

**Step 10.** Strip down again and weigh yourself on the same scale, same surface, same conditions. Write the number down.

**Step 11.** Calculate your sweat rate:

**Sweat rate (litres/hr) = Pre-ride weight (kg) minus post-ride weight (kg) plus any fluid consumed (litres)**

Example: a rider who weighed 74.2 kg before, 73.3 kg after, and drank 300 ml (0.3 litres) during the ride:

74.2 - 73.3 = 0.9 kg lost on the scale
0.9 + 0.3 = **1.2 litres per hour sweat rate**

### How Many Tests You Need

One test gives you a data point. Three tests give you a plan.

Here's where it gets really interesting. Sweat rate is not a fixed number. It shifts dramatically with temperature, and moderately with intensity and humidity. The same rider can produce these results:

- **Cool morning (12°C):** 0.5 L/hr
- **Mild afternoon (22°C):** 0.9 L/hr
- **Hot afternoon (32°C):** 1.4 L/hr

That range — 0.5 to 1.4 — is the real number you need. Not a single figure, but a corridor that maps onto the conditions you'll actually ride in. Test at least three times:

1. **Cool conditions (10-15°C)** — your baseline
2. **Mild conditions (18-24°C)** — your typical training environment
3. **Hot conditions (28°C+)** — your race day or summer riding scenario

If you race or ride in extreme heat (35°C+), do a fourth test at that temperature. It matters. The jump from 28°C to 35°C is not linear — it's often the steepest increase in the curve.

Record all three results alongside the temperature, humidity, and effort level. You now have a personal sweat rate chart. Use the [Hydration Calculator](/tools/hydration) to translate those numbers into bottle-per-hour targets for specific rides.

## Part Two: Measuring Your Sodium Concentration

This is the variable you cannot DIY with a bathroom scale. Sweat sodium concentration requires lab analysis — or a service like Precision Hydration that brings the lab to you.

### The Precision Hydration Sweat Test

Andy Blow's team at Precision Fuel & Hydration runs the most accessible sodium testing service for endurance athletes. Here's what happens.

**The setup.** A technician applies a small pilocarpine patch to your inner forearm. Pilocarpine is a pharmaceutical compound that stimulates your sweat glands locally — no exercise required. The patch triggers sweating in that small area of skin, and the sweat collects on an absorbent disc inside the patch.

**The wait.** You sit still for about 15-20 minutes while the patch collects enough sweat for analysis. It's not uncomfortable — a slight tingling sensation, nothing more.

**The analysis.** The technician removes the patch and analyses the sodium concentration of the collected sweat, typically using a conductivity meter. You get a result in milligrams of sodium per litre of sweat.

**The categories.** Precision Hydration groups results into four tiers:

| Category | Sodium Concentration | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 200-400 mg/L | Standard electrolyte products are usually sufficient |
| Moderate | 400-800 mg/L | You need a deliberate sodium strategy on longer rides |
| High | 800-1,200 mg/L | Generic tabs will not cover your losses — increase dosing |
| Very High | 1,200-2,000+ mg/L | Aggressive sodium replacement required in all conditions over 90 minutes |

**The cost.** Around $40 for the test. Some bike shops and sports nutrition clinics offer it as part of a hydration consultation. Precision Hydration also runs testing at major cycling and triathlon events.

**How often to test.** Once. Sodium concentration is genetically determined and does not change meaningfully with fitness, diet, or age. One test, one number, for life. Retest only if your original result was borderline between categories and you want to confirm.

### The Observation Method

If you cannot access a Precision Hydration test, observation gets you into the right ballpark — not the exact number, but the right category.

**Signs you are a salty sweater (likely 800+ mg/L):**

- White tide-marks on your jersey, shorts, or helmet straps after rides
- Salt stinging your eyes mid-ride
- Crystalline residue on your forehead or arms when you rub your skin post-ride
- A strong salty taste when sweat runs into your mouth
- A pattern of cramping late in hot or long rides

**Signs you are a low-sodium sweater (likely under 500 mg/L):**

- Clean kit after rides — no salt marks
- You rarely notice salt on your skin
- You tolerate heat relatively well without extra sodium
- Cramping is rare, even on longer efforts

Most riders fall somewhere in the middle. If you are unsure, dose for moderate (600-900 mg per litre of fluid) and adjust based on how you feel. But the test removes the guesswork, and for $40, it's the most cost-effective piece of performance data you'll ever buy.

## Part Three: Building Your Replacement Strategy

Now you have two numbers: your sweat rate range (from the DIY test) and your sodium concentration (from Precision Hydration or observation). Multiply them together and you have your hourly sodium loss.

**Example 1: Low-sodium, moderate sweater**
- Sweat rate: 0.8 L/hr in mild conditions
- Sodium concentration: 400 mg/L
- Hourly sodium loss: 320 mg

This rider needs minimal supplementation. One standard electrolyte tab (typically 300-500 mg sodium per 500 ml) covers their losses. Plain water is fine for rides under 90 minutes.

**Example 2: Moderate-sodium, heavy sweater**
- Sweat rate: 1.2 L/hr in warm conditions
- Sodium concentration: 900 mg/L
- Hourly sodium loss: 1,080 mg

This rider needs deliberate sodium strategy. Two electrolyte tabs per hour, or one strong-dose product like Precision Hydration 1000 (1,000 mg sodium per serving). Standard tabs will leave a deficit that accumulates over hours.

**Example 3: Salty sweater in the heat**
- Sweat rate: 1.5 L/hr at 30°C
- Sodium concentration: 1,500 mg/L
- Hourly sodium loss: 2,250 mg

This rider needs aggressive replacement. Standard electrolyte products are wholly inadequate. They need Precision Hydration 1500, supplemental salt capsules (SaltStick or similar), or DIY mixing at high concentrations. Without it, they will cramp, fade, and potentially face medical issues on long rides.

### Replacement by Ride Duration

Not every ride needs the same approach. Let me break this down by how long you're actually in the saddle.

**Under 60 minutes:** Water is fine. Sodium losses are small enough that your body's reserves handle it. Don't overcomplicate a recovery ride or a lunchtime spin.

**60-90 minutes:** Add electrolytes if it's warm (above 22°C) or if you're a salty sweater. One tab or half a teaspoon of salt in your bottle is enough. If it's cool and you're a low-sodium sweater, water still works.

**90 minutes to 3 hours:** Electrolytes become essential for most riders. Match your sodium intake to your calculated hourly loss. This is where your Precision Hydration category matters — a low-sodium sweater might manage with 300-500 mg per hour, while a salty sweater needs 1,000-1,500 mg per hour.

**3-5 hours:** The sodium deficit is cumulative and the consequences are real. A salty sweater on a five-hour ride in warm conditions could lose 10,000+ mg of sodium. Pre-ride sodium loading the night before becomes important here — [the pre-race sodium protocol](/blog/cycling-sodium-loading-hydration-guide) covers this in detail. During the ride, you need a systematic plan: set a timer, drink on schedule, and hit your sodium target every hour without fail.

**5+ hours (sportives, ultra-distance, multi-day):** This is where individual testing separates finishers from the ambulance queue. At this duration, even small hourly deficits compound into large ones. Your replacement strategy needs to be dialled in before the event. Test your exact product mix, your bottle schedule, and your supplemental salt intake during training rides of similar duration. Nothing new on race day.

## Part Four: Commercial Products Versus DIY Mixing

There are dozens of electrolyte products on the market. Some are excellent. Some are overpriced water flavouring with a pinch of salt and a lot of marketing. Here's how to evaluate them.

### What to Look For on the Label

The only number that matters is **milligrams of sodium per serving**. Not "electrolytes." Not "mineral blend." Sodium. Some products list it clearly. Others bury it in a proprietary formula or list it per 100 ml, which requires arithmetic to compare.

**Strong-dose products (800-1,500 mg sodium per serving):**
- Precision Hydration 1000 and 1500 — designed specifically for salty sweaters, backed by Andy Blow's testing data
- SaltStick Caps — 215 mg sodium per capsule, stackable to your target
- LMNT — 1,000 mg sodium per packet (though the flavouring divides opinion)

**Mid-range products (400-700 mg sodium per serving):**
- Nuun Sport — around 300 mg per tab in 500 ml
- SIS Hydro — approximately 350 mg per tab
- High5 Zero — around 250 mg per tab

**Low-dose products (under 300 mg sodium per serving):**
Most of the supermarket "sports drinks" and cheaper effervescent tabs fall here. They are fine for low-sodium sweaters on moderate rides. They are insufficient for salty sweaters or hot conditions.

### The DIY Alternative

A quarter teaspoon of table salt contains approximately 575 mg of sodium. Mixed into 500 ml of water, that gives you 1,150 mg of sodium per litre — stronger than most commercial tabs and dramatically cheaper.

**The basic DIY recipe:**

- 500 ml water
- 1/4 teaspoon table salt (575 mg sodium)
- 1 tablespoon honey or maple syrup (for flavour and a small amount of carbohydrate)
- Juice of half a lemon or lime (flavour and potassium)

Total cost: pennies. Total sodium: more than a premium electrolyte tab.

**When DIY wins:** Training rides, high-volume summer weeks when you're going through electrolytes at a rate that makes commercial products expensive, and any situation where you want precise control over the sodium dose.

**When commercial products win:** Race day, when you don't want to be faffing with measuring spoons in a transition area. Travel, when carrying bulk salt and mixing bottles at the hotel is a pain. And taste compliance — some riders simply will not drink salty water consistently, and a product that tastes good enough to finish is better than a DIY mix that sits half-drunk in the cage.

The pragmatic approach: DIY for training, commercial for racing and travel. Your body doesn't know the difference. It just needs the sodium.

## Part Five: How Heat and Cold Change Everything

This is the section that catches out every rider who tests their sweat rate once in April and assumes the number holds in July. It does not.

### Heat Multiplier

When ambient temperature rises, your body produces more sweat to cool itself. The relationship is not gentle. A rider who loses 0.7 L/hr at 15°C might lose 1.4 L/hr at 32°C — a doubling of fluid loss at the same power output.

But here's where it gets really interesting: sweat *rate* increases with heat, but sweat sodium *concentration* stays roughly the same. This means your total sodium loss per hour scales almost linearly with temperature. If you lose 630 mg of sodium per hour at 15°C (0.7 L × 900 mg/L), you're losing 1,260 mg per hour at 32°C (1.4 L × 900 mg/L). Your electrolyte strategy needs to scale with the thermometer.

**Practical adjustments for heat:**

- Increase fluid intake by 40-80 per cent compared to your cool-weather baseline
- Increase sodium dosing proportionally — double the temperature jump often means double the sodium
- Pre-load with sodium the evening before hot rides (1,500 mg in the final meal plus 500 ml of strong electrolyte solution in the morning)
- Carry an extra bottle or plan refill stops — running dry in heat is dangerous, not just inconvenient
- Consider ice in bottles at the start (slower warming, more palatable)

If you regularly ride in hot conditions — summer racing, training camps, hot-climate holidays — the [heat acclimation protocol](/blog/cycling-heat-acclimation-protocol-guide) is worth reading. Heat acclimation lowers your core temperature at a given effort and reduces perceived exertion, but it also increases sweat rate. Acclimated riders sweat more efficiently, which is good for cooling but means more fluid and sodium out the door. Retest your sweat rate after completing a heat acclimation block.

### Cold Conditions

Cold air suppresses the thirst drive. You feel like you don't need to drink because your body is not overheating, and you're not drenched in visible sweat. But you are still losing fluid — through respiration (breathing cold, dry air increases respiratory water loss), through sweat under layers, and through urine output (cold-induced diuresis, where your body shunts blood to your core and your kidneys increase urine production).

The total fluid loss in cold conditions is lower than in heat — typically 400-600 ml/hr versus 800-1,400 ml/hr — but it is not zero. And because you feel less thirsty, you are more likely to under-drink.

**Practical adjustments for cold:**

- Reduce fluid targets to 400-600 ml/hr, but still drink on schedule
- Use a warm drink if it helps compliance — warm electrolyte solutions are easier to drink when it's 5°C
- Keep sodium in the bottle even at lower fluid volumes — the concentration per litre should stay the same, even if total volume drops
- Insulated bottles prevent your drink from getting too cold to want in sub-zero conditions

### Humidity

Humidity is the hidden variable. High humidity reduces sweat evaporation, which impairs cooling and drives your body to produce *more* sweat to compensate. A ride at 25°C and 30 per cent humidity might generate 0.8 L/hr of sweat. The same ride at 25°C and 80 per cent humidity could push that to 1.2 L/hr because the sweat sits on your skin rather than evaporating.

If you live or race in a humid climate, test your sweat rate in humid conditions specifically. The number will be higher than the temperature alone would suggest.

## Putting It All Together: Your Testing Checklist

Here's the protocol reduced to a checklist you can execute over the next two to three weeks.

**Week 1:**
- [ ] Do your first DIY sweat rate test at your most common training temperature
- [ ] Record the number alongside temperature, humidity, and effort level
- [ ] Book a Precision Hydration sweat test if one is available in your area (check their website for locations)

**Week 2:**
- [ ] Do a second sweat rate test at a different temperature — ideally the warmest conditions you can find
- [ ] If you've had your Precision Hydration test, calculate your hourly sodium loss for both test conditions
- [ ] Buy or mix your electrolyte solution to match your calculated sodium needs

**Week 3:**
- [ ] Do a third sweat rate test in the remaining temperature band
- [ ] Ride a longer session (2-3 hours) using your calculated fluid and sodium targets
- [ ] Note how you feel at the end compared to previous rides of similar length — legs, energy, headache, cramping

After three tests, you have a personal sweat rate range, a sodium concentration number, and a calculated replacement strategy. That is more hydration data than 95 per cent of cyclists have ever collected. And it took three rides and $40.

## The Mistake That Wastes All of This

You do the testing. You calculate the numbers. You buy the products. And then you don't practise it.

Race day hydration compliance is a skill, not a plan. Your gut needs to be trained to absorb the volume of fluid you're asking it to handle. Your taste tolerance for sodium-heavy drinks needs building. Your rhythm of drinking every 15-20 minutes needs to become automatic, not something you remember at kilometre 60 when you notice your unopened second bottle.

Practise your hydration strategy on training rides. Start with shorter sessions and build up to the duration you're targeting. Note any stomach discomfort and adjust concentration or volume. The goal is that by race day, drinking on schedule at the right sodium dose is something your body does without thinking about it.

Time trialling is the purest thing you can do on a bike. No bunch to hide in. No wheel to follow. No excuse when the result comes in. It's you, the road, and the clock — and the clock does not care about your training plan, your equipment, or what you did last week.

That honesty is exactly why it's the best place to start racing.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about time trialling: the barrier to entry is almost zero. You don't need a TT bike. You don't need a skinsuit. You don't need £5,000 worth of carbon. You need a road bike that works, a helmet, and the willingness to hurt for twenty minutes. That's it.

I've watched riders turn up to club 10s on commuter bikes and post times that would embarrass people on full TT rigs. Because the variable that matters most in your first year isn't equipment. It's effort management. It's knowing how to pace yourself, how to warm up properly, and how to hold a position that doesn't fall apart at minute twelve.

Let me break this down.

## Why Time Trialling Is the Best Entry Point Into Racing

Road racing is brilliant, but it's also chaotic. You need bike handling skills, bunch awareness, tactical nous, and the ability to stay upright when someone clips your wheel at 45kph. For a beginner, that's a lot of variables to manage simultaneously.

Time trialling strips all of that away.

You start at minute intervals. You ride alone. The only decisions you make are how hard to go, when to shift, and whether to sit up or stay aero. It's the closest thing cycling has to a controlled experiment — and that makes it incredibly useful for tracking your fitness over time.

Matt Bottrill, who went from club time triallist to national champion, has said repeatedly that club 10s were the foundation of everything he built. Not because the racing itself was elite-level, but because the repeatability gave him a weekly data point. Same course, same distance, different number on the result sheet. You can see whether your training is working in a way that's impossible in road racing, where the result depends on who showed up and what the wind did.

There's another reason. Time trialling teaches you to suffer productively. In a road race, you can soft-pedal through the bunch for half the race and then contest the sprint. In a time trial, every second of easing off is a second you can't get back. You learn what your body can sustain, and you learn it fast.

## Equipment: What You Actually Need

The internet will tell you that you need a time trial bike, deep-section wheels, an aero helmet, a skinsuit, shoe covers, and a set of number boards. The internet is wrong — at least for your first season.

**What you actually need:**

- A road bike in good working order
- A helmet (any standard cycling helmet — no aero helmet required)
- A rear light (mandatory for most UK evening events)
- Front and rear brakes (non-negotiable on open roads)
- Something to pin your number to — a jersey, a number belt, or safety pins through your shorts

**What's nice to have but not necessary:**

- A cycling computer, ideally with a power meter reading
- Clip-on aero bars (the single best cost-per-second upgrade available)
- A skinsuit (saves 30-60 seconds over 25 miles, less over 10)
- An aero helmet (saves similar — but position matters more)

Here's where it gets really interesting. Dan Bigham, who held the Hour Record, has talked extensively about the hierarchy of aerodynamic gains. The rider's body accounts for 70-80% of total aerodynamic drag at time trial speeds. The bike and wheels are the remaining 20-30%. What that means in practice: getting your body into a good, sustainable position on a road bike with clip-on bars will save you more time than a £4,000 TT frame with a poor position.

A set of clip-on aero bars costs £30-80. They bolt onto most road handlebars. They let you get your arms narrow and your torso lower, which is where the real aerodynamic savings come from. If you're going to spend money on one thing, spend it on those.

The TT bike comes later. Much later. When your pacing is dialled, your position is stable, and your times have plateaued on the road bike — that's when a dedicated frame starts to make sense. For most riders, that's year two at the earliest.

## Finding Events: The Club TT Scene

Club time trials are one of the best-kept secrets in cycling. They run weekly throughout the summer — usually a Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday evening — and they cost nothing or next to nothing. A pound or two for the tea fund. That's it.

In the UK, almost every cycling club runs a weekly evening 10. The courses are registered with Cycling Time Trials (CTT), the national governing body, and you'll find them listed on the CTT website or your local club's page. In Ireland, check Cycling Ireland and local club websites — the format is similar, though courses tend to be on open roads rather than dual carriageways.

The atmosphere at a club 10 is nothing like what you'd expect from a race. There's no start gun. There's no peloton. You sign on, get a number, get told your start time, ride the course, and come back. Someone reads out the times at the end. People drink tea. It's gloriously low-key.

Your first one will feel strange. You'll arrive not knowing anyone, not knowing the course, and not knowing how hard to go. That's normal. Everyone who has ever ridden a time trial has had that exact experience. The good news is that time triallists are, as a group, absurdly welcoming. It's one of the few parts of cycling where the fastest riders in the club will actively help the slowest riders get better, because they remember being there.

**How to find your first event:**

1. Search the CTT website for events near you
2. Check your local cycling club's website — most list their evening TT schedule
3. Turn up 30 minutes before the first start time
4. Sign on and tell the organiser it's your first time trial — they will walk you through the process
5. Don't overthink it

Open events are different from club events. They're more formal, often on faster courses, and they cost £15-25 to enter. They're worth doing once you've found your feet at club level, but they're not where you should start.

## The Warm-Up: More Important Than You Think

Here's the thing nobody tells you about time trialling: the warm-up matters more here than in any other cycling discipline.

In a road race, you have a neutralised start. You roll along in the bunch for a few kilometres, your heart rate comes up gradually, and by the time the racing starts, your body is ready. In a time trial, you go from standing still to near-maximal effort in the space of a single pedal stroke. If your body isn't primed for that transition, you lose time before you've even hit the first turn.

Michael Hutchinson, four-time British time trial champion and the author of some of the best writing on the discipline, described the feeling of starting a TT without warming up as being like trying to sprint through treacle for the first three minutes. Your legs are heavy, your breathing is ragged, and your power is 10-15% below what it should be. You never get those watts back.

**A warm-up that actually works (20-25 minutes):**

| Time | What to do | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| 0-10 min | Easy spinning, 50-60% FTP | Light, conversational |
| 10-14 min | Progressive build to 80% FTP | Moderate, breathing harder |
| 14-16 min | Two x 30-second efforts at 110% FTP, with 30 seconds recovery between | Hard, sharp breathing |
| 16-18 min | Easy spinning, recover fully | Back to light |
| 18-20 min | One 10-second sprint, near-maximum | All-out |
| 20-25 min | Easy spinning to the start line | Light, keeping legs moving |

The two 30-second efforts and the short sprint serve a specific purpose: they prime your anaerobic system and open up the blood vessels in your working muscles. Without those efforts, your body has to do that work during the first two minutes of the race, which means you're slower precisely when you should be settling into your pace.

If you're doing a club 10 and you only have 15 minutes to warm up, cut the easy spinning down but keep the efforts. The efforts are the part that matters.

One more thing. After you warm up, keep your legs moving. Don't stop and stand around chatting for ten minutes before your start time. Pedal on the spot, walk around, do whatever you need to do to keep the blood flowing. The moment you stop moving, you start losing the benefit of the warm-up.

## Pacing: Where Most Beginners Get It Wrong

Pacing is the single biggest performance variable after fitness. Full stop. And almost every beginner gets it wrong in exactly the same way: they go out too hard.

It feels right at the time. The adrenaline is flowing. The first mile feels easy. You look at your power meter and you're 20 watts above target. No problem, you think. I feel great.

You don't feel great at mile four.

The physiology behind this is simple. When you exceed your sustainable power output, you accumulate fatigue at an exponential rate, not a linear one. Going 5% too hard for the first half of a 10-mile TT doesn't cost you 5% on the second half. It costs you 8-12%. The overall result is 2-3% slower than if you'd paced it evenly. That's a minute on a 25-minute ride. A minute you can never claw back.

John Archibald, one of the most thoughtful riders in British time trialling, has made the point that even pacing isn't actually the goal — it's the minimum standard. The real goal is to pace in a way that accounts for the course, the conditions, and your own physiology.

**Specific pacing numbers for a 10-mile TT:**

If you know your FTP — and you should, because [your power zones](/tools/ftp-zones) are the foundation of everything — here's the framework:

- **Minutes 0-2:** 95% of FTP. Deliberately hold back. This is the hardest part psychologically because you feel fresh and everyone around you seems to be going faster. They're not. They're going too hard.
- **Minutes 2-15:** 100% of FTP. Settle in. This is your sustainable race pace. It should feel hard but controlled. Your breathing should be fast but not ragged.
- **Minutes 15-20:** 102-105% of FTP. Now you can push. The finish is close enough that the fatigue won't catch you. Empty the tank over the last two to three minutes.
- **Final 30 seconds:** Everything you have left. Sprint if you can.

If you don't have a power meter, pace by perceived effort. The first quarter should feel like a 7 out of 10. The middle half should feel like an 8. The last quarter should feel like a 9. The last minute should feel like a 10.

The single best piece of pacing advice I can give you: if you feel comfortable in the first two miles, you're going the right speed. If you feel fast, you're going too hard.

## Course Recon: Know What's Coming

Riding a course blind is a mistake. It's a fixable mistake, and fixing it is free.

The reason course recon matters is effort distribution. A flat, straight 10-mile course is paced differently from a rolling course with corners. A course with a headwind on the way out and a tailwind on the way back is paced differently from one that's sheltered. If you don't know what's coming, you can't plan for it, and your pacing will be reactive rather than deliberate.

**What to look for on a recon ride:**

- **Junctions and turns.** Where do you need to slow down? Where can you carry speed through? Every second you spend braking and re-accelerating is a second lost.
- **Gradient changes.** Even gentle rises change the effort calculation. A 2% gradient that lasts 800 metres will spike your power by 15-20% if you try to maintain the same speed. Better to let speed drop slightly and keep power steady.
- **Wind exposure.** Open sections — dual carriageways, exposed ridgelines, gaps in hedgerows — are where the wind hits you. Note these so you can plan to work harder through headwind sections and recover slightly in tailwind sections.
- **Road surface.** Rough sections slow you down and drain energy. Smooth sections are where you make time. If there's a choice of line through a corner, the smooth one is faster than the short one.
- **The finish.** Know exactly where the finish timekeeper is standing. You don't want to sprint to the wrong lamppost.

You don't need to do this ride at race pace. An easy spin around the course a day or two before is enough. Look at it, memorise the landmarks, note the sections where you'll need to manage your effort. That twenty minutes of preparation is worth more than twenty minutes of extra training.

If you can't ride the course in person, study it on a map. CTT course descriptions include turn-by-turn directions, and you can trace most courses on Google Maps or Strava. It's not as good as riding it, but it's vastly better than going in cold.

## The Mental Side: What Happens When It Hurts

Time trialling hurts. There's no getting around that. Somewhere between minute eight and minute fifteen of a well-paced 10-miler, your body will start sending very clear signals that it wants you to stop. Your legs burn. Your lungs burn. Your brain tells you to ease off.

The riders who go fast are the ones who manage that discomfort rather than being managed by it.

Here's what actually works, based on what I've seen with the riders I work with:

**Segment the effort.** Don't think about the whole twenty minutes. Think about getting to the next lamppost. Then the next junction. Then the next mile marker. Break the race into small, manageable pieces and deal with each one as it comes. Michael Hutchinson wrote about this brilliantly — the idea that the only bit of the race that matters is the bit you're in right now.

**Breathe with intention.** When the effort gets hard, your breathing becomes shallow and panicked. Deliberately lengthen your exhale. Count it if you need to: in for two pedal strokes, out for three. This sounds too simple to work. It works.

**Use a mantra.** Not something inspirational. Something functional. "Smooth and steady." "Push the pedals." "Stay low." A short phrase that keeps your brain occupied so it can't spiral into the "this is too hard" narrative.

**Accept the pain.** This is the counterintuitive one. The more you fight the discomfort, the worse it feels. Acknowledge it — yes, this hurts, it's supposed to hurt, I chose to be here — and then redirect your attention to something technical. Your pedal stroke. Your breathing rhythm. Your position on the bars.

The mental approach that doesn't work: motivational anger. Screaming at yourself internally, treating the race like a war. That spikes your heart rate, tenses your muscles, and wastes energy. The fastest time triallists look calm. They are calm — or at least, they've practised looking calm until the calmness became real.

## Common Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

I've watched hundreds of first-time time triallists. The mistakes are remarkably consistent.

**Mistake 1: Starting too hard.** Already covered, but it bears repeating because it's so universal. If your first mile is your fastest mile, you've paced it wrong. Hold back early. Trust the process.

**Mistake 2: Skipping the warm-up.** The rider who warms up properly and starts 2 minutes before you will come past you at mile three. Every time. A 20-minute warm-up is not optional for a 20-minute race.

**Mistake 3: Looking down.** Your head is a bowling ball. When you tuck your chin to your chest and stare at your stem, you create a dam that the air has to flow over. Look up the road. Michael Hutchinson has pointed out that the head position alone can account for 5-10 watts of drag at 40kph. That's 15-30 seconds over 10 miles. Free speed, just from where you point your eyes.

**Mistake 4: Gripping the bars too tight.** Tension in your arms and shoulders transfers to your upper body, which wastes energy and ruins your aero profile. Your hands should be resting on the bars, not strangling them. Consciously relax your grip every few minutes.

**Mistake 5: Not eating or drinking beforehand.** A 10-mile TT doesn't require fuelling during the ride, but your pre-race nutrition matters. Eat a proper meal 2-3 hours before. Have a coffee 45-60 minutes before if that's part of your routine. Don't arrive fasted — your glycogen stores need to be full for a threshold effort.

**Mistake 6: Changing everything at once.** New position, new bars, new skinsuit, first race. Pick one variable to test at a time. Club 10s are the perfect testing ground precisely because they're low-stakes. Use them as experiments, not performances.

**Mistake 7: Comparing yourself to fast riders too early.** The rider who posts a 20-minute 10 has probably been doing this for a decade. Your first 10-mile time might be 28 minutes. It might be 32 minutes. It might be 35. That number is your starting point, not your judgement. What matters is the direction of travel.

## The Progression Path: From First 10 to Competing

Time trialling has a natural progression, and it follows a clear path.

**Stage 1: Club 10s on a road bike (months 1-6).** Ride as many as you can. Focus on pacing, warm-up, and getting comfortable with the format. Don't buy anything. Just ride.

**Stage 2: Consistent club 10s with clip-on bars (months 3-9).** Once you've established a baseline, add clip-on aero bars and start working on your position. Your times will drop. Focus on holding a stable aero position for the full duration.

**Stage 3: Open events and 25-mile TTs (months 6-12).** Once your 10-mile pacing is solid, try a 25. The pacing is different — you're riding at 90-95% of FTP instead of 100% — and the mental challenge is significant. The 25 is where you learn what sustained effort really means.

**Stage 4: Equipment and position refinement (year 2+).** This is where a TT bike, an aero helmet, a skinsuit, and a proper bike fit start to matter. Not because they didn't matter before, but because your fitness and pacing have now developed to the point where equipment gains are meaningful rather than theoretical.

**Stage 5: Structured training (ongoing).** Your first season should focus on just riding time trials regularly. From year two onwards, [structured interval training](/blog/cycling-interval-training-beginners) and a properly [periodised season](/blog/how-to-periodise-cycling-season) will drive further improvement. The gains from better training methodology are larger and more lasting than the gains from better equipment.

Matt Bottrill's story illustrates this perfectly. He started with club 10s. He was decent but unremarkable. Then he started working systematically on his pacing, his position, and his training. By the time he was buying a dedicated TT bike, he'd already improved by several minutes through fitness and technique alone. The bike was the finishing touch, not the foundation.

## What a First Season Looks Like

If you're reading this and thinking about giving it a go, here's what a realistic first season looks like:

**April-May:** Find your local club TT. Turn up on your road bike. Ride three or four evening 10s. Get a feel for the format, the pacing, and the effort level. Don't worry about times.

**June-July:** Start dialling in your warm-up protocol. Add clip-on bars if you haven't already. Begin paying attention to your pacing — use a power meter if you have one, or work off perceived effort. Ride every week if you can.

**August-September:** Enter one or two open events. Try a 25-mile TT. Compare your times from early season to now. You'll likely see a 2-3 minute improvement on your 10-mile time, mostly from better pacing and a proper warm-up.

**October:** Season ends. Review your numbers. Think about what to work on over the winter. If you've caught the bug — and most people do — start planning your structured winter training around next season's goals.

The riders who improve fastest are the ones who race consistently. Not the ones who train the hardest, buy the most equipment, or obsess over marginal gains. Consistency and repeatability are what drive improvement in year one. Everything else is detail.

## The Bit Nobody Mentions: It's Actually Fun

For something that sounds like a pure suffering exercise, time trialling is remarkably enjoyable. There's a satisfaction in the simplicity of it. You against the clock. No variables you can't control. No bad luck, no crashes, no arguments about the sprint finish.

And the community around it is exceptional. Club time triallists are some of the most knowledgeable people in cycling. They've been doing this for decades, many of them, and they'll tell you everything they know about pacing, position, equipment, and courses if you ask. You don't have to figure this out alone.

If you've been thinking about it, stop thinking and go and do it. Find your local club TT. Turn up next week. Ride your road bike. Pin on a number. And find out what you can do when it's just you and the clock.

That's all it takes to start.

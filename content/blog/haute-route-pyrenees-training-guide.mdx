The Haute Route Pyrenees starts at the Atlantic and finishes at the Mediterranean. Seven stages. 800 kilometres. Roughly 20,000 metres of climbing across the most combative mountain range in European road cycling. That trajectory — ocean to sea through France's steepest cols — is the draw. The reality is less romantic and more physical than any brochure suggests.

The Pyrenees are not the Alps. If you have ridden the [Haute Route Alps](/blog/haute-route-alps-training-guide), you know what long, steady-state climbing feels like — the Galibier sitting at 6% for 35km, the Bonette grinding upward at a rhythm you can set and hold. The Pyrenees do not work that way. Pyrenean cols are shorter, steeper, and more unpredictable. The Tourmalet does not let you settle. It eases to 4%, lets you recover for 90 seconds, then kicks to 11% and demands a different effort entirely. The Aubisque does the same. The Col d'Aspin, which looks moderate on paper, punishes riders who pace it by averages instead of what the road is doing right now. This is the mountain range that breaks riders who train on steady gradients and arrive expecting rhythm.

The trap is stage one. You are fresh, the legs feel strong, and the adrenaline of riding through the Pays Basque foothills makes 80% FTP feel pedestrian. Every year, the same pattern: riders hammer stage one, feel brilliant at the finish, and spend stages four through seven wondering why the col they climbed easily on Tuesday now feels like a wall. The Haute Route Pyrenees is not seven days of hard riding. It is one week-long effort where the opening three stages are investment and the closing four stages are the return.

- Pyrenean cols are steeper and more variable than their Alpine equivalents. Where the Galibier grinds at a steady 6-7%, the Tourmalet and Aubisque alternate between 4% respite and 10-12% ramps that punish riders who set a fixed pace.

## What the Haute Route Pyrenees Actually Demands

Seven stages over seven days. Approximately 800km. Between 19,000 and 21,000 metres of climbing. Those are the headline numbers. Here is what they actually mean when you are standing at the start line somewhere in the western Pyrenees with a week of racing ahead of you.

Each stage averages 110-130km with 2,500-3,500m of climbing. Some stages are short and brutal — 90km with 3,000m of vertical packed into three steep cols back to back. Others stretch to 150-160km with rolling valleys between the passes, testing a different kind of endurance. Two or three stages cross above 2,000m. The Port d'Envalira in Andorra tops out at 2,408m, the highest paved road in the Pyrenees. The Tourmalet sits at 2,115m. Lower than the big Alpine passes, but the approaches are steeper — you arrive at the summit with more muscular fatigue per metre of elevation gained.

Every stage is timed against the field. Mass start, neutralised through any traffic sections, then racing. The GC is cumulative. Riders compete for stage results and overall classification. The Haute Route attracts a strong international field — former amateur racers, ex-pros doing the masters circuit, and well-trained sportive riders who treat this as their season target. The back of the field is not casual. Time limits are enforced, and at 3.2 W/kg the longer stages leave almost no margin against the cut-off.

Put the total climbing in context. The 2025 Tour de France covered roughly 22,000m of climbing across 21 stages. The Haute Route Pyrenees covers 20,000m of climbing across seven stages. That is three weeks of Grand Tour vertical compressed into one week. The pros recover between mountain stages with flat or rolling transition days. You do not get transition days. Every day is a mountain stage. The [Training Load Calculator](/tools/training-load) will show you what that kind of accumulated TSS looks like by day five — and it is not a number most amateurs have ever seen in training.

## The Pyrenean Difference

If you have trained for the [Haute Route Alps](/blog/haute-route-alps-training-guide), do not assume the same preparation transfers directly. The Pyrenees demand a different rider.

**Col du Tourmalet.** 17km from Luz-Saint-Sauveur at 7.4% average. That average is a lie. The first 5km sit at 5-6%, lulling you into a rhythm. Then the road kicks to 8-9% for 4km. Then a brief respite at 6%. Then the final 3km ramp to 10-13% before the summit at 2,115m. The Tourmalet does not reward steady-state pacing. It rewards a rider who can adjust effort every two minutes without losing composure. If you rode the Galibier at a locked-in 260 watts for 90 minutes, the Tourmalet will force you between 230 and 310 watts across 70 minutes — same average power, completely different physiological cost.

**Col d'Aubisque.** 16.5km at 7.2% average from the eastern approach. The middle section is deceptive — a false flat at 3-4% that tempts riders to accelerate, burning matches they need for the final 5km which average 9% with pitches above 11%. The Aubisque is where impatient riders pay. Use the [Climbing Time Estimator](/tools/climb-time) to model your ascent time and pace the effort based on the section profile, not the overall average.

**Col d'Aspin.** 12km at 6.5%. The shortest of the major cols and the one most riders underestimate. It often features mid-stage — sandwiched between longer climbs — and the temptation is to push tempo and bank time. On a single-day event, that works. On day five of a stage race, the extra 15 watts on the Aspin costs 40 watts on whatever col comes after it.

**Col de Peyresourde.** 13.2km at 7.0%. Variable gradient with sections at 10% and brief descents within the climb itself — the road dips then ramps back up, forcing repeated acceleration. This is the most technically Pyrenean of all the cols. Riders used to long, consistent Alpine grades struggle with the rhythm.

**Port d'Envalira.** The Andorran detour. 24km at 5.2% from Pas de la Casa — a long, relatively steady climb by Pyrenean standards, but at 2,408m the altitude bites. Riders lose 8-12% of sea-level sustainable power above 2,000m, and the Envalira spends 15km above that threshold. Pace to heart rate and RPE above 2,000m, not to absolute watts.

This is the central difference: Alpine cols are long and steady, Pyrenean cols are short and volatile. The Alps reward an engine that can hold 250 watts for 90 minutes. The Pyrenees reward a rider who can switch between 220 and 300 watts every three minutes for 70 minutes and still recover enough to do it again after a 25-minute descent. Train for the variable effort, not the steady one.

## Stage Racing Strategy: Pacing Across Seven Days

The GC format changes everything. In a single-day sportive, you empty the tank. In a seven-day stage race, the rider who empties the tank on day one has nothing left for the six days that follow.

**Days 1-2: Banking discipline.** Pace the climbs at 75-80% FTP. Not higher. This feels uncomfortably easy when the legs are fresh and faster riders are rolling past. That is correct. You should finish stages one and two feeling like you left 15-20 minutes on the table. Those minutes are banked for days five, six, and seven when cumulative fatigue erodes your power by 5-10% and every watt costs more than it did on Tuesday.

**Day 3: The hinge.** By the third stage, the pattern of the week becomes clear. Riders who paced well feel strong. Riders who raced days one and two feel the first warning signs — heavier legs on the first col, heart rate sitting 5-8 beats higher at the same power. Day three is the last day where discipline can save the week. Push past 80% FTP on the climbs and the back half of the week becomes survival.

**Days 4-5: The mid-week crisis.** This is where the Haute Route is won and lost. Glycogen reserves are depleted despite eating. Sleep has been disrupted by tired legs, unfamiliar beds, and early starts. Muscle damage from four consecutive days of 2,500-3,500m climbing accumulates in the quads and hip flexors. The riders who maintained rigid pacing through the first three stages have a 5-8% power advantage over those who raced hard early. That advantage compounds through the remaining stages.

**Days 6-7: Finishing strong.** The riders who negative-split the Haute Route — riding the final stages harder than the opening ones — are the riders who move up the GC. By day six, half the field is in survival mode. The rider who still has matches to burn can gain 20-30 GC positions over the final two stages. This is where the conservative opening pays its dividend.

## 16-Week Training Framework

Four blocks of four weeks. The back-to-back climbing capacity layers in from week five and peaks in weeks nine through thirteen. Volumes assume you are starting from an 8-10 hour/week base with regular climbing in the legs. Adjust for your own starting point.

### Weeks 1-4: Aerobic Base

Volume: 9-11 hours/week. Four to five rides, 80% in [Zone 2](/blog/zone-2-training-complete-guide).

Build the aerobic floor. The long ride progresses from 3 hours to 4.5 hours over the block with 800-1,200m of climbing. One quality session per week: 3x10 minutes at 85-90% FTP on the best gradient you can find locally. Keep the rest truly easy. Riders who hammer the base block plateau in the build block and crack on the Tourmalet in race week. The polarised principle — credited to [Stephen Seiler](/guests/stephen-seiler)'s research — applies hardest in these four weeks.

Gym work starts here. Two sessions per week: Bulgarian split lunges (3x10 each leg), step-ups with dumbbells (3x12 each leg), single-leg hip thrusts (3x10 each side), and a core circuit — dead bugs, pallof press, side planks — for 15 minutes. This builds the single-leg stability that steep Pyrenean gradients demand without the injury risk of heavy bilateral loading. Keep the weights moderate. You are building resilience, not maximal strength.

### Weeks 5-8: Build

Volume: 11-14 hours/week. Two quality sessions appear. The back-to-back weekends start.

**Threshold climbing.** 4x10 minutes at 95-100% FTP on gradient, building to 3x15 minutes by week eight. Do this on a real climb. The recruitment patterns — glute, hamstring, calf engagement — are different on an 8% gradient compared to a flat road or turbo trainer at the same wattage.

**Variable-gradient intervals.** This is the Pyrenees-specific session. Within a single 20-minute effort on a climb, alternate between 3 minutes at 85-90% FTP (simulating the easier ramps) and 2 minutes at 105-110% FTP (simulating the steep pitches). Four rounds. This teaches the body to surge and recover within a single climb — exactly what the Tourmalet and Peyresourde demand.

**Back-to-back weekends.** From week six: Saturday 4-5 hours with 1,500m climbing, Sunday 3-4 hours with 1,000m climbing. You arrive at Sunday feeling yesterday's legs. That is the point.

Gym reduces to one session per week. Maintain the movements — Bulgarian split lunges, leg press, step-ups, core — but drop volume by 30%. The legs need recovery capacity for the back-to-back weekends.

### Weeks 9-13: Peak

Volume: 14-16 hours/week. This block separates Haute Route preparation from sportive preparation.

By week ten, the weekend looks like this: Saturday 5-6 hours with 2,000-2,500m of climbing including two sustained climbs of 25+ minutes. Sunday 4-5 hours with 1,500-2,000m of climbing at sportive pace. Repeat this structure three times across the block with a recovery week between each.

**Multi-day simulation blocks.** At least once during weeks 10-12, stack three consecutive riding days: Friday 3 hours, Saturday 5-6 hours with maximum climbing, Sunday 4-5 hours at sportive pace. This is the closest simulation of race-week fatigue you can create in training. If you can ride well on Sunday afternoon after two big days, you can handle the Pyrenees. If Sunday falls apart, you know where the work is.

Mid-week quality tightens to one session: 3x12 minutes at 95-100% FTP. The back-to-back weekends are the priority stimulus now. Stacking high-intensity mid-week sessions on top of 10-hour climbing weekends pushes you toward overtraining, not toward race fitness.

Gym drops entirely by week eleven. The on-bike volume is sufficient for muscular maintenance.

### Weeks 14-16: Taper and Travel

Volume drops 30-40% in week fourteen, another 50% in weeks fifteen and sixteen. Keep short intensity alive — 3x5 minutes at threshold, 4x90 seconds at VO2 max — to keep the legs sharp without adding fatigue. The taper is where most amateurs panic. You feel fresh by day three of reduced volume and decide you are losing fitness. You are not. You are shedding accumulated fatigue. Trust the process.

Travel to the Pyrenees 5-7 days before stage one if possible. Ride easy at altitude. Eat well. Sleep long. The fitness is already built. The taper is about arriving with fresh legs and full glycogen, not building anything new.

## Key Sessions

Four sessions carry the most transfer to the Haute Route Pyrenees. Everything else in the plan supports these.

**Variable-gradient intervals.** 4x(3 minutes at 85-90% FTP + 2 minutes at 105-110% FTP) continuous within a single climb, 8 minutes recovery between rounds. This is the Pyrenees-specific session. It mimics the surge-and-settle rhythm of cols like the Tourmalet and Aubisque where the gradient changes every 500 metres. Run it once per week through the build and peak blocks.

**Back-to-back climbing weekends.** Saturday 5-6 hours, 2,000-2,500m climbing. Sunday 4-5 hours, 1,500-2,000m climbing. Both at sportive pace. This is the single most important training stimulus. Nothing else replicates recovering overnight and climbing hard the next morning. Three of these weekends in the peak block is the minimum.

**Climbing threshold repeats.** 3x15 minutes at 95-100% FTP on a sustained gradient of 6-8%. Five minutes easy between efforts. This builds the ceiling. The [FTP Zones tool](/tools/ftp-zones) will map these targets precisely. Run this session mid-week during the build block, reducing to one threshold session in the peak block as back-to-back weekends take priority.

**Recovery ride discipline.** The day after a long climbing day, ride 90 minutes at Zone 1 — below 65% FTP, heart rate below 130bpm. This is not a junk ride. It is active recovery that promotes blood flow and flushes metabolic waste without adding training stress. The quality of your recovery rides in training is a direct rehearsal for between-stage recovery during race week. If you cannot ride easy the day after riding hard, you have a pacing problem.

## Recovery Protocol for Stage Racing

Between-stage recovery is where most amateurs lose the Haute Route. The riding is hard. The recovery is harder — because it requires discipline when you are tired, hungry, and surrounded by other riders heading to the bar.

**The protocol.** Finish. Recovery shake within 30 minutes — 3:1 carb-to-protein ratio, 40-60g carbs, 15-20g protein. Legs up the wall for 15 minutes. Shower. Full meal within 90 minutes of finishing. Compression sleeves on for the evening. Light foam rolling — calves, quads, hip flexors, 10 minutes total, nothing aggressive. Massage if available. Lights out by 21:30. Sleep 8 hours minimum. Every night. For seven nights.

**When to ease off vs. push through.** Muscle soreness that arrives in the morning and fades within the first 30 minutes of riding is normal cumulative fatigue. Keep racing. Joint pain that worsens during riding — particularly in the knees at steep gradients — is a warning sign. Drop intensity by 10-15% and reassess at the next stage. Stomach issues that persist beyond a single stage usually indicate dehydration or gut fatigue from insufficient fuelling practice. Switch to liquid calories, reduce fibre, and eat small amounts frequently rather than large meals.

**Sleep is non-negotiable.** Riders who bank 8 hours per night across the week recover. Riders sleeping 6 hours crack on day four regardless of fitness. The post-stage evenings are preparation time, not celebration time. Ear plugs. Eye mask. No screens after 21:00. The riders in the hotel bar at 23:00 are the riders losing 10 minutes a stage by day five.

## Nutrition Strategy

Multi-day nutrition is a compounding problem. The deficit from underfuelling on day one does not appear on day one. It appears on day three as a crack that cannot be repaired mid-race.

**On-stage fuelling.** 80-90g of carbohydrate per hour, every stage, every day. Two gels plus a bar each hour, or a 90g/hour drink mix paired with one solid item every 90 minutes. The 2:1 glucose-fructose ratio allows higher absorption than glucose alone. Train your gut to this volume on at least eight long rides before the event. Use the [Fuelling Calculator](/tools/fuelling) to map your targets based on body weight and stage duration.

**Between-stage nutrition.** Daily carb intake during race week climbs to 8-10g per kg body weight. A 72kg rider eats 576-720g of carbs daily — far more than normal training intake. Protein reaches 1.5-2g/kg/day for overnight muscle repair. This is more food than feels normal. Eat it anyway. Set alarms if you need to. Eat to a schedule, not to hunger. Hunger signals lag behind the deficit by 12-24 hours.

**Pyrenean heat.** Valley floors in the Pyrenees hit 30-35C in August and September. Fluid loss on a 130km stage through hot valleys with three cols can reach 1.5-2 litres per hour. Carry two bottles, refill at every feed station, and add electrolyte tabs. Dehydration compounds across a week faster than within a single day.

## Kit and Equipment

**Gearing.** 34x32 minimum. 34x34 if your frame and derailleur accept it. Pyrenean gradients are steeper than Alpine averages, and by day five your climbing power sits at 85-90% of fresh-leg output. The same 10% ramp that you rode at 68rpm on day one becomes 55rpm on day five with the same gearing. Under-geared is the most common equipment mistake at this event. Test your gearing on the steepest local climb at the end of a 5-hour ride — if you are below 58rpm in your easiest gear, change the cassette before you fly out.

**Weather layers.** The Pyrenean weather problem is speed of change. A valley start at 28C can become a summit at 6C with rain within 90 minutes of riding. Carry a gilet and arm warmers on every stage regardless of the morning forecast. A full rain jacket on any stage where the cols exceed 1,800m. Full-finger gloves for descents — the [wind chill](/tools/wind-chill) on a 15-minute descent from the Tourmalet pushes feels-like temperature well below 10C even in August. Cloud rolls in within 20 minutes in the Pyrenees. If you can see the summit, the weather is good. If you cannot, prepare for anything.

**Bike preparation.** Disc brakes if you have them — seven days of mountain descents on tired hands is where disc brakes earn their weight. Fresh brake pads. New chain and cassette. Tubeless tyres or quality clinchers with two spare tubes. A mechanical on day three of a stage race is a GC-ending event. Service the bike completely before travel. Carry a mini tool kit daily.

## Common Mistakes

**Racing day one.** Fresh legs and adrenaline are a trap. The riders who attack stage one at 85-90% FTP on the climbs are the riders calling for the transfer van on stage five. Stage one should feel uncomfortably easy. Pace to 75-80% FTP on the climbs. No exceptions.

**Underfuelling between stages.** Eating 8-10g/kg carbs daily for seven consecutive days is difficult. The volume of food is abnormal. Many riders eat 5-6g/kg out of habit and accumulate a 1,500-2,000 calorie daily deficit. By day four the glycogen debt is real and no amount of on-bike gels can compensate. Force-feed if needed. Set timers. Treat eating as part of the race, not a break from it.

**Wrong gearing for Pyrenean gradients.** An 11-28 cassette that works on a training ride becomes a punishment device on day five when a 10% ramp appears and your FTP has dropped 8% from accumulated fatigue. The Pyrenees are steeper than the Alps. Gear accordingly.

**Ignoring weather.** Riders who leave the hotel without arm warmers because the valley is 30C at 08:00 are the riders hypothermic on the Aubisque at 13:00 when the cloud rolls in. Carry layers every day. The weight penalty is 200 grams. The consequence of getting it wrong is a 40-minute descent in the rain at 8C with no protection.

**Insufficient back-to-back training.** A single 6-hour climbing day does not prepare you for seven consecutive 5-hour climbing days. The training problem is not riding hard — it is riding hard the day after riding hard. Build at least three back-to-back climbing weekends into the final twelve weeks.

**Pacing Pyrenean cols by average gradient.** The Tourmalet averages 7.4%. That number is useless for pacing because the gradient oscillates between 4% and 13% within the same climb. Pace by sections, not averages. Use your head unit to display gradient and adjust power in real time rather than locking into a fixed wattage.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about sportives: the event itself is rarely where things go wrong. The puncture you can't fix because you forgot a spare tube. The stomach that rebels at kilometre 40 because you tried a new pasta dish last night. The dead cycling computer. The wrong layering choice that leaves you frozen on the first descent or overheating on the first climb. All of these happen before you clip in. They happen the night before, in the morning rush, in the bag you packed badly or didn't pack at all.

The training is banked. Your fitness is whatever it is — nothing you do tonight will change that. But the operational side? That's entirely within your control. And it is the difference between the ride you trained for and a slow accumulation of small little leaks that add up into a miserable day.

## Work backwards from your alarm

Most people plan their evening forward: have dinner, watch something, go to bed, set the alarm. That's backwards. You need to start from the start line and count back.

Say your sportive starts at 7:30am. You want to arrive 45 minutes early for registration and a warm-up spin. That means leaving home at 6:00am if the venue is 45 minutes away. You need 30 minutes to get dressed, load the car, and get out the door — so you're up at 5:30am. You want to eat breakfast 2.5 hours before your start, which means eating at 5:00am, which means the alarm goes off at 4:45am.

Now work backwards from that alarm. You want 7-8 hours in bed (not asleep — in bed, giving yourself the opportunity for sleep). That puts lights out at 9:00pm. Dinner needs to be finished and cleared by 8:00pm. Cooking starts at 7:00pm.

Write these times down. Put them on your phone. The number of riders who "just wing it" and then find themselves rushing through breakfast at 6:15am for a 7:00am start is extraordinary. Don't be one of them.

## The bike check: 15 minutes that prevent 90 per cent of mechanical problems

Do this the evening before. Not at 5am in the car park, not at the start line, not while you're waiting for registration. The evening before, in decent light, with time to fix anything you find.

### Tyres

Check the pressure with a track pump and gauge. Don't squeeze them with your thumb — that tells you nothing useful. Run your fingers around the tread and sidewalls, feeling for cuts, embedded glass, or bulges. If you find a cut that exposes the casing, replace the tyre. Tonight. Not tomorrow. Use our [tyre pressure calculator](/tools/tyre-pressure) if you're unsure of your numbers — the right pressure depends on your weight, tyre width, and the conditions you'll be riding in.

### Chain and drivetrain

The chain should be clean and lubricated. If it's black, gritty, or making noise, clean it and re-lube tonight. A contaminated chain costs you watts and increases the risk of a dropped chain or a skipping cassette — that moment's absolutely no fun when you're grinding up a hill in a group. Apply lube, wipe the excess, and let it sit overnight.

### Brake pads

Look at them. If you're on rim brakes, there should be visible grooves in the pad surface. If the surface is smooth and worn flat, replace them. If you're on disc brakes, check the pad thickness through the calliper — you need at least 1mm of pad material. Give each lever a firm squeeze and check that the wheel stops cleanly, without rubbing or juddering.

### Gear indexing

Shift through the entire cassette, both up and down, while turning the pedals. Every gear should click cleanly into place. If you're getting hesitation, ghost shifting, or noise in certain gears, adjust the barrel adjuster a quarter turn at a time until it's clean. Do this on the stand or with the bike flipped — not on the road tomorrow morning.

### Computer and lights

Charge your cycling computer tonight. Fully. Plug it in now and leave it until morning. Same for any lights — front and rear. Even for a daytime event, a rear light is increasingly a requirement at UK events and you might finish later than expected. Check that your route or course is loaded on the computer if the event provides a GPX file.

### Spares

Check that your spare tube matches your wheel size and valve type. Check that your CO2 cartridges aren't empty from last time. Check that your tyre levers aren't cracked. Check that your multi-tool has the Allen keys you need. This takes 60 seconds and prevents the "standing at the roadside with a useless spare" scenario that ruins more sportives than mechanical failure itself.

## Kit preparation: lay it all out

Everything you're going to wear or carry should be laid out tonight. Everything. Not stuffed in a bag, not "I know where it is." Physically laid out on a table, a chair, or the floor. Here is your checklist:

- Base layer
- Jersey
- Shorts or bibs
- Socks
- Shoes
- Gloves (check the forecast — full finger or mitts)
- Glasses
- Helmet
- Race number pinned to the jersey (pin it tonight, not at 5am with numb fingers)
- Arm warmers or a gilet if the forecast is borderline

Check the weather forecast — not a glance at the phone, but an hourly breakdown for the event location. The weather at your house may be different from the start line, especially if there's altitude involved. Pay attention to the temperature at your start time versus the predicted high — you might start in 8 degrees and finish in 18. A gilet you can stuff in a pocket solves this better than a heavy jacket you'll overheat in by 10am.

Here's where it gets really interesting, and where most people get caught: the number. Pin it to your jersey tonight. Four safety pins, one in each corner. A flapping number catches wind and, in the worst case, gets caught in your wheel. Pin it flat, pin it secure, do it tonight.

### Race nutrition

Portion out your on-bike food. Gels, bars, chews — whatever you've been training with. Put them in your jersey pockets now. If you're doing a 160km sportive, you need roughly one gel or equivalent every 30-40 minutes after the first hour. Count it out. Do not rely on feed stations alone — they might not have what you're used to.

Fill your bottles the night before and put them in the fridge. One with plain water, one with your electrolyte mix if you use one.

## The evening meal: boring wins

Your evening meal the night before a sportive should be three things: carb-forward, familiar, and unremarkable.

Pasta with a simple tomato sauce and chicken. Rice with salmon and vegetables. Jacket potato with beans and cheese. These are not exciting meals. That's the point. Asker Jeukendrup, one of the most cited sports nutritionists on the planet, consistently makes the same point: the pre-event meal is not the time for experimentation. It's the time for reliability.

Aim for 2-3g of carbohydrate per kilogram of your body weight at dinner. For an 80kg rider, that's 160-240g of carbs — roughly a large plate of pasta with bread, or a big bowl of rice with a side. Add a moderate portion of protein. Keep fat and fibre low — both slow gastric emptying, and you want your stomach clear by morning.

Eat by 7:30-8:00pm at the latest. This gives your gut three to four hours to process the meal before you try to sleep, and ensures you're not still digesting when the alarm sounds.

Hydration is simple. Drink normally through the evening. A glass of water with dinner, another before bed. You don't need to force litres down — your body will tell you. If your urine is pale straw colour before bed, you're fine. Avoid alcohol entirely. Even one glass of wine fragments sleep architecture and impairs recovery, and the mild dehydration is not worth it the night before a hard effort.

## Sleep: realistic expectations

Let me break this down, because sleep anxiety before an event is one of the most common things I hear about — and the worry itself makes the problem worse.

You will probably not sleep well the night before a sportive. Your brain is running through logistics. The alarm is set earlier than usual. You're slightly nervous. This is completely normal and, here's the good news, it barely matters.

Research from Waterloo and other sleep labs consistently shows that one night of reduced sleep has almost no measurable impact on endurance performance. Your body has reserves. The sleep that actually matters is two nights before the event. So if your sportive is Saturday, protect Thursday night. Get a full eight hours then, and Friday night becomes far less critical.

That said, give yourself the best chance:

- **Lights out early.** Even if you don't fall asleep immediately, being in bed in the dark with your eyes closed is better than scrolling your phone. Aim for 9:00-9:30pm.
- **Phone away.** Not on the bedside table — in another room, or at minimum face-down and on silent. You don't need to look at the event WhatsApp group at 10:30pm.
- **Room temperature.** Cooler is better. 16-18 degrees Celsius is the sweet spot. Crack a window if your room runs warm.
- **Alarm redundancy.** Set two alarms on two devices. The anxiety of "what if I sleep through it" is a genuine sleep disruptor. Remove the worry by having a backup.

If you find yourself lying there at 10:30pm, still awake, staring at the ceiling — that's fine. Don't catastrophise it. Your performance tomorrow will not suffer from one patchy night. Lie still, breathe slowly, let whatever happens happen.

## The morning routine: rehearsed, not improvised

The order matters, and the timing matters more.

**4:45am — Alarm.** Get up. Don't snooze. The schedule is tight and snoozing eats the margin you built in.

**4:50am — Coffee on.** If you're a coffee drinker, this is non-negotiable. The caffeine serves a dual purpose: it wakes you up, and it stimulates the gut. You need your gut to do its work before you leave the house, not 30km into the ride. Allow 20-30 minutes between the first sip and the expected result. Rushing this step is how you end up desperately searching for a hedge at the worst possible moment on the road. Every experienced sportive rider knows exactly what I'm talking about.

**5:00am — Breakfast.** Eat what you've tested in training. Porridge with honey and a banana is the classic. Toast with jam and peanut butter. A bagel with cream cheese. Rice pudding if you can stomach it that early. The principle is the same as dinner: carb-forward, familiar, nothing new. Aim for 1-2g of carbs per kilogram of body weight. Eat slowly. Drink a glass of water or diluted juice alongside it.

**5:20am — Toilet.** Give it time. The coffee and food will do their job. Do not rush this. Do not skip this. The consequences of leaving the house before your gut has had its say are predictable and unpleasant. This is one of those things nobody talks about in cycling, but every single experienced rider will tell you it's the most important part of the morning routine.

**5:30am — Kit on.** Everything is already laid out. Chamois cream applied. Number pinned. Pockets loaded with race nutrition. Shoes by the door. This should take five minutes because you did the thinking last night.

**5:40am — Load the car.** Bike in, wheels on or off depending on your rack. Bag in the boot. Bottles from the fridge. Quick scan: helmet, shoes, glasses, gloves. If it's not in the car now, it's not coming.

**5:45am — Drive.**

## The car bag: what future-you will thank you for

Pack a bag and leave it in the boot. This is for when you finish, and trust me — you will be in no state to think clearly. You'll be cold, stiff, probably emotional, and absolutely starving. Having this bag ready is the difference between a great day and a miserable drive home.

- **Dry clothes.** Clean t-shirt, joggers or tracksuit bottoms, dry socks, a warm hoodie. Peeling off wet, salty kit and putting on clean dry clothes is one of the best feelings in cycling.
- **Towel.** For wiping off sweat and road grime before you get in the car.
- **Post-ride food.** A recovery shake, a sandwich, a flapjack — something with carbs and protein you can eat immediately. Don't waste the post-ride recovery window queueing for an overpriced burger at the event village.
- **Cash or a card.** For coffee, for the car park, for the "I need something I didn't bring" moment.
- **Flip-flops or sliders.** Your feet will thank you.

## The mental side: anxiety is normal and temporary

If you're lying in bed the night before with a racing mind — that's not a problem, that's a sign you care about what you're doing. Anxiety before an event is a performance signal, not a performance problem. Your body is preparing. Adrenaline is doing what adrenaline does.

Here are the facts: you have done the training. The fitness is there, banked over weeks and months. Nothing that happens tonight or tomorrow morning will undo that. And the start line nerves? They disappear. Every single time. By kilometre 5, you'll be in the rhythm, breathing settled, legs turning over, wondering what you were so worried about.

The riders who have a good day are not the ones who slept perfectly and felt calm at the start line. They're the ones who had a plan, followed the plan, and didn't let the inevitable small hiccups derail them. A creaky knee at km 10 that fades by km 20. A headwind on the return leg that wasn't in the forecast. These are normal. They're fixable or they're tolerable, and either way, you keep pedalling.

## The mistakes that ruin sportives

I've watched hundreds of riders make these mistakes. Every single one is preventable.

**New kit or nutrition on event day.** That brand-new jersey you bought last week? Leave it in the drawer. Anything that hasn't been tested on at least two training rides stays at home. Chafing and pressure points from unfamiliar kit become unbearable over four or five hours. Same goes for nutrition — if you haven't trained with it, don't race with it. Your gut does not care that the feed station gels were free.

**Over-eating the night before.** More pasta does not mean more energy. Your glycogen stores have a ceiling, and once they're full, excess carbs just sit in your gut. If you're uncomfortably full at bedtime, you ate too much.

**Under-sleeping due to anxiety.** Do not stay up late scrolling through the event route or reading Strava segments. That information is not going to help you at 11pm. Phone down, lights out.

**Forgetting to charge devices.** Cycling computer, lights, heart rate monitor, power meter. Plug everything in by 8pm. A dead computer means riding blind on pacing.

**Skipping the bike check.** "The bike was fine last ride" is the sentence that precedes a slow puncture at km 3 or a chain that skips under load on the first climb. Fifteen minutes of checking prevents hours of suffering.

## By 8pm, you should be done

Bike checked. Kit laid out. Bag packed for the car. Bottles in the fridge. Alarms set. Evening meal eaten. Route loaded on the computer. There is nothing left to do — and that's the point. Every choice you make tonight is one fewer choice your sleep-deprived, nervous, 5am brain has to make tomorrow. You are setting up a conveyor belt: alarm, coffee, food, toilet, kit, car, go.

This is what preparation actually looks like. Not talent, not expensive equipment. Just a person who took 90 minutes the evening before to remove every possible source of friction from the day ahead. You're ready. Go have a great ride.

The word *cyclosportive* is French. That is not a minor detail — it tells you where this tradition was born and why France remains the centre of gravity for amateur cycling events anywhere in the world.

The idea is simple and slightly mad: take the same roads the professionals race on, often on the same day or the day before, close them to traffic, mark them with the same signage you see on television, and let thousands of amateurs ride them. Not as a race, exactly — there are timing chips and classifications, but most people are racing the mountain, not each other. The Tour de France built the mythology of the cols. The French sportive scene lets you ride inside it.

I have ridden sportives in Italy, Spain, Belgium, and across the UK. Nothing compares to the French ones. The organisation, the feed stations, the spectators lining switchbacks with cowbells, the sheer drama of arriving at the summit of the Galibier knowing the Tour peloton will be on that exact spot 48 hours later — France does this better than anyone because France invented it.

What follows are the four events I think every serious amateur cyclist should have on their list, plus four more worth knowing about. For the big four, we have published full 16-week training guides that go deep on preparation, gearing, fuelling, and pacing. This page is the overview. Start here, then go deeper on whichever one calls to you.

## Etape du Tour

The Etape du Tour is the biggest sportive in the world by profile, and it earns that status every year. The format has not changed since it started in 1993: one stage of the Tour de France, ridden on closed roads, the day before the professionals race it. The route changes annually because the Tour route changes — one year you might be riding over the Tourmalet, the next over the Col de la Loze or up Alpe d'Huez. That unpredictability is part of the appeal. You sign up not knowing exactly what you will face, only that it will be a full mountain stage.

The distance varies with each year's route, typically between 130 km and 170 km with anywhere from 3,000 m to 5,000 m of climbing. The roads are fully closed to traffic — a privilege almost no other sportive can match at this scale, with around 15,000 riders on the road. The atmosphere on the climbs is extraordinary. Spectators camp at the summits. Hand-painted names cover the tarmac. Feed stations are stocked with food that goes well beyond gels and bananas.

The Etape suits any rider who has watched the Tour de France on television and imagined being on those roads. You do not need to be fast. You need to be fit enough to ride the distance, prepared enough to fuel correctly, and geared low enough to spin up whatever gradient the organisers throw at you. A compact chainset with a 34x34 cassette is a sensible starting point for most riders.

Registration opens in October or November and sells out in minutes. I am not exaggerating — popular routes go in under 15 minutes. Pre-register on the Le Tour website, have your payment details saved, and set an alarm. Travel operators hold block entries if you prefer a package with accommodation and transfers.

We have written a full [Etape du Tour training guide](/blog/etape-du-tour-training-plan) covering the 16-week build — base, build, taper, gearing, fuelling, and race-day pacing. Start there if you have an entry confirmed.

## La Marmotte Alpes

La Marmotte is the sportive that other sportives are measured against. The numbers alone are sobering: 174 km, roughly 5,000 m of climbing, four cols that define Alpine cycling — the Col du Glandon, the Col du Telegraphe, the Col du Galibier, and a finish on Alpe d'Huez. You ride all four in a single day. For most riders, that means eight to twelve hours in the saddle.

The Glandon comes first, around 25 km into the ride, and it sets the tone. It is long, irregular, and exposes anyone who started too fast. The descent into the Maurienne Valley is cold and technical. Then the Telegraphe, which functions as a warm-up for the Galibier — the two climbs are separated by a short descent through Valloire and together represent roughly 35 km of continuous uphill. The Galibier summit sits at 2,642 m and the air is noticeably thinner. You feel it in your legs and your lungs. After the Galibier, the descent to Bourg-d'Oisans is fast and long, and you arrive at the bottom knowing you still have 13.8 km of climbing to the top of Alpe d'Huez. The 21 numbered hairpins are famous for a reason.

This event takes place in early July, usually the first or second weekend. It fills within weeks of registration opening, but it is more accessible than the Etape — you have days rather than minutes to secure an entry. Bourg-d'Oisans is the natural base, sitting at the foot of Alpe d'Huez with plenty of accommodation options.

La Marmotte demands genuine climbing fitness. You should be comfortable holding 85 to 95 percent of FTP for sustained 30-minute efforts and have completed training rides of five to six hours with significant elevation. Use the [FTP Zone Calculator](/tools/ftp-zones) to establish your training zones and the [VAM Calculator](/tools/vam) to benchmark your climbing speed against the grades you will face on the Galibier — sustained sections at 7 to 9 percent for over an hour.

## Marmotte Granfondo Pyrenees

The Pyrenean Marmotte is the younger sibling, and it does not get the same attention as the Alpine original. That is a mistake. The route covers roughly 170 km with around 4,700 m of climbing, including the Col du Tourmalet and the Col d'Aubisque — two of the most storied climbs in Tour de France history. The Tourmalet has appeared in the Tour more than any other climb. The Aubisque, approached from the western side, is one of the most beautiful road cycling descents in Europe, dropping through a hanging valley with views that would stop you mid-pedal stroke if your legs were not already begging for mercy.

The Pyrenees ride differently to the Alps. The climbs are generally steeper and more variable in gradient — the Tourmalet averages 7.4 percent but has regular kicks above 10 percent that punish riders who sit at a steady tempo. The roads are narrower, more technical on descents, and more remote. You will see fewer cyclists, fewer support vehicles, and fewer spectators than at the Alpine Marmotte. For some riders, that is precisely the appeal.

The other difference is heat. The Pyrenees in late June or early July can be brutally hot at lower elevations — mid-30s Celsius in the valleys. Your [hydration strategy](/tools/hydration) matters more here than in the Alps, where altitude keeps temperatures manageable. Carry more fluid than you think you need, and use every feed station.

The event runs in late June or early July. Fly into Toulouse or Pau. Accommodation in the Pyrenean valleys is more scattered and smaller-scale than in the Alps — book early and expect village guesthouses rather than resort hotels. The riding culture in the Pyrenees is quieter and more local than in the Alps, and that gives it a character that the bigger events cannot replicate.

## Haute Route Alps

The Haute Route Alps is not a sportive in the traditional sense. It is a seven-stage mountain race across the Alps, covering roughly 800 km with over 20,000 m of climbing across a week. Each stage is a standalone mountain day — typically 100 km to 140 km with 3,000 m to 4,000 m of climbing. Then you wake up the next morning and do it again. And again. For seven consecutive days.

The route changes annually, but the character does not: this is a Tour de France for amateurs, with professional-level organisation, timed stages, feed zones, mechanical support, medical cover, and a rolling road closure. The event runs in August, usually over eight days including a rest day, and attracts riders from across Europe and beyond.

What makes the Haute Route different from a single-day sportive is the cumulative load. Day one might feel manageable. Day three is where most riders discover whether their training has prepared them for sustained multi-day efforts. By day five, the limiting factor is not fitness — it is recovery. Sleep quality, nutrition between stages, and the discipline to ride within yourself on days when your legs feel good become the variables that determine whether you finish.

Training for the Haute Route requires a fundamentally different approach to training for a single-day event. You need back-to-back long days in your programme — not just one six-hour ride, but two or three consecutive days of four to five hours with sustained climbing. You need to practise your post-ride recovery routine: what you eat within the first 30 minutes, how you manage sleep in unfamiliar accommodation, whether your body can absorb enough calories across a week of 4,000 to 5,000 kcal daily expenditure. ## Also Worth Your Entry Fee

Beyond the big four, France has a depth of sportive culture that no other country matches. Four events deserve a mention.

**L'Ardechoise** is the people's sportive. Held in the rolling hills of the Ardeche in southern France every June, it offers 16 different routes from 60 km to 310 km, and around 10,000 riders take part. It is not about legendary cols — it is about the festival atmosphere, the village feed stations serving local food, and the sheer range of options that make it accessible to everyone from first-time sportive riders to ultra-endurance veterans. If you want to experience what French cyclosportive culture feels like at its most democratic, this is the event.

**Tour du Mont Blanc Cyclosportive** takes you around the highest peak in Western Europe, crossing from France into Italy and Switzerland before returning. The route is roughly 330 km with around 8,000 m of climbing over two or three days, depending on the format. The scenery is Alpine postcard material — glaciers, high meadows, border crossings at altitude — and the logistics of riding through three countries in a weekend make it a proper adventure.

**Paris-Brest-Paris** is the grandfather of endurance cycling. Established in 1891 — predating the Tour de France by 12 years — this 1,200 km audax is held every four years, with the next edition falling in 2027. You ride from Paris to Brest and back, unsupported, within 90 hours. It is not a sportive in the French sense — it is an audax, with control points rather than timing chips — but it belongs on this list because it is the most prestigious long-distance cycling event in the world and it happens on French roads.

**Raid Pyreneen** challenges riders to traverse the entire Pyrenees from the Atlantic to the Mediterranean — or the reverse — covering 720 km with over 11,000 m of climbing. It is not a mass-start event but a brevet you can ride at any time during the summer season, collecting stamps at control points along the way. The Raid rewards self-sufficiency, route-finding, and the kind of stubbornness that keeps you pedalling over the Col du Somport at dusk with 200 km still to ride.

## Alps vs Pyrenees: What the Difference Means for Your Training

If you are choosing between an Alpine and a Pyrenean sportive, the distinction matters more than geography.

**Climbing profile.** Alpine cols are generally longer and more consistent in gradient. The Galibier is 17.7 km from the Telegraphe junction at an average of 6.9 percent — long, steady, relentless. The Tourmalet is shorter at 17.1 km but averages 7.4 percent with multiple sections above 10 percent. Alpine climbing rewards a rider who can hold a steady watts-per-kilo output for 60 to 90 minutes. Pyrenean climbing rewards a rider who can handle surges — steep ramps that demand bursts above threshold, followed by brief recovery sections. Use the [W/kg Calculator](/tools/wkg) to benchmark where you stand, and the [Climb Time Calculator](/tools/climb-time) to model your expected times on specific cols.

**Altitude.** The Galibier summit sits at 2,642 m. The Col de l'Iseran, which sometimes features in Haute Route stages, reaches 2,770 m. At these altitudes, oxygen availability drops by roughly 20 to 25 percent compared to sea level, and your sustainable power output drops with it. The Tourmalet tops out at 2,115 m — still high, but the physiological cost is meaningfully lower. If you live and train at sea level, altitude matters. You will feel it above 2,000 m, and it gets worse above 2,500 m. There is no substitute for spending time at altitude before an Alpine event, even if that means arriving a few days early and riding some easier passes to acclimatise.

**Heat.** The Pyrenees in summer are consistently hotter than the Alps at equivalent elevations. Valley temperatures in the western Pyrenees regularly exceed 35 degrees Celsius in late June and July. Your hydration strategy for a Pyrenean sportive should account for significantly higher fluid and electrolyte needs. The [Hydration Calculator](/tools/hydration) can help you model requirements based on expected temperature and duration.

**Road character.** Alpine roads, especially on the big cols, tend to be wider and better surfaced — these are major through routes that see heavy traffic outside event days. Pyrenean roads are narrower, more technical, and less frequently maintained. Descending the Aubisque or the Col de Peyresourde requires sharper bike handling than descending Alpe d'Huez. If your descending is a weakness, the Pyrenees will expose it more than the Alps.

**Training implications.** For an Alpine event, build your programme around sustained threshold climbing — 20- to 40-minute efforts at 88 to 95 percent of FTP, ideally on the longest hills you can find. For a Pyrenean event, include more over-under intervals that simulate the gradient changes: two minutes at 105 percent, three minutes at 85 percent, repeated across a 30-minute block. Both require a base of five- to six-hour endurance rides with significant climbing. ## Registration, Travel, and Logistics

**Getting entries.** The Etape du Tour is the hardest entry to secure — registration opens once per year, typically in October or November, and popular routes sell out in minutes. Create your account on the Le Tour website well in advance. La Marmotte fills within weeks rather than minutes. The Haute Route operates on a rolling entry system and rarely sells out before spring. L'Ardechoise is the most accessible — entry remains open for months.

**Flights and transport.** For Alpine events, fly into Geneva, Lyon, or Grenoble. For Pyrenean events, Toulouse and Pau are the closest airports. The TGV train network in France is excellent and accepts bike bags in the luggage area — the Paris-Lyon or Paris-Toulouse routes are fast and convenient if you are connecting through the capital. Car hire gives you flexibility for reaching mountain accommodation but parking at event starts can be chaotic.

**Accommodation.** Alpine resort towns like Bourg-d'Oisans, Briançon, and Albertville have good hotel infrastructure geared towards cyclists. Pyrenean accommodation is more scattered — expect village guesthouses, small hotels, and the occasional campsite. Book as early as possible for La Marmotte and the Etape — the good places within riding distance of the start fill months in advance. For the Haute Route, accommodation is included in the entry package.

**Bike logistics.** If you are flying, a hard bike box or semi-rigid bag is worth the investment. French airports handle bike boxes routinely. If you prefer not to fly with your bike, several rental companies in Geneva, Nice, and Bourg-d'Oisans offer high-end road bikes — but test the fit thoroughly before event day. Nothing ruins a 174 km mountain ride faster than a saddle height that is five millimetres too low.

## French Cycling Culture: Why It Matters

The culture surrounding cycling in France is different from anywhere else, and it shapes the sportive experience in ways that are hard to explain until you have been there.

Feed stations at French sportives are not an afterthought. They are events within the event. Expect tables loaded with dried fruit, nougat de Montelimar, local cheese, bread, pretzels, Coca-Cola, water, and sometimes wine — yes, wine, at the later stations, because this is France. Volunteers cheer you through. Local spectators set up camping chairs at switchbacks and ring cowbells. The atmosphere on a climb during a French sportive is closer to a mountain stage of the Tour than anything you will find elsewhere.

The café ride is embedded in French cycling culture in a way it is not in the UK. You stop. You eat. You drink a coffee that is better than it has any right to be given that you are in a village of 200 people on the side of a mountain. This carries into sportives — riders do not treat feed stations as something to blast through. They stop, refuel properly, and carry on. If you are used to British or American sportives where the culture is to ride through as fast as possible, adjust your expectations. Stopping is not weakness. Stopping is French.

The culture of the col — collecting climbs, tracking which passes you have ridden, comparing notes on the Galibier versus the Tourmalet versus the Ventoux — is woven into French amateur cycling. Many riders wear jerseys marking specific cols they have conquered. The idea that a mountain pass is an achievement worth commemorating, not just an obstacle to be endured, is something France understands better than any other cycling nation.

## Where to Start

If this is your first French sportive, the Etape du Tour is the obvious choice — the closed roads, the atmosphere, and the variation in route difficulty from year to year mean it is accessible to a wider range of riders than La Marmotte. Secure an entry first, then build a 16-week training block around the route.

If you are already comfortable with six-hour mountain days and you want the hardest single-day challenge France offers, La Marmotte is the benchmark. Four cols, 5,000 m, finishing on Alpe d'Huez. Nothing else in European amateur cycling matches it for sheer ambition.

If you want the Pyrenees — the heat, the steep ramps, the wildness — the Marmotte Pyrenees gives you the Tourmalet and the Aubisque in a single day.

And if you want to find out what you are truly made of, the Haute Route Alps will answer that question across seven stages and 20,000 metres of climbing.

France gave amateur cycling its language, its traditions, and its best roads. The sportives are the way in.

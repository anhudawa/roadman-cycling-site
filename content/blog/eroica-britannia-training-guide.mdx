Eroica Britannia is not the Italian original on English soil. It is something harder. 100 miles of Peak District lanes, gravel tracks, and farm roads on a pre-1987 steel bike with downtube shifters and toe clips. The climbs are steeper than Tuscany. The weather is worse than Tuscany. The surfaces are rougher than Tuscany. And the gearing restrictions that make L'Eroica a romantic challenge make Eroica Britannia a brutal one, because Derbyshire does not do gentle gradients.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about this event. The fitness matters. The bike matters. But the real problem is that the Peak District puts 18% ramps under your wheels when your easiest gear is 42x24, your brakes barely work, and you are standing on toe clips in the rain trying not to stall on wet gravel. That combination — steep, slippery, and restricted — is what separates Eroica Britannia from every other sportive on the UK calendar.

Here is how to train for it across 12 weeks, with the climbing specificity, the surface adaptation, and the bike rehearsal that turn the vintage bike from a liability into something you actually trust.

## Key Takeaways

- Eroica Britannia covers up to 100 miles across Peak District terrain on a pre-1987 vintage bike — the combination of steep Derbyshire gradients and restricted gearing makes every climb a strength test.
- The 100-mile route climbs over 2,500m on roads that regularly hit 15-20% — on a bike where 42x24 is often your easiest gear. Low-cadence climbing strength is non-negotiable.
- Mixed surfaces — tarmac, farm tracks, gravel paths — demand comfort on rough terrain and confidence in braking with vintage caliper brakes that lose effectiveness in the wet.
- Peak District weather adds a genuine wildcard: June temperatures can swing from 8 to 22 degrees in a single day, and rain turns gravel tracks into mud that clogs vintage brakes.
- A 12-week build with progressive long rides on the actual race bike is the minimum — at least 6 rides of 4+ hours on the vintage bike before event day.
- Fuelling is harder on a vintage bike — reaching for bottles and food with downtube shifters and toe clips demands practice, not improvisation.
- The event is half race, half festival. Pacing the first 40 miles conservatively is the difference between riding the final climbs and walking them.

## What Eroica Britannia Actually Demands

Eroica Britannia starts and finishes in Bakewell, Derbyshire — the market town at the heart of the Peak District. Four route options: 30 miles, 55 miles, 75 miles, and 100 miles. All routes share the same terrain DNA: narrow Peak District lanes that pitch upward without warning, gravel tracks through farmland, cattle grids, loose surfaces, and gradients that belong on a Ronde van Vlaanderen profile more than an English sportive.

The 100-mile route climbs around 2,500 metres. That number alone would be a serious day on a modern bike. On a vintage bike with 42x24 as your smallest gear, it becomes a different kind of effort entirely. The climbs are not long Alpine drags — they are short, steep, and repeated. 12%. 15%. 18%. Over and over, all day, with minimal recovery between them. The Peak District landscape rolls constantly. Nothing is flat for long.

The surfaces are what make Eroica Britannia distinct from its Italian parent. L'Eroica's strade bianche are smooth chalk-dust over compacted earth. The Peak District's off-road sections are rougher — farm tracks with loose gravel, rutted lanes, waterlogged gateways, and patches of grass between stone walls. After rain, some of these sections become properly muddy, and vintage caliper brakes on chrome rims lose 60-70% of their stopping power in the wet. That is not an exaggeration. That is a fact you need to train for.

The weather. June in the Peak District is not June on the Mediterranean. Morning temperatures of 8-10 degrees are normal. So are afternoon temperatures of 20-22 degrees. So is horizontal rain at midday followed by sunshine at two o'clock. The combination of temperature swings, wind exposure on the moors, and wet gravel tracks creates a layering problem that wool jerseys solve imperfectly and modern kit solves better — but the event has a dress code, and the dress code is vintage.

The bike rules require a pre-1987 steel frame with downtube shifters or non-aero brake levers. Toe clips, not clipless. Most riders run a 52/42 chainring with a 5 or 6-speed freewheel — typically 14-24 or 14-28 if you can find one. The gearing limitation is the multiplier on every other difficulty. A 15% gradient on 34x32 is hard. A 15% gradient on 42x24 at 45 RPM on a bike that weighs 12kg with wet-weather braking and toe clips is a different sport.

## Course Analysis: Critical Sections

### The Opening Miles — Deceptively Rolling

The first 15-20 miles out of Bakewell follow the Wye and Derwent valleys on a mix of lanes and tracks. The terrain looks gentle on the profile. It is not. The short ramps between villages — 200-400 metres at 10-14% — are the ones that set the pacing tone for the day. Riders who attack these early pitches at 250-280 watts on the vintage bike arrive at the first major climb with less in the legs than they realise. The rolling terrain of the first third is where Eroica Britannia takes your matches quietly.

Let me break this down. On a modern bike with 34x28, those short ramps are 90-second efforts at 90 RPM. On a vintage bike with 42x24, the same ramp is a 90-second grind at 50-55 RPM with your full bodyweight on the pedals. The neuromuscular cost is different. The glycogen cost is different. Five of those ramps before the first feed station and your legs are carrying a debt that does not show up on a heart rate monitor.

### The Major Climbs

The specific climbs vary year to year, but the route consistently includes gradients that would be difficult on a modern road bike. Monsal Head — a classic Peak District climb with ramps above 15% — appears on most route iterations. The dale climbs out of Lathkill Dale and Long Dale pitch upward on narrow lanes where the surface degrades at the steepest point. These are not climbs where you can sit and spin. On 42x24, you are standing, weighting the pedals through the full stroke, and hoping the rear tyre holds traction on loose tarmac.

The cumulative climbing is the real problem. No single climb on the course is longer than 2-3 kilometres. But the density of climbing — a steep pitch every 3-5 miles, relentlessly, for 100 miles — creates a fatigue pattern that road cyclists rarely train for. Your legs never fully recover between efforts. By mile 60, every 12% ramp feels like 18%.

### The Gravel and Off-Road Sections

The off-road sections are where Eroica Britannia becomes its own event. Farm tracks through the White Peak. Gravel paths along disused railway lines. Rutted lanes between dry stone walls where the surface changes from tarmac to gravel to mud within 50 metres. On a modern gravel bike with disc brakes and 40mm tyres, these sections are fun. On a vintage road bike with 25-28mm tyres and caliper brakes, they demand concentration, confidence, and a willingness to feather brakes you do not fully trust.

The effort cost of off-road sections on a vintage bike is 15-25% higher than the same sections on a modern gravel setup. You are fighting the bike more. The steering is heavier. The tyres track less predictably. The braking distances are longer. And every section of rough surface shakes the bars, the saddle, and the rider in ways that accumulate across eight hours.

### The Final 20 Miles

This is where the event is won or lost. The final 20 miles back toward Bakewell include some of the steepest climbs on the course. By this point, you have 80 miles and 2,000+ metres of climbing in your legs. Your hands are tired from gripping the bars on rough surfaces. Your feet are sore from toe clips. Your lower back is complaining about a position that has not changed in seven hours. And the road pitches upward at 16%.

The riders who paced the first 40 miles conservatively still have the muscular reserve to grind up these final climbs at 45 RPM. The riders who went too hard in the opening third are walking. There is no shame in walking — plenty of experienced cyclists walk the final climbs at Eroica Britannia — but if you want to ride the whole thing, the pacing has to start at mile one.

## 12-Week Training Framework

This plan assumes a starting base of 6-8 hours per week, regular riding on hills, and access to a vintage bike for at least the final eight weeks. If you have never ridden a vintage bike, add 6-8 weeks of acclimatisation before this block.

### Weeks 1-4: Base + Vintage Bike Acclimatisation

Volume: 6-9 hours/week.

Four to five rides, 80% in [Zone 2](/blog/zone-2-cycling-heart-rate-vs-power-vs-rpe). The aerobic foundation for an 8-10 hour event day. [Stephen Seiler](/guests/stephen-seiler)'s research on training intensity distribution matters here — the easy rides have to be actually easy, because the specific work in weeks 5-8 needs legs that are fresh enough to absorb the stimulus.

Anchor session: one long ride building from 3 hours to 4.5 hours over the block, ideally on hilly terrain. If you have the vintage bike, ride it at least once a week from week 2 — even if it is just a 90-minute spin around local lanes. The goal is comfort, not fitness. Learn the gear changes. Learn the braking distances. Learn how to stand on toe clips without the pedal flipping. Learn how to reach for a bottle with your left hand while your right hand stays on the bars near a downtube shifter.

One weekly hill ride on any bike — 4-6 short climbs at a steady effort, practising seated climbing at cadences below 60 RPM. Use a heavier gear than normal on your modern bike (53x17 on a 6-8% gradient) to simulate the cadence constraint.

### Weeks 5-8: Build with Hill-Specific Work

Volume: 7-10 hours/week.

Two quality sessions per week. One threshold session: 4x8 minutes at 95-100% FTP, building to 3x12 minutes by week 8. One low-cadence hill session: 5x4 minutes at 50-55 RPM on a gradient above 8%, at RPE 7-8/10. Rest 3 minutes between efforts. This is the session that builds the muscular endurance Eroica Britannia demands — [John Wakefield](/guests/john-wakefield) prescribes this kind of torque work for event-specific preparation, and at this event it has direct application to every climb on the course.

Long ride extends to 5-6 hours by week 8, with at least 1,200m of climbing. Ride the vintage bike for at least two of these long rides. Include a section of gravel or rough surface — a canal towpath, a farm track, a bridleway — to build confidence in the bike's handling on loose surfaces.

Add one mixed-surface ride per week: 2-3 hours on a combination of road and off-road. The goal is comfort. You should be able to ride a loose gravel track at 25 km/h on the vintage bike without gripping the bars like you are trying to strangle them.

### Weeks 9-11: Peak with Race-Simulation Rides

Volume: 8-12 hours/week.

This is the block that decides your Eroica Britannia experience. At least 4 of the long rides in these three weeks should be on the vintage bike.

Week 9: Long ride of 5-6 hours on hilly terrain with 1,500m+ of climbing on the vintage bike. Include at least 30 minutes of gravel. Practise fuelling at race-day intake — 60-80g carbs/hour. Practise drinking from bottles mounted on the downtube while riding at 30 km/h on a rough surface. If you cannot do it smoothly, you need more practice, not more fitness.

Week 10: The simulation ride. 6-7 hours on the vintage bike, ideally on Peak District terrain if you can get to it, otherwise on the hilliest local terrain available. 1,800-2,200m of climbing. Mixed surfaces. Full race-day kit. Full race-day nutrition. This ride is the rehearsal. Every problem you find — saddle discomfort at hour 5, cold hands on the descent, toe clips that slip when you stand — is a problem you can fix before event day. Every problem you do not find is a problem that finds you at mile 70.

Week 11: Back-to-back days on the vintage bike. Saturday 4-5 hours hilly with quality efforts. Sunday 3 hours easy on mixed surfaces. The second day on tired legs is the closest you will get to the fatigue of miles 70-100 without riding the event itself.

Quality sessions tighten: one threshold session (3x10 minutes at 95-100% FTP), one low-cadence hill session (6x3 minutes at 50-55 RPM, gradient above 10%, RPE 8/10). These are short, sharp, and specific.

### Week 12: Taper

Volume drops 40-50%. Three rides: Monday 90 minutes easy, Wednesday 75 minutes with 3x5 minutes at threshold as openers, Friday 60 minutes easy spin on the vintage bike to keep the legs familiar with the position.

No new training stimulus. No long rides. No experiments. The fitness is in. Trust it.

## Key Sessions That Simulate Eroica Britannia's Demands

### Low-Cadence Hill Repeats

The session that directly transfers to Eroica Britannia's climbing profile. Find a climb of 8-12% gradient, 3-5 minutes long. Ride it in a gear that holds your cadence at 50-55 RPM. Effort at RPE 7-8/10 — hard but repeatable. Recover on the descent plus 2 minutes. Repeat 5-6 times.

On a modern bike, use 53x17 or equivalent. On the vintage bike, use whatever gear puts you in that cadence window on that gradient — which is probably your easiest gear. The point is not power output. The point is teaching your legs to produce force at low cadence for sustained efforts without cramping, without losing form, and without burning through glycogen at the rate that high-torque efforts normally demand.

### Mixed-Surface Long Rides

A 4-5 hour ride that includes at least 60 minutes of off-road — gravel tracks, bridleways, farm lanes. The transition between tarmac and gravel on a vintage bike is where concentration lapses cost the most. Your hands need to learn the different braking feel. Your body needs to learn the different vibration pattern. Your eyes need to learn to read the surface 20 metres ahead instead of 5 metres ahead.

### Steep Gradient Climbing Intervals

Different from the low-cadence repeats. Find a gradient above 15% — a short, steep ramp of 200-400 metres. Ride it on the vintage bike. Standing. Full weight on the pedals. Toe clips engaged. The point is not sustained effort — it is the specific skill of keeping a heavy bike moving at 6-8 km/h on a gradient where the bike wants to stop. Practise getting out of the saddle smoothly. Practise restarting on a steep gradient with toe clips. Practise the moment where you decide to walk versus the moment where you can still ride. That decision at mile 80 depends on having made it 30 times in training.

### Back-to-Back Days on the Vintage Bike

Saturday: 5 hours hilly on the vintage bike with structured efforts. Sunday: 3 hours easy on the vintage bike with off-road sections. The Sunday ride matters more than the Saturday ride. Sunday is the day that teaches your body what mile 80 feels like — the position is uncomfortable, the hands are sore, the lower back is tight, and the climbs are still there. If you can ride Sunday's climbs without walking, you can ride the last 20 miles of Eroica Britannia without walking.

## Nutrition and Fuelling Strategy

Fuelling on a vintage bike is mechanically harder than on a modern bike. The bottle cages are on the downtube, not the seat tube. Reaching for a bottle means taking one hand off the bars and bending forward while your feet are strapped into toe clips — on a rough surface, at speed, on a bike with no aero brake hoods to rest on. Practise this. Practise it on gravel. Practise it until it is automatic, because at mile 60 you will not have the concentration to think about it.

**On the bike.** 60-80g carbs/hour from the gun. Gels in the jersey pocket, bars in the back pocket, concentrated electrolyte mix in one bottle, plain water in the other. The jersey pocket on a wool jersey is smaller than a modern jersey pocket — plan the carry before race day. Three gels and two bars per pocket is a reasonable loadout for a 2-3 hour window between feed stations.

**Aid stations.** Eroica Britannia's aid stations are part of the event's charm. Cake. Tea. Sandwiches. The atmosphere is friendly and the temptation to linger is real. Keep stops to 10-15 minutes. Eat real food. Drink something hot if it is cold. Fill bottles. Leave. Every minute sitting is a minute of cooling legs, stiffening back, and tightening hamstrings. The riders who spend 25 minutes at every stop finish in the dark.

**Pre-race nutrition.** 8-10g carbs per kilogram of body weight in the 24-36 hours before the event. A Bakewell pudding the night before is period-appropriate and calorie-dense. Breakfast 3 hours before the start: porridge, toast, banana, coffee. Nothing new. Nothing untested.

**Hydration.** 500-750ml/hour depending on temperature and effort. June in the Peak District can be cool enough that you forget to drink — the wind hides the sweat. Set a timer on your watch if you need to. Electrolyte mix in every bottle. Sodium at 500-1,000mg/hour in warmer conditions.

**Caffeine.** A caffeine gel 30 minutes before the hardest climbing section sharpens the effort. Two well-timed caffeine hits across a 100-mile ride — one at mile 30, one at mile 70 — are worth more than caffeine at every feed.

For the underlying fuelling science, the [carbs-per-hour guide](/blog/carbohydrate-per-hour-cyclists) covers gut training and absorption rates in detail.

## Kit and Equipment Considerations

**The vintage bike.** Service it properly before the event. Cables, brake pads, chain, freewheel — all need checking. Vintage components are less tolerant of wear than modern ones, and a slipping freewheel at mile 50 is a day-ending problem. If you are borrowing or renting a bike, get it at least six weeks before the event and ride it weekly.

**Tyres.** The most consequential equipment choice. 25-28mm clinchers are standard on vintage road bikes. For Eroica Britannia's mixed surfaces, run the widest tyre your frame will clear — 28mm if possible, 25mm if not. Tread pattern matters: a tyre with some texture handles wet gravel and farm tracks better than a slick. Run pressure at 80-90 PSI for a 75kg rider — lower than road, higher than modern gravel. Carry a spare tube, tyre levers, and a frame pump. CO2 inflators do not fit vintage valve stems reliably.

**Clothing.** The event expects vintage-style clothing, and most riders wear a wool jersey. Wool is excellent in cool, dry conditions and truly terrible in sustained rain — it absorbs water, gets heavy, and takes hours to dry. The practical compromise: a modern baselayer underneath the wool jersey, modern bib shorts, wool socks, and a packable modern gilet in the jersey pocket for descents and rain. A cycling cap under the helmet is both period-correct and functionally useful for rain, sun, and warmth.

Layer for the temperature swing. Start with arm warmers and a gilet for the cool morning. Strip to the jersey for the warm afternoon. Add layers for the late-afternoon chill. A 14-degree temperature swing in a single day is normal in the Peak District in June, and the riders who prepare for only one temperature discover the other.

**Spares and tools.** Multi-tool, spare tube, tyre levers, frame pump, chain quick link, a small rag for cleaning mud from brakes. Vintage components have quirks — carry a spare brake cable if yours is more than two years old. Toe-clip straps loosen over time; check them at every feed stop.

**Lighting.** The 100-mile route can take 8-10 hours. If you start at 7am and ride conservatively, you may finish in daylight. If you are slower, start earlier, or the weather slows you, a front and rear light is non-negotiable. Clip-on lights that fit vintage handlebars are worth sourcing before the event.

## Race Week and Race Day Logistics

**Travel to Bakewell.** The town is accessible by car from the M1 via the A6. Parking fills early on event morning — arrive the day before if possible. Accommodation in Bakewell books out months ahead; Matlock, Buxton, and Ashbourne are alternatives within 20 minutes.

**Registration and the festival.** Eroica Britannia runs a multi-day festival alongside the ride: bike jumble, vintage bike displays, live music, food stalls. Registration is typically the day before. Collect your number, check the route briefing, and walk through the bike expo. The atmosphere is warm, enthusiastic, and distinctly different from a standard UK sportive.

**Pacing plan.** This is where most riders get it wrong.

*30-mile route:* Ride at a comfortable tempo throughout. Use it as a day to enjoy the event, the scenery, and the vintage bike experience. No specific pacing required beyond sensible effort management.

*55-mile route:* Pace the first 20 miles at high Zone 2. Eat from mile 15. Ride the middle section at steady tempo. Save 10% for the final climbs. Aim for a 4-5 hour day.

*75-mile route:* Treat it as two rides. The first 40 miles at controlled Zone 2 effort with disciplined eating. The second 35 miles at whatever you have left, with the understanding that the final climbs will be hard regardless of pacing. Aim for 5-7 hours.

*100-mile route:* The first 40 miles are the investment. Ride them 5-10% easier than you think you should. Eat every 20 minutes from the start. The temptation to push on the early climbs is strong — the legs are fresh, the group is enthusiastic, the atmosphere is electric. Resist it. The return on conservative early pacing is paid back at mile 80 when the gradients hit 16% and the riders who went too hard are walking. Aim for 7-10 hours depending on fitness and stops.

**Warm-up.** Minimal. A 15-minute spin around Bakewell to check the gears, the brakes, and the toe clips. The first miles of the route are the warm-up. Save the legs.

## Common Mistakes Riders Make at Eroica Britannia

**Not riding the vintage bike enough before the event.** This is the most common mistake, and it is the most consequential. Everything about the vintage bike is different from your modern bike. The gearing, the braking, the position, the handling, the pedal retention, the shift mechanism. Riders who train on carbon all spring and collect their rental steel bike the morning of the event are the riders walking the climbs at mile 50. Six rides of 4+ hours on the vintage bike is the minimum. More is better.

**Wrong tyre choice for mixed terrain.** Slick road tyres on wet Peak District gravel are dangerous. The front wheel washes out on loose corners. The rear wheel spins on steep climbs. The wrong tyre choice turns a rideable section into a walking section and a rideable corner into a crash. Run the widest tyre your frame clears, with some tread pattern, at slightly lower pressure than you would on dry road.

**Going too hard on the early climbs.** The Peak District's early climbs feel manageable on fresh legs. They are also the climbs that set the debt for the final 20 miles. Pace by heart rate, not by feel. If your heart rate is above Zone 3 in the first 30 miles, you are going too hard. The event is 8 hours, not 90 minutes. Ride like it.

**Ignoring weather preparation.** June in the Peak District is not reliable weather. Rain is common. Wind is constant. Temperature swings are large. The riders who check the forecast the night before and pack accordingly finish comfortably. The riders who assume it will be fine because it is June finish cold, wet, and miserable. Pack a gilet. Pack arm warmers. Pack a thin rain layer that fits under a wool jersey. The weight penalty is 200 grams. The comfort difference is the entire day.

**Spending too long at aid stations.** The aid stations are wonderful. The cake is excellent. The tea is hot. The atmosphere is friendly. And every minute you spend sitting down is a minute of cooling muscles, stiffening joints, and losing momentum. 10-15 minutes per stop. Eat. Drink. Fill bottles. Leave. The riders who treat every aid station as a 30-minute break are the riders still on course at 9pm.

**Underestimating fuelling on a vintage bike.** Reaching for food and bottles is physically harder on a vintage bike than a modern one. Downtube bottle cages require a different movement. Toe clips restrict how much you can shift your weight. Jersey pockets are smaller in wool. Practise eating and drinking on the move during training. If you cannot do it smoothly on gravel at 25 km/h, the event is not the place to learn.

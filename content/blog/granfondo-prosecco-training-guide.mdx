The GranFondo Prosecco is the Italian gran fondo that catches British and Irish riders off guard — not because the climbing is massive, but because it never stops. The route rolls through the UNESCO-listed Prosecco hills above Valdobbiadene, and from the outside it looks like a pleasant day in wine country. Gentle green hills, vineyards, small roads winding between villages. Then the road kicks up to 14 per cent between two rows of vines, drops you down the other side, and kicks up again before you've found a gear. Do that forty times in thirty-degree heat and the pleasant day in wine country becomes a genuine physical test.

Here is the thing most riders miss about this event. The GranFondo Prosecco is not a climbing sportive. It is a repeated-effort sportive in heat. The climbs are short — most are 1-4km — but they are steep, they come in clusters, and the rolling terrain between them never lets your heart rate settle into a rhythm. If you've trained for a Marmotte or a [Maratona](/blog/maratona-dles-dolomites-training-guide) by stacking long threshold climbs, you have trained the wrong energy system for Valdobbiadene.

This guide covers the 12-week preparation we use with riders inside [Not Done Yet](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) who are targeting the Prosecco and similar Italian punch-climb gran fondos.

## Key Takeaways

- 130-165km with 2,000-2,800m of climbing through short, steep vineyard ramps (8-15 per cent gradients)
- 2.5 W/kg minimum for the medio route, 3.0+ W/kg for a strong lungo, 3.5+ W/kg for competitive riding
- Muro di Ca' del Poggio is the queen climb — 1.1km at 12 per cent average, 19 per cent maximum, a Giro d'Italia stage finish
- Heat in the Veneto regularly hits 32-35 degrees — acclimatisation is a training block, not a race-week thought
- Fuel 60-80g carbs per hour from the start — punch climbs burn glycogen faster than steady-state efforts
- Train 2-4 minute repeated efforts at 110-120 per cent FTP, not long threshold intervals
- Arrive a day early and ride the opening kilometres — the atmosphere is half the reason to enter

## What the GranFondo Prosecco Actually Demands

The route starts and finishes in or near Valdobbiadene, a small town in the Veneto foothills about an hour north of Venice. Route options typically range from a shorter medio of around 130km to a lungo of 160-165km, with the lungo adding an extra loop that pushes the climbing total above 2,800m. The entire course threads through the Prosecco DOCG wine region — the same hills where Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco comes from — and the terrain is defined by the viticulture. Vineyards grow on steep slopes. The roads that connect them follow the same gradients.

The climbing profile is the part that separates the Prosecco from most other Italian gran fondos. There is no single 20km alpine grind. Instead, the route delivers dozens of short climbs, most between 1km and 4km, at gradients between 6 and 15 per cent. The transitions between climbs are rolling — not flat, not recovery — which means your legs never fully clear the lactate from the last effort before the next one starts. By kilometre 100, those 2-minute ramps that felt manageable at kilometre 30 are taking noticeably longer.

The Muro di Ca' del Poggio is the signature ascent. Used as a Giro d'Italia stage finish, it is roughly 1.1km at 12 per cent average with ramps touching 19 per cent. It is short enough that you can ride it on determination alone, but it typically arrives deep enough into the route that determination is more expensive than it was at the start. Riders who attack the bottom find out what 19 per cent feels like when the legs are six hours into the day.

Then there is the heat. The event runs in summer, and the Veneto foothills in July are properly hot. Thirty-two degrees is normal. Thirty-five degrees is common. The vineyards offer intermittent shade at best — most of the climbing is on open, south-facing slopes that radiate heat off the tarmac. If your preparation has been a British spring and your longest warm ride was 22 degrees with a breeze, the thermal load on race day is going to cost you more than any climb on the course.

The defining physical demand is repeatable short-climb power in heat. Not your 20-minute FTP. Not your VO2max. Your ability to ride 2-4 minutes at 110-120 per cent of FTP, recover partially on a rolling descent, and do it again. Forty times.

## The Physical Bar: W/kg and Fitness Targets

Three numbers tell you where you stand.

**FTP in W/kg.** The floor for a comfortable medio finish is 2.5 W/kg. The lungo asks for 3.0 W/kg if you want to ride it rather than survive it. Above 3.5 W/kg with good heat tolerance, you can ride the front groups and enjoy the steep ramps instead of fearing them. Use the [W/kg calculator](/tools/wkg) to get a real number from your last test, and the [FTP zones tool](/tools/ftp-zones) to set your training targets.

**Repeatable 2-4 minute power.** This is the number that actually predicts your day. If your FTP is 250W but your 3-minute power drops from 320W to 270W across six efforts, the Prosecco hills will find that gap by mid-afternoon. Train the repeatability, not just the peak.

**Heat tolerance.** If race conditions will be materially hotter than normal
training, prepare progressively and rehearse pacing, cooling and fluid access. Do
not use a universal closed-window or no-fan prescription. The [heat training
guide](/blog/heat-training-cyclists-30-watts-ftp-protocol) explains how to match
exposure to the event and rider while protecting training quality.

## The 12-Week Framework

Three blocks of four weeks: Base, Build, Specific plus Taper. Volumes assume a starting base of 6-8 hours per week. The Prosecco does not demand the ultra-distance preparation of a Mallorca 312 or a multi-day stage race — the distance is manageable. The specificity of the efforts is what matters.

### Weeks 1-4: Base

Volume: 6-8 hours/week. Four to five rides, 80 per cent in Zone 2.

The aerobic engine underpins everything. Riders who skip this block and start with intervals plateau in week 8 when the specificity work needs a platform to sit on.

**Anchor session:** Long Zone 2 ride building from 3 hours to 4 hours over the block. Include at least 500-800m of climbing per long ride. Find rolling terrain if you can — the Prosecco is never flat, so your base rides should not be either.

**Midweek sessions:** Two 60-90 minute rides at easy pace, one with 4-6 short (90-second) efforts at high cadence to start building the neuromuscular pathways for the punch climbs. These are not intervals. They are primers — short enough that they do not accumulate fatigue, hard enough that your body starts remembering what rapid force production feels like.

**Strength work:** Two sessions per week of lower-body and core stability. Single-leg press, Bulgarian split squats, step-ups with dumbbells, plank variations. No heavy compound barbell lifts — the injury risk for riders in the 35-55 bracket outweighs the training return when cycling-specific strength can be built on the bike and with lighter, controlled movements. Twenty minutes per session is enough.

### Weeks 5-8: Build

Volume: 8-10 hours/week. Two quality sessions appear alongside the endurance work.

**Session one — Repeated short climbs:** 6x3 minutes at 105-115 per cent FTP with 3 minutes easy between efforts, building to 8x3 minutes by week 8. Find a 6-10 per cent gradient for these. If you are on the trainer, stand for the first 30 seconds of each effort — the Prosecco ramps demand out-of-saddle acceleration before settling into a seated grind.

**Session two — Sweet spot tempo:** 2x20 minutes at 88-93 per cent FTP on rolling terrain, building to 2x25 minutes by week 8. This trains the sustained output that fills the gaps between the punch climbs. The Prosecco is not all spikes — there are 5-10km sections of rolling tempo between the vineyard kickers, and the riders who lose their day are the riders who cannot hold tempo on these sections because they overcook every short climb.

**Long ride:** Building to 4.5-5 hours with 1,000-1,500m of climbing. Deliberate practice on short, steep local climbs — if your area has a 1km hill at 8-10 per cent, ride it six times in the middle of the long ride with 5 minutes rolling recovery between. That is the Prosecco in miniature.

**Heat introduction:** From week 6 onwards, begin heat acclimatisation. One indoor session per week with reduced fan or warmer conditions for the first 20-30 minutes. Not the whole session — just enough that the body starts adapting its sweat response. Build gradually.

### Weeks 9-12: Specific + Taper

Volume peaks at 9-11 hours in weeks 9-10, then tapers.

**Weeks 9-10: Specificity.**

The quality sessions tighten to race simulation. The key session is the Prosecco simulation ride:

**Prosecco simulation:** On a rolling route, ride 3-4 hours with eight to ten efforts of 2-4 minutes at 110-120 per cent FTP, spread across the ride rather than clustered at the start. Fuel at race-day rates (60-80g carbs per hour) throughout. This is the session that tells you whether your preparation is where it needs to be. If you can hold effort number eight at 90 per cent of effort number one's power, you are ready. If effort eight is a grind at 75 per cent, there is work to do in the repeatability.

**Over-distance ride:** One ride of 5-5.5 hours with 1,500-2,000m of climbing, ridden at a pace that leaves you tired but functional. This is not an endurance test for an ultra — the Prosecco distance is within most trained riders' range. The purpose is to rehearse the full fuelling plan and confirm that your body handles 5+ hours in warm conditions without falling apart.

**Heat block:** Two to three sessions per week with deliberate heat exposure for the first 30-40 minutes. By week 10, your body should be managing warm conditions without excessive heart rate drift.

**Weeks 11-12: Taper.**

Volume drops 30 per cent in week 11, another 40 per cent in week 12. Keep two short, sharp sessions: 4-5 efforts of 2 minutes at 110-115 per cent FTP with full recovery, and one 20-minute sweet spot effort to maintain the aerobic feel. Everything else is easy spinning. The taper is where most amateurs panic and add sessions. Do not do this. The fitness is already in the legs. The taper lets the body absorb it.

If you build your plan in [TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/), the structured workouts and ATL/CTL tracking make the taper legible — you can see the training stress dropping while the form line rises. For a specificity-driven event like the Prosecco, that visibility matters.

## Nutrition Strategy

The punch-climb profile of the Prosecco burns glycogen faster than a steady-state ride of the same distance. Every 2-4 minute effort above threshold dips into the anaerobic system, and each one costs more carbohydrate per minute than riding tempo on a long alpine climb. If you fuel the Prosecco like a steady-state sportive, you will bonk.

**On the bike.** 60-80g of carbohydrate per hour from the first 30 minutes. That is two gels per hour, or a 60g/hour drink mix plus one gel every 45 minutes, or a combination of bars and gels that hits the target. The [carbs-per-hour guide](/blog/carbohydrate-per-hour-cyclists) covers the gut-training and absorption detail. Train your gut to this rate on at least three long rides before race day. The first time you eat 70g in an hour should not be on the start line.

**Aid stations.** Italian gran fondo feed zones are a different experience from northern European sportives. Expect real food — panini, fruit, biscuits, sometimes a slice of cake — alongside gels and water. Eat real food at the early aid stations when the gut is fresh, switch to gels and liquid carbs as the day heats up and digestion slows. Do not sit down. Refill, eat, leave. Five minutes at an aid station is discipline. Fifteen minutes is a holiday.

**Hydration.** 500-750ml per hour is the baseline, rising to 750ml-1 litre per hour in the hot afternoon sections. Sodium is critical — sweat rate at 33 degrees is significantly higher than most riders train at, and plain water without electrolytes dilutes sodium levels rather than maintaining them. Salt tabs in the bottles, electrolyte mix in your drink, and if the aid stations offer broth or salted food, take it. The [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) helps you set race-day targets based on your weight and expected duration.

**Pre-race.** Carb-load 24-36 hours before: 8-10g per kilogram of body weight across the day before the event. A 75kg rider eats 600-750g of carbohydrate in 24 hours. You are in the Veneto — the local pasta will handle this effortlessly. Do not experiment with unfamiliar food. Eat what you know, drink water, sleep early.

**Race morning.** A familiar breakfast 3 hours before the start — porridge, toast, coffee, whatever you've rehearsed. A final gel or banana 15 minutes before the gun. Then eat from the first 30 minutes on the bike. Riders who wait until they feel hungry are already behind.

## Common Mistakes

**Treating it like a flat sportive because the climbs are short.** The cumulative elevation across dozens of short, steep ramps is really demanding. Riders who look at the 2,000-2,800m total and think "that's less than a Marmotte" are missing the profile shape. Forty climbs of 50-70m at 10-15 per cent are harder in practice than three climbs of 700m at 6-7 per cent because you never settle, never find a rhythm, and never fully recover between efforts.

**Training long threshold intervals instead of short repeats.** A 3x15 minutes at threshold session is excellent preparation for a Stelvio or an Etape. It is mediocre preparation for the Prosecco. The event demands 2-4 minute repeatable power, not 15-minute sustained power. Both matter, but the specificity must match the terrain.

**Ignoring heat preparation.** UK and Irish riders arrive in the Veneto having trained through spring at 12-18 degrees. Race day is 32 degrees. That gap is not trivial. Heart rate rises 8-10 beats per minute in heat, perceived exertion climbs, sweat rate doubles, and the cognitive load of pacing and fuelling in heat is measurably higher. Four to six weeks of gradual heat acclimatisation is the single highest-value addition most riders can make to their preparation.

**Overcooking the early climbs.** The first vineyard kickers feel short and easy. Fresh legs, cool morning, group energy. Riders attack them at 130-140 per cent FTP because they can. Then the legs are already loaded by kilometre 60, and the second half of the course — which has the same climbs — feels like a different event. Pace the first half at 90-95 per cent of what you know you can do. Leave the heroics for the Muro di Ca' del Poggio.

**Not eating enough because the ride is "only" 130-165km.** The distance is moderate. The caloric demand is not. Repeated above-threshold efforts burn through glycogen at a rate disproportionate to the total kilometres. A 5-hour ride with forty punch climbs can empty stores faster than a 7-hour ride at steady tempo. Start fuelling from the gun.

**Skipping the Muro di Ca' del Poggio reconnaissance.** If you arrive a day early and have legs for a shakeout, ride the Muro. The gradient ramps and road surface are specific enough that one rehearsal is worth twenty minutes of Strava analysis. Know where the steep section is, know where it eases, and know where to sit and where to stand. On event day, that familiarity is a genuine advantage.

## Kit, Gearing, and Logistics

**Bike.** A road bike with reliable braking — disc brakes if you have them, but good rim brakes are fine. The descents are short and technical through narrow vineyard roads, not long alpine drops, so braking demands are frequent rather than sustained. Tubeless tyres or quality clinchers with a repair kit. Italian road surfaces vary — the main roads are clean but the vineyard lanes can be rough.

**Gearing.** Compact chainset (50/34) with a 30-tooth or 32-tooth cassette is the sensible choice. The 15-19 per cent ramps on the steepest climbs, including the Muro, are not the place to discover your gearing is too tall. Test your lowest gear on a local hill of comparable gradient at the end of a 4-hour ride, not on fresh legs after a warm-up. A 34x32 that feels easy at hour one feels very different at hour five.

**Clothing.** Light summer kit — a single jersey layer, quality bib shorts, and a mesh base layer that wicks in heat. Arm warmers are unnecessary unless the start is unusually early and cool. Sun sleeves are worth considering for the open vineyard sections — the Veneto sun at midday burns through SPF in under two hours on exposed arms. Reapply sunscreen at every aid station. A cap under the helmet, wet at every water stop, is the cheapest and most effective cooling tool available.

**Hydration carry.** Two bottles minimum. Three if your frame or jersey pockets allow it. The distances between aid stations are manageable, but in 33-degree heat you can drain two bottles in 90 minutes. Running out of fluid on a vineyard climb with no shade is the fastest way to turn a good day into a crisis.

**Travel.** Venice Marco Polo or Treviso airports are the closest — Valdobbiadene is roughly an hour's drive from either. Car hire is the simplest logistics; trains reach Conegliano, from which it is a short taxi or local bus to Valdobbiadene. Book accommodation early — the town is small and event weekend fills up. Agriturismi in the surrounding hills are the best option if you want the full experience and don't mind a 10-minute drive to the start.

**Bike transport.** If you're flying, a bike bag and the usual airline boxing applies. If you're driving from the UK, the Eurotunnel and autoroute south through France into Italy is a long but straightforward drive that avoids the airport bike-damage lottery. Many riders combine the Prosecco with a few days' riding in the Dolomites to the north — the mountains are an hour away and the training terrain is world-class.

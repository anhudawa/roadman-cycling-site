Velothon Wales is one of those events that looks straightforward on paper and then quietly humbles you. 140 kilometres on closed roads. Cardiff to the Brecon Beacons and back through the South Wales Valleys. Around 1,800 metres of climbing across rolling terrain that never lets you settle. And then Caerphilly Mountain at km 125 — 2.5km at 10% average — when your legs have already absorbed five hours of attrition.

The closed-road format is the draw. No traffic, no roundabouts, no nervous moments with HGVs on single-track Welsh lanes. Thousands of riders on roads that feel like a race. And that is exactly where the trap sits. Closed roads and group riding create a pace that flatters everyone for the first 40 kilometres and then exposes anyone who took the bait.

Here is how to train for Velothon Wales across 12 weeks, with the right sessions, the right pacing, and the weather preparation that turns a Welsh autumn day from a problem into a manageable variable.

## Key Takeaways

- Velothon Wales is 140km on closed roads through the Brecon Beacons and South Wales Valleys — the terrain is relentlessly rolling rather than Alpine, and the closed-road format invites pace errors from the gun
- Caerphilly Mountain at km 125 is the sting — 2.5km at 10% average after 120km of attrition. Riders who overpace the first 80km walk this climb
- Welsh weather is the second course: rain, crosswinds, and temperatures that swing 8 degrees between the valley floor and the Beacons tops are normal, not unlucky
- Target 2.8 W/kg minimum for a comfortable finish inside 5.5 hours; 3.5+ W/kg to ride Caerphilly at threshold and finish under 4.5 hours
- Group riding on closed roads creates a false sense of pacing — riding in the wheels at 38km/h for the first hour burns more than it saves if you are a 3.0 W/kg rider
- A 12-week build with two quality sessions per week and a progressive long ride is enough if your base is 6-8 hours a week
- Kit choice matters: arm warmers in the pocket, gilet on from the start, and overshoes if the forecast shows morning rain. Getting cold in the Valleys at km 90 costs more watts than any climb

## What Velothon Wales Actually Demands

The numbers first. 140 kilometres. Approximately 1,800 metres of total climbing. No single climb longer than 5km until the finale. Closed roads the entire way. Finish times from 4 hours for the strong to 6.5 hours for the steady. Average finish sits around 5-5.5 hours for a well-prepared rider at 2.8-3.2 W/kg.

The course leaves Cardiff heading north-west through the Vale of Glamorgan and into the foothills of the Brecon Beacons. The first 40km are rolling — nothing steep, nothing sustained, just constant undulation that masks the effort. The road then rises into the Beacons proper, exposed ridgelines and open moorland where the weather arrives sideways. From there, the route drops into the South Wales Valleys — Rhondda, Cynon, Taff — on roads that dip and climb through old mining towns and tight valley walls. The final 20km brings you south towards Cardiff with the sting: Caerphilly Mountain, a proper climb at the end of a long day.

Typical conditions sit between 10 and 18 degrees. Rain probability in a Welsh autumn is honest — call it 50/50 on any given year, and that is being generous. Crosswinds on the exposed Beacons sections are the norm, not the exception. The temperature gap between the valley floor and the Beacons tops can swing 6-8 degrees in the space of a 30-minute climb. You dress for the tops, not the valleys.

The closed-road dynamic shapes the ride more than most riders anticipate. Drafting groups form within the first 10 kilometres. Experienced riders slot in and save 20-30% of their output. Less experienced riders sit in a group travelling at 36-38km/h, feel comfortable because they are sheltered, and don't realise they are burning 20-30 watts more than their sustainable pace. By km 60, the groups begin to fracture. By km 100, the riders who sat in too-fast groups are riding solo.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about closed-road sportives: the group does not set your pace. Your power meter does.

## Course Analysis: Where the Event Is Won and Lost

### First 40km: Cardiff to the Foothills

Flat to gently rolling. The road is wide, the groups are large, the pace feels social. This is where discipline matters most.

What most people do: ride in a fast group because it feels easy, chat with the riders around them, ignore their power numbers, and average 32-35km/h when their sustainable race pace is 28-30km/h.

What actually works: find a group travelling at your sustainable pace — not the fastest group you can hold. Watch your normalised power. If it is above 70% of your FTP in the first hour, you are too hot. The first 40km should feel boring. That is correct.

### 40-80km: The Brecon Beacons

The climbing starts. Not Alpine-steep, but persistent — 1-3km ramps at 5-8% that arrive every few kilometres. The exposed ridgeline sections bring crosswinds. Groups thin.

The key here is rhythm. Every short climb tempts you to surge. Every descent tempts you to soft-pedal. Neither is efficient. Hold your effort steady — sit, spin a comfortable cadence, and resist the urge to attack each rise. The riders who surge 25 times across 40km of rolling terrain pay for every one of them on Caerphilly Mountain.

The wind on the exposed sections is the hidden tax. A headwind on the Beacons ridgeline at km 55 is worth 30 extra watts for the same speed. Sit in. Find wheels. Don't grind solo into a Welsh crosswind unless you have watts to spare.

### 80-120km: The Valleys

The road descends into the Valleys and the terrain changes character. Less exposed, more enclosed, but the climbs keep coming — short, punchy Valley roads that pitch up to 10-12% for 500 metres at a time. Twenty-five to thirty of these across 40km.

This is where fuelling becomes critical. Five hours in, the riders who started eating at km 20 feel strong. The riders who waited until they were hungry are now managing a deficit they cannot close. Eat through the Valleys. Every 20 minutes. Whether you feel like it or not.

The other factor is cold. Valley roads are shaded. If it has been raining, the road surface stays wet, the air temperature drops 3-4 degrees compared to the ridgeline, and the chill sets in. This is where the gilet earns its place. Cold muscles produce less power. Getting cold at km 90 is more expensive than any climb on the course.

### 120-140km: Caerphilly Mountain and the Finish

Caerphilly Mountain arrives at km 125. Two and a half kilometres at 10% average gradient with ramps touching 14-16% in the steepest sections. After 120km of rolling attrition, this climb is the honest reckoning.

Let me break this down. A 75kg rider at 3.0 W/kg produces 225 watts. On a 10% gradient, that is roughly 10km/h. The climb takes 15 minutes. At 2.5 W/kg, the same rider produces 187 watts. That is 8km/h. The climb takes 19 minutes. On the steepest ramps at 16%, a 2.5 W/kg rider is grinding at 5-6km/h. One bad ramp and you are walking.

The riders who ride Caerphilly Mountain have two things in common: they paced the first 80km conservatively, and they ate consistently through the Valleys. The riders who walk it have one thing in common: they went too hard, too early.

After the summit, the road drops into Cardiff. Six to eight kilometres of mostly downhill to the finish. If you have ridden Caerphilly cleanly, the run-in is a celebration. If you walked it, it is a cold, demoralising limp.

## 12-Week Training Framework

This framework assumes a starting base of 6-8 hours per week with some hills in your regular riding. If you are below that, extend to 16 weeks and build the base block to 6 weeks.

### Weeks 1-4: Base Consolidation

**Volume:** 7-9 hours/week across 4-5 rides.

Eighty per cent of your riding in [Zone 2](/blog/zone-2-cycling-heart-rate-vs-power-vs-rpe). The aerobic engine. Stephen Seiler's polarised principle is the framework — easy stays easy. The temptation is to ride every session at a moderate grind. Resist it.

**Anchor session:** Long ride building from 2.5 hours to 4 hours over the block, on rolling terrain. Not flat. Find hills. The Velothon has no flat sections longer than 5km — your long rides should reflect that.

**Midweek quality:** One tempo session — 2x20 minutes at 76-82% FTP with 5 minutes recovery between efforts. Steady, conversational-pace effort that builds muscular endurance without digging into threshold reserves.

**Strength:** Two gym sessions per week. Bulgarian split lunges, step-ups, leg press, single-leg hip thrusts, and core stability work. Twenty to thirty minutes. Nothing dramatic. Build the chassis that holds the engine.

### Weeks 5-8: Build Phase

**Volume:** 9-11 hours/week.

Two quality sessions per week now. The training starts to get specific.

**Session one — threshold repeats:** 3x10 minutes at 95-100% FTP building to 4x10 minutes by week 8. Three to four minutes recovery between efforts. These build the sustained power you need for the Beacons climbs and the Caerphilly finish.

**Session two — over-under intervals:** 3x12 minutes alternating between 1 minute at 105% FTP and 2 minutes at 88% FTP. This mimics the rolling terrain of the Valleys — constant fluctuation in effort around threshold, never quite recovering, never quite blowing up.

**Long ride:** Building from 4 hours to 5 hours on rolling terrain with 1,200-1,500m of climbing. Include 3-4 climbs of 5-10 minutes at steady aerobic effort. The long ride is not a hard session. Ride it at 65-72% of FTP.

**Gym:** Reduce to once per week. Maintain the movements, reduce the volume. The bike work is now doing the heavy lifting.

### Weeks 9-11: Peak and Race Simulation

**Volume:** 10-12 hours/week. This is the highest the volume goes.

**Session one — sustained threshold:** 3x15 minutes at 95-100% FTP. Five minutes recovery. If you can complete three clean 15-minute efforts at threshold by week 10, you are ready for Caerphilly Mountain.

**Session two — Caerphilly simulation:** This is the key session. Do your long ride (4.5-5 hours of steady riding with 1,200m of climbing), and then at the end — when you are tired, when you want to stop — ride 3x5 minutes at 105-110% FTP with 3 minutes recovery. Simulate the finishing climb on fatigued legs. If you can hold 105% of FTP for 5 minutes after 4.5 hours of riding, Caerphilly Mountain will not break you.

**Race simulation ride (week 10 or 11):** One ride of 120-140km on the most rolling terrain you can find. Full fuelling rehearsal. Kit rehearsal. Pace to your race-day power target. Treat it as a dress rehearsal for everything — nutrition timing, clothing, hydration, effort management. This ride tells you if you are ready.

**Rest weeks:** Take an easier week at the end of week 10 — drop volume by 30%, keep one short threshold session, ride easy the rest of the time. You cannot peak without recovery.

### Week 12: Taper

Volume drops 40-50%. Three to four rides across the week, each shorter than usual. Two of those rides include 3x3 minutes at threshold — short, sharp openers that keep the neuromuscular system primed without accumulating fatigue.

The fitness is in. You cannot gain meaningful fitness in the final week. You can lose the race by training too hard. Monday and Tuesday ride easy or rest. Wednesday do the openers. Thursday rest. Friday spin 30-45 minutes. Saturday ride or rest depending on the event day.

## Key Sessions That Simulate Velothon's Demands

### 1. Rolling Terrain Repeats

Find a circuit with 3-5 short climbs (1-2km each at 5-8%). Ride 4-6 laps at a steady effort — 75-85% FTP on the climbs, 60-65% FTP on the descents and flats. Do not surge on the climbs. Do not coast on the descents. Hold the rhythm. This is what Velothon feels like from km 40 to km 120.

**Duration:** 90 minutes to 2 hours in the build phase.

### 2. Over-Under Intervals

3-4 sets of 12 minutes: alternate 1 minute at 105% FTP and 2 minutes at 88% FTP. Three minutes recovery between sets. This trains the ability to absorb repeated surges around threshold — the exact demand of rolling Valley roads where the gradient never settles.

### 3. Caerphilly Simulation

At the end of a 4-5 hour long ride, ride 3x5 minutes at 105-110% FTP with 3 minutes easy spinning between efforts. This is specific preparation for riding a steep climb on tired legs. The first effort will feel hard. The second will feel worse. The third will feel like Caerphilly Mountain. That is the point.

### 4. Closed-Road Pace Discipline Session

Ride with a group for 90 minutes. Set a hard ceiling on your normalised power at 68% of FTP. Do not go above it, regardless of what the group does. If the group accelerates, let them go. If the pace drops, hold your ceiling. This trains the self-discipline that the first 40km of Velothon demands.

## Nutrition and Fuelling Strategy

A 140km rolling sportive at race effort burns 3,000-4,500 kilojoules depending on your weight and intensity. You cannot start replacing that debt on the road — you have to start full and stay ahead.

**Pre-race evening:** A carbohydrate-rich dinner 12-14 hours before the start. Rice, pasta, potatoes — familiar food, nothing experimental. 8-10g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight across the final 24 hours. For a 75kg rider, that is 600-750g of carbs across the day before the event.

**Race morning:** Breakfast 3 hours before the start. Porridge, toast with jam, a banana, coffee. 100-150g of carbohydrate. Nothing new. If you have not eaten it before a long ride in training, do not eat it on race morning.

**On the bike — the first hour:** Start eating within 20 minutes of the gun. A gel or a few bites of a bar. Sixty to eighty grams of carbohydrate per hour from the start. Not from hour two. From the start. By the time you feel hungry, you are already behind.

**On the bike — hours 2-5:** Maintain 60-80g of carbs per hour. Alternate between gels, bars, and whatever the feed stations provide. Set a timer on your head unit if you need to — eat every 20 minutes regardless of appetite. Welsh conditions suppress hunger. Cold and rain make you forget to eat. The timer does not forget.

**Hydration:** 500-750ml per hour even in cool weather. Cold conditions suppress thirst but the body still sweats under effort. Electrolyte mix in every bottle. Plain water is not enough across five hours.

**Caffeine:** A caffeinated gel at km 80-90, thirty minutes before the Valleys section ends and Caerphilly Mountain begins. The caffeine takes 20-30 minutes to peak. Time it for the climb, not for the Valley roads.

Use the [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) to plan your exact intake based on weight and target duration.

## Kit and Equipment Considerations

Welsh weather is the kit decider. Not the terrain. Not the distance. The weather.

**Clothing baseline:** Jersey, bib shorts, arm warmers in the back pocket, gilet on from the start. The gilet comes off when the sun comes out and goes back on the moment you hit a descent. If the forecast shows temperatures below 14 degrees at the start, leg warmers are worth considering for the first two hours.

**Rain preparation:** Overshoes if the morning forecast shows rain. A lightweight rain cape or jacket in the back pocket if the forecast is really threatening. Getting soaked at km 40 and then descending into a shaded Valley at km 80 drops your core temperature fast. Cold muscles produce less power. Cold hands brake worse. Cold riders make poor decisions on descents.

**Tyres:** 28mm minimum. On wet Welsh roads, grip matters more than aero. If you are running clinchers, check the wear. If you are running tubeless, check the sealant. A puncture on a wet Welsh hillside in October is a miserable 20-minute stop that costs you more in body heat than in time.

**Gearing:** Compact chainset — 50/34 — with an 11-32 or 11-34 cassette. Caerphilly Mountain at 10% average on tired legs is not the place to discover you are undergeared. If your smallest gear is a 28-tooth, reconsider. Test your gearing by riding a 10% local climb after a 4-hour ride. If you are grinding below 60rpm, you need a bigger cassette.

**Bike fit:** Check your position before the 12-week block, not the week before the event. A position that works for 3-hour rides can cause knee or back issues at 5 hours. If you have not ridden 4+ hours in your current position, get a fit check. The [bike fit guide](/blog/bike-fit-guide-cyclists) covers the basics.

## Race Week and Race Day Logistics

**Travel to Cardiff:** Arrive the day before. Registration, bike check, course briefing. Cardiff is well connected by road and rail. If you are driving, park early — event parking fills. If you are coming from London, the M4 is 3 hours in good traffic. From Bristol, 45 minutes.

**Pre-race day:** Registration, short spin of 30-45 minutes with a few 30-second efforts to keep the legs sharp. Lay out all kit the night before. Two options: a warm-weather set and a cold-weather set. Check the forecast at 9pm and again at 6am. Pick the kit to match.

**Race morning:** Wake 3 hours before your start time. Breakfast immediately. Coffee. Kit on. Arrive at the start line 30-45 minutes early. Warm up on the turbo for 10-15 minutes if conditions allow — or ride easy for 15-20 minutes before lining up. Cold muscles on the start line lead to early cramping and poor first-hour pacing.

**Pacing plan:** Set a power ceiling for the first 40km at 65-70% of FTP. Ignore the groups flying past. From km 40 to km 120, ride at 72-78% of FTP on the climbs, 60-65% on the descents and flats. Save 5% for Caerphilly Mountain — you want to arrive at km 125 feeling strong, not surviving.

**Head unit setup:** Display normalised power, elapsed time, and heart rate. Set a lap marker at km 40, km 80, km 120, and km 125. Each segment has a different effort target. The lap markers keep you honest.

## Common Mistakes Riders Make at Velothon Wales

**Going out too fast in the group.** This is the most common error and it costs more riders their finish than any climb on the course. Closed roads create a group dynamic that pulls everyone 10-15% above their sustainable pace. A 3.0 W/kg rider sitting in a group travelling at 38km/h on the flat is working at 82-85% of FTP — too hot for a 5-hour effort. Ride your own pace. Let the fast groups go.

**Underdressing for Welsh weather.** The rider who starts in a jersey and shorts because the morning looks dry and then gets rained on at km 50 loses more performance to cold than they saved in weight. A gilet weighs 90 grams. Arm warmers weigh 60 grams. The performance cost of carrying them is zero. The performance cost of not having them is enormous.

**Not eating enough through the Valleys.** The Valley roads between km 80 and km 120 are where riders stop eating. The terrain is enclosed, the effort feels manageable, the appetite drops in cooler air. And then Caerphilly Mountain arrives and the legs are empty. Eat through the Valleys. Set the timer. Every 20 minutes.

**Not saving legs for Caerphilly Mountain.** Caerphilly is not the hardest climb you will ever ride. On fresh legs, it is a solid 15-minute effort. But it does not arrive on fresh legs. It arrives after 120km of rolling terrain, and the riders who surged on every short climb in the Valleys arrive at the foot of Caerphilly with a muscular debt they cannot service. Ride the Valleys steady. Save the legs. Ride Caerphilly with intent.

**Treating closed roads as a race from km 1.** Velothon is a sportive. There are no prizes for first across the line. The closed roads are there so you can enjoy the riding, not so you can race your mates into the ground before the Beacons. The riders who treat the first hour as a race are the riders walking on Caerphilly Mountain. Every time.

**Ignoring the crosswinds on the Beacons.** The exposed ridgeline sections of the Brecon Beacons carry crosswinds that add 20-40 watts to your effort for the same speed. Riders who grind solo into the wind burn through their reserves faster than riders who sit in, find shelter, and save their legs for the climbs. If you have never ridden in echelons, learn before race day.

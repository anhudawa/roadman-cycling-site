Every July we get three weeks of the same magic trick. A rider empties himself over five hours of racing, crosses the line grey, and turns up tomorrow morning to do it again — 21 times, with the best of them getting stronger into week three. You and I do one ride like that and lose the rest of the weekend to the sofa.

The trick isn't genetics alone, and it isn't anything darker. It's a checklist — one that nearly every WorldTour rider runs after every stage, because the science underneath it is solid. I went through it piece by piece on [this week's episode](/podcast/pogacar-tour-de-france-recovery-routine), and the honest headline is this: half of the pro checklist will improve your riding, and half of it can quietly work against everything your training is trying to do.

The difference comes down to one question the recovery industry never asks you.

## The Question That Sorts Everything

A Tour rider's next stage is 18 hours away. Everything about his recovery is tuned to a single goal: *perform again tomorrow*. Not get fitter — survive, and be fast, tomorrow. Mid-Tour, nobody is trying to build adaptations. They're managing a deficit until Paris.

Your situation, most weeks of the year, is the mirror image. Your next hard ride is Tuesday or Wednesday. Nothing about your weekend needs rescuing by Monday morning. What you want from training is the slow compounding of adaptation — and adaptation is triggered, in meaningful part, by the very stress and inflammation the pro checklist is built to suppress.

Hold onto that split — *performing tomorrow* versus *adapting over months* — and the whole checklist sorts itself.

## Copy: The Carbs, Immediately (When It Matters)

Watch a stage finish and you'll see it: riders eating before they've reached the bus, never mind the shower. That's the least glamorous and most transferable habit on the list. Stage racing drains muscle glycogen close to empty daily, and refilling it fast is the single biggest lever for having legs tomorrow.

The number comes from Dr Sam Impey — co-author of *Fuel for the Work Required*, and a recent [guest on the podcast](/podcast/sam-impey-most-cyclists-fuel-wrong): after a hard or long ride, roughly **1 to 1.5 grams of carbohydrate per kilo of bodyweight in the first hour**. A 75 kg rider is looking at 75-110 g — a proper bowl of pasta or rice, a recovery drink, a banana. (Don't confuse this with on-the-bike fuelling, where Impey has amateurs starting nearer 60 g per hour and building tolerance — that's a different number for a different job.)

Here's the context the supplement industry strips off: the urgency only exists when the next hard effort is close. Back-to-back weekend blocks, training camps, multi-day events — hit the window, every time, and you'll feel the difference on day two. Ordinary training week, next quality session days away? Your normal meals refill the tank with room to spare. The window is real; it just isn't always open.

## Skip (Mostly): Cherry Juice And The Ice Bath

The tart cherry shot at the finish line has become pro recovery's most recognisable image, and the evidence behind it is real — strongest exactly where the pros use it, in repeated back-to-back efforts. Anthocyanins take the edge off muscle damage and inflammation, so you feel less trashed tomorrow.

But inflammation isn't purely the enemy. It's part of the signal that tells your body to adapt — the entire point of the session you just rode. Blunt it every day and you may be sanding down the response you trained to create. That's why the sensible split is: stage races, big event weekends, race weeks — use it. Normal training blocks — skip it. The [tart cherry guide](/blog/cycling-tart-cherry-juice-recovery-guide) has doses and timing for the weeks it earns its place.

The cold plunge is the same trade-off with the volume turned up. For next-day freshness in congested racing — plus core-temperature management in heat — the evidence is decent. But a growing stack of research shows *routine* post-training cold-water immersion blunts long-term adaptation, most clearly strength and muscle gains, with some endurance signalling too. As I put it on the episode: an ice bath after a Tuesday training session is a workout followed by the undo button. Our [cold water immersion deep dive](/blog/cold-water-immersion-cyclists) goes through the studies; the one-line version is *race weeks yes, build phases no*.

## Enjoy, Don't Rely On: The Massage

The nightly soigneur session might be the most iconic image in cycling recovery, so this lands strangely: the flushing-toxins story is a myth. Lactate is long gone from your legs within hours of finishing — it isn't pooled there waiting to be squeezed out — and the measured performance-recovery benefits of massage are small.

What massage reliably does is make you *feel* better — less sore, less stiff — and force half an hour of parasympathetic stillness with a bit of human care attached. Twelve stages into a Grand Tour, that's worth a lot. For you, it's a pleasant extra, never the foundation. The moment massage competes with sleep — for your hour or your money — sleep wins. It's the most powerful recovery tool in the sport and it costs nothing.

## Copy: The Periodised Dinner (And The Bedtime Protein)

The piece that outweighs the juice, the massage and the plunge combined is the one nobody photographs: dinner. When Chris Harper of Q36.5 [walked me through a rider's post-stage day](/podcast/ep-2538-this-is-exactly-how-pro-riders-fuel-a-stage-in-the-giro-with), the day ran to nearly 7,000 calories — dinner alone around 200 g of carbohydrate and 50-odd of protein, on top of the shake and the bus meal.

Numbers like that terrify amateurs, because we've absorbed the idea that eating big at night makes you fat. The principle that dissolves the fear comes from performance nutritionist Dr David Dunne: **periodise the evening meal**. Dinner scales to load — big day done or big day coming, dinner is big; rest days both sides, it shrinks. Think in 72-hour windows rather than calendar days. A pro's dinner is huge because his load is huge, not because he's lost control.

The classic amateur pattern is the exact inverse: restrained all day, under-fuelled by evening, then a willpower collapse at 9pm — followed by guilt about the meal that was, in fact, the correct size. Plan the big dinner and the panic version never happens.

And the highest-return add-on in all of this: 30-40 g of slow-digesting protein about half an hour before bed — casein shake, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese. [Dr Mike Ormsbee's research](/blog/michael-ormsbee-protein-before-bed-cyclists), two decades of it, shows it keeps muscle protein synthesis running overnight and doesn't add fat even on top of normal daily intake. If you train in the evening like most working cyclists, it's close to free gains. Full protocol in [the bedtime protein guide](/blog/bedtime-protein-cyclists-recovery-protocol).

## What About The Gadgets?

A quick word on the rest of the recovery aisle, because the pro bus is full of it — compression boots, massage guns, wearables scoring your sleep out of a hundred.

The honest reading of the evidence puts nearly all of it in the massage category: small or unclear measured benefits, real comfort value, no harm. Compression boots feel great and force you to sit still for half an hour, which may be most of what they do. Massage guns can take the edge off perceived soreness before a session. None of it belongs ahead of the basics in the queue for your money — and every one of these purchases is quietly competing with the two tools that dominate the whole conversation: food and sleep.

There's a reason teams now employ sleep coaches and travel with riders' own mattresses and pillows while the cherry juice gets the camera time. The people who run recovery for a living put sleep at the top, every time. If your budget for recovery this year is a few hundred dollars, spend it on blackout blinds and a better pillow before anything with a compressor in it. ## The Weekly Template

Pulling it together for a rider with a job:

On normal training days, recovery is boring on purpose: proper meals across the day, a dinner matched to the work, protein before bed, and as much sleep as life allows. No cherry juice, no ice, no window anxiety. Let the inflammation do its job — soreness is the signal.

On back-to-back days — the big Saturday-Sunday block, the training camp, day one of a stage race — flip to pro mode: carbs in the first hour at 1-1.5 g per kilo, a deliberately big dinner, bedtime protein, and if you've got access, this is the week the cherry juice and the cold water finally earn their keep.

Take a concrete example: the classic big weekend. Saturday is four hard hours. Within the hour of getting home: 80-100 g of carbs — pasta, recovery drink, banana — before the shower if you can manage it, exactly like the pros walk to the bus eating. Dinner is deliberately big and planned in advance, so the 9pm cupboard raid never happens. Casein shake at 10. Sunday you'll have legs you didn't earn — that's the window doing its work. Then Monday, back in adaptation mode: normal meals, no ice, no juice, early night.

And in every week of either kind, sleep is the non-negotiable that beats everything you can buy. The pros' real secret was never the tub or the shot. It's knowing which tool, in which situation, at what time. Now you do too.

The 5,700 metres of climbing on the longest Ardéchoise route is not the hard part. The hard part is that the climbing never, ever stops. There is no respite. No long valley road where you spin easy for an hour and reset. You crest a col, descend for three minutes on a narrow road through chestnut trees, round a corner, and there is another one. And another one after that. And another one after that. For 260 kilometres.

La Marmotte gives you the Galibier. The Etape gives you a stage of the Tour. The Ardéchoise gives you the Ardèche — volcanic hills, limestone gorges, roads so narrow that two cars cannot pass, and a landscape that belongs on a postcard from a France that does not exist any more except, somehow, here. Fifteen thousand riders line up in Saint-Félicien every June. Most of them are French. Most of them have done it before. That tells you something. The people who know what this event is keep coming back.

What makes the Ardéchoise different from every other sportive in this category is the combination of cumulative climbing, genuine heat, ultra-distance, and an atmosphere that makes you want to stop at every village and eat cheese for twenty minutes. The preparation framework below is what we work through with riders in [Not Done Yet](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) who target long-distance French sportives — built around the specific demands of 10-14 hours in the saddle across dozens of climbs in 30-degree heat.

- The longest Ardéchoise route covers approximately 260km with 5,700m of climbing — but no single climb exceeds 10km, making the challenge cumulative rather than singular.
- Mid-June Ardèche temperatures regularly hit 30-35 degrees, making heat acclimatisation and hydration strategy as important as climbing fitness.
- Target 2.8 W/kg for a comfortable finish on the 260km route, 3.2+ W/kg for a strong ride — the relentless climbing rewards sustained aerobic power over raw W/kg.

- L'Ardéchoise draws 15,000 riders annually, making it France's largest cyclosportive — the atmosphere, the villages, and the landscapes are as much the point as the riding.

## Course Profile and Key Climbs

The Ardéchoise starts and finishes in Saint-Félicien, a small town in the northern Ardèche. The routes loop through the department in various configurations, with the longest route — the one this guide focuses on — tracing a figure-of-eight through the gorges, the volcanic plateau, and the foothills of the Cévennes.

The climbing profile is distinctive. Where Alpine events build towards signature climbs of 15-20km, the Ardéchoise stacks short, steep efforts end to end. A typical 20km section involves a 4km climb at 6-7 percent, a 2km descent, a 3km climb at 5-6 percent, a brief flat, and another 5km climb at 7-8 percent. The gradient is rarely extreme — you will not see 12 percent ramps for sustained periods — but the recovery between efforts is measured in minutes, not kilometres.

### The Gorges Section

The route drops into the Ardèche gorges in the middle third of the day. Limestone cliffs, the river below, and a road that twists and climbs out of the gorge in a series of switchbacks. The climbing out of the gorges is the steepest section of the route — short ramps of 9-10 percent that punch the legs after 150km. The descents through the gorges are technical. Narrow roads, gravel on the corners, and limited visibility through the twists. This is not the place to set a Strava descent PR.

### Col de Mézilhac

Approximately 8km at 5.5 percent average. The longest sustained climb on the 260km route and the one that most closely resembles an Alpine col. Mézilhac sits on the volcanic plateau and the landscape shifts here — from gorges and chestnut forests to open moorland and granite. The wind can be exposed on the upper slopes. Pace it at 70-80 percent FTP. It is not the hardest climb on the route, but it arrives after 180km and the temptation to push too hard on a recognisable gradient is real. Use the [climb time calculator](/tools/climb-time) to estimate your split before the event.

### Col de la Croix de Bauzon

A genuine mountain pass at over 1,300m, linking the Ardèche valley to the higher ground of the Cévennes. The approach is roughly 10km at 5 percent average, but the gradient kicks up to 7-8 percent in the final 3km. The altitude is enough to notice if you have been riding in lowland heat all day — the air thins perceptibly and the temperature drops. This is often the hardest climb of the event by virtue of where it falls in the route. Your legs have been working for 8-10 hours. Your glycogen is depleted. The climb itself would be moderate on fresh legs. On tired legs, it becomes the exam.

### The Unnamed Cols

This is where the Ardéchoise earns its reputation. Between the major climbs, there are dozens of unnamed côtes and cols — 1-3km at 5-8 percent, cresting without fanfare and descending into the next valley. These are not on any list. They are not dramatic. They are relentless. By kilometre 200, each one costs more than the last because there is no recovery between them. The riders who finish the 260km route strongly are the riders who pace these unnamed climbs at or below threshold every single time, saving the deeper efforts for the sections that require them.

## Fitness Benchmarks

The Ardéchoise rewards a specific kind of fitness: sustainable aerobic power over very long durations with the ability to repeat short climbing efforts without accumulating excessive fatigue. Your [FTP zones](/tools/ftp-zones) are the starting point, but the event-specific demand is your ability to ride at 65-75 percent of FTP for 10-14 hours while pushing to 80-90 percent on each climb.

| Capacity | Strong Finish (260km) | Comfortable Finish (260km) | Completion (260km) |
|----------|----------------------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| FTP | 3.2+ W/kg | 2.8-3.2 W/kg | 2.3-2.8 W/kg |
| 5-minute power | 4.0+ W/kg | 3.5-4.0 W/kg | 3.0-3.5 W/kg |
| Longest training ride | 7+ hours | 6-7 hours | 5-6 hours |
| Weekly volume (peak) | 12-14 hours | 10-12 hours | 8-10 hours |

For the shorter routes, scale these numbers down. The 170km route requires roughly 2.3-2.5 W/kg for a comfortable finish. The 220km route sits between the 170km and 260km benchmarks. Choose your route based on where your fitness sits eight weeks out, not where you hope it will be on event day.

## 16-Week Training Plan Overview

This plan targets the 260km route and assumes you are currently riding 7-9 hours per week with a solid aerobic base. If your longest ride is under three hours, add a 4-6 week base block of Zone 2 riding before starting.

### Phase 1: Aerobic Base (Weeks 1-4)

Volume: 8-10 hours per week. Four to five rides. 80 percent in Zone 2.

The base phase extends your aerobic endurance and begins the long-ride progression that is central to Ardéchoise preparation. This event is not decided by your threshold power. It is decided by how long you can sustain moderate power output without falling apart. That capacity is built here.

**Weekly structure:**
- 3-4 Zone 2 rides (1-2 hours midweek)
- 1 quality session: sweet spot intervals, 2x20 minutes at 88-93 percent FTP
- 1 long ride building from 3 hours to 4.5 hours, deliberately hilly if possible
- 1-2 off-bike sessions: core stability and hip mobility (planks, dead bugs, side planks, single-leg glute bridges)

**Key metric:** By week 4, your long ride should be 4.5 hours on hilly terrain at a comfortable aerobic pace. If that is not there yet, extend this phase.

### Phase 2: Climbing Volume (Weeks 5-8)

Volume: 10-12 hours per week. This is where the specificity begins.

The Ardéchoise demands repeated short climbing efforts over extreme duration. The build phase introduces climbing repetition sessions that train exactly this: not one long threshold climb, but many shorter efforts with brief recovery, teaching your body to clear lactate on the descent and produce power again on the next rise.

**Weekly structure:**
- 2 quality sessions (see Key Sessions below)
- 2-3 Zone 2 rides
- 1 long ride building to 5-6 hours, choosing the hilliest available route
- 1 off-bike session: core stability (maintenance)

**Quality session 1 — climbing repetitions:** Warm up 20 minutes. Ride 8-12 repetitions of a 3-5 minute climb at 85-95 percent FTP. Descend and recover for 2-3 minutes between repetitions. This session replicates the Ardéchoise climbing pattern — push over a col, recover briefly, push over the next one. The power is moderate. The accumulation is the point.

**Quality session 2 — sustained tempo on rolling terrain:** 90-120 minutes at 75-85 percent FTP on a rolling course. No coasting on descents — maintain cadence and light pressure through every part of the terrain. This teaches the pacing discipline that separates a strong Ardéchoise finish from a 230km bonk.

### Phase 3: Event-Specific (Weeks 9-12)

Volume: 10-14 hours per week. Long rides build to their peak. Quality sessions sharpen.

**Weekly structure:**
- 2 quality sessions: climbing repetitions (increased to 10-15 reps) and a race-simulation ride of 3-4 hours at event intensity with continuous climbing
- 1 long ride building to 6-7 hours on hilly terrain — this is the centrepiece of the week
- 1-2 Zone 2 rides
- 1 heat acclimatisation session (details below)

**The long rides matter more than anything else in this phase.** The Ardéchoise is a 10-14 hour event. If your longest training ride is four hours, you are not prepared. The long ride in weeks 9-12 should reach 6-7 hours with as much climbing as you can find. Practise your fuelling strategy. Practise eating real food — you will eat at the village stops and your gut needs to be trained for it. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to calculate your gram-per-hour targets across a 12-hour effort.

**Heat acclimatisation.** The Ardéchoise is held in mid-June. Temperatures in the Ardèche regularly reach 30-35 degrees. If you are training in the UK or northern Europe, the heat will be a genuine performance variable. Two approaches work:

1. **Indoor heat sessions.** Ride the turbo in a closed room with no fan, wearing a long-sleeve layer. Start with 45 minutes and build to 90 minutes over three to four sessions across weeks 9-12. The adaptation is quick — five to seven sessions generates measurable improvement in heat tolerance.

2. **Layered outdoor rides.** Overdress on warmer training days. A gilet and arm warmers on a 20-degree day raises your core temperature and triggers the same adaptation. Remove layers if you feel dizzy, nauseous, or lightheaded — heat training is productive only when controlled.

Use the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) to frame your fluid targets for 30-degree conditions. The numbers will be higher than you expect.

### Phase 4: Taper (Weeks 13-16)

Volume drops progressively. Week 13: 80 percent of peak volume. Week 14: 60 percent. Week 15: 40 percent. Week 16 (event week): two short rides with openers.

Maintain two quality sessions per week through weeks 13-14, reducing to one in week 15. Keep the climbing repetitions but reduce the number of reps — 6-8 instead of 12-15. The taper sheds fatigue while preserving the neuromuscular snap of repeated climbing efforts.

**In week 14 or 15, do a dress rehearsal.** Ride 4-5 hours on hilly terrain, execute your fuelling plan exactly, and wear the clothing you plan to use on event day. Test sunscreen application. Test your gel-and-bar strategy alongside real food. Everything that can fail on event day should be tested before event day.

## Key Sessions

### Climbing Repetitions

The signature session for Ardéchoise preparation. Find a climb of 3-5 minutes. Ride it at 85-95 percent FTP. Descend. Ride it again. Do this 8-15 times depending on your phase.

The power is moderate — this is not VO2max work. The adaptation is the body's ability to produce consistent climbing power after repeated efforts with minimal recovery. At kilometre 200 of the Ardéchoise, your 40th climb of the day will demand the same power as your 5th. This session teaches that.

If you do not have a suitable climb, use a turbo trainer with ERG mode: 3-5 minutes at 85-95 percent FTP, 2-3 minutes at 50 percent FTP, repeated 8-15 times. The outdoor version is better because it includes the descending and cornering skills, but the turbo version delivers the metabolic adaptation.

### The Long Ride

Nothing replaces this. The Ardéchoise is a properly long event. Your body must experience the demands of 6-7 hours in the saddle — the glycogen depletion, the saddle pressure, the mental fatigue, the gut challenges of eating for hours. Ride the long ride on the hilliest available route. Practise eating at regular intervals. If possible, ride with other people — the social element of a 6-hour ride prepares you for the group dynamics of the event.

### Sustained Tempo on Rolling Terrain

90-120 minutes at 75-85 percent FTP. The key discipline: never coast. Descents are active — maintain 60-70 percent FTP through every downhill. Flats are controlled — 75-80 percent FTP. Climbs are steady — 80-90 percent FTP. This session replicates the pacing pattern of the Ardéchoise, where there is no flat section long enough to properly recover and the effort must be managed continuously.

## Nutrition Strategy

A 260km day with 5,700m of climbing in 30-degree heat is one of the most metabolically demanding events in amateur cycling. The caloric expenditure is in the range of 6,000-8,000 kcal. You cannot replace all of it. You can replace enough to finish strongly.

**Target: 60-90g of carbohydrate per hour from kilometre one.** The higher end of that range is for riders who have trained their gut. The lower end is your floor — below 60g per hour across a 12-hour event, you will bonk. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to calculate exact numbers against your bodyweight and planned intensity.

| Hour | What to Eat | Notes |
|------|------------|-------|
| 1-3 | Drink mix (40-50g/hr) + gels or bars | Build the carbohydrate bank early while the gut is cooperative |
| 3-6 | Drink mix + real food at village stops | Rice cakes, bananas, cheese, bread — the village ravitaillements provide excellent options |
| 6-9 | Drink mix + gels + whatever the village offers | Gut tolerance may narrow — test this in training long rides |
| 9-12+ | Gels and liquid carbs primarily | Solid food becomes harder to process — rely on tested liquid nutrition |

**The village stops.** This is where Ardéchoise nutrition differs from a standard sportive. The ravitaillements offer real food — saucisson, local cheese, bread, fruit, pastries, sometimes wine (leave the wine for the finish-line celebration). Eating real food after 5-6 hours of riding makes a measurable difference — the salt, the fat, the variety breaks the monotony of gels and provides micronutrients that sports nutrition does not. Plan three to four village stops of 10-15 minutes each. Eat real food alongside your sports nutrition. Do not sit down for more than 15 minutes — your legs will stiffen and the remount is painful.

**Hydration in the heat.** The Ardèche in June is hot. The climbs are exposed. The descents offer brief respite before the next exposed climb. Target 750-1,000ml per hour with 400-600mg of sodium per litre. Carry two bottles and refill at every opportunity. The [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) will frame your baseline — then add 20-30 percent for the heat. If your urine is dark at a feed station, you are behind on fluids and the next hour needs to be liquid-heavy.

**Pre-race loading.** 8-10g of carbohydrate per kg of bodyweight the day before. Breakfast 3 hours before the start — 2-3g/kg of carbs, moderate protein, low fibre. The Ardéchoise typically starts early (6-7am for the longest routes), so set your alarm for 3-4am for breakfast. This is not optional. Starting a 260km day with a half-empty glycogen tank is a decision you will regret at kilometre 180.

## Equipment and Gear

### Gearing

Fit the lowest gearing your bike can accommodate. A compact chainset (50/34) with an 11-34 cassette is the baseline. Many experienced Ardéchoise riders run a sub-compact (46/30) with an 11-36. The climbs average 5-8 percent but the gradient spikes above that in sections, and at hour 10, a gear that felt easy at hour 2 feels impossible. Nobody finishes the 260km route wishing they had harder gears. Many riders finish wishing they had easier ones.

### Tyres

25-28mm at sensible pressures. The road surfaces in the Ardèche are mixed — some are beautifully smooth, others are rough and patchy on the smaller mountain roads. 28mm tyres at 5.5-6.5 bar offer the best balance of comfort and speed across a 12-hour mixed-surface day. Tubeless is preferred for the puncture protection on remote mountain roads where a roadside repair costs 30 minutes.

### Sun Protection

This is not an afterthought. Twelve hours of mid-June southern French sun will burn through standard sunscreen. Apply SPF 50 before the start, carry a small tube in your jersey pocket, and reapply at the village stops. Arm sleeves with UV protection are worth considering — they are cooler than sunscreen once wet with sweat and provide continuous coverage.

### Spares and Repairs

The Ardèche is remote. Between villages, there is no mobile phone signal on some sections, no roadside assistance, and no bike shops. Carry two inner tubes (or tubeless sealant and plugs), a multi-tool, a chain quick link, tyre levers, and a CO2 canister. If your rear derailleur breaks at kilometre 200, you need to be able to improvise. The event provides mechanical support but the wait can be 30-60 minutes on the remote sections.

### Lights

If you are targeting the 260km route and your estimated ride time exceeds 13 hours, carry a front and rear light. The Ardéchoise finishes in Saint-Félicien and some riders on the longest route finish after dark. A rear light is mandatory after sunset. A small front light weighs nothing and might save you on a technical descent in fading light.

## Race Day Pacing

The Ardéchoise is not a race. It has a ranking, and the fast riders treat it competitively, but for the vast majority of the 15,000 participants it is a challenge ride with the best atmosphere of any sportive in France. Pace it accordingly.

### Kilometres 1-80: Settle In

The first 80km are rolling hills through the northern Ardèche. The climbing is steady but not severe. Ride at 65-70 percent FTP. Eat and drink from the first kilometre. The temptation in the first two hours — when the legs feel fresh and the atmosphere is electric — is to push the pace. Do not. You have 180 kilometres and 10 hours of riding ahead of you. Every watt saved in the first three hours is a watt available at kilometre 230.

### Kilometres 80-180: The Heart of the Route

The gorges section and the major climbs arrive in the middle third. This is where the Ardéchoise reveals itself. The climbs stack up. The descents are technical and demand concentration. The heat builds through the middle of the day.

Climb at 75-85 percent FTP. Not harder. The col you are riding now is not the last one. There are twenty more behind it. Descend smoothly — no braking drama, no late-corner panic. Take the descents as recovery, spinning easy, drinking, eating a gel. Stop at two or three village ravitaillements for 10-15 minutes each. Eat real food. Refill bottles. Reapply sunscreen. Stand up, stretch, move your neck and shoulders. Remount before your legs cool down completely.

### Kilometres 180-260: The Final Third

This is where the event gets honest with you. The climbing does not ease off. The unnamed cols keep coming. Your glycogen stores are depleted regardless of how well you have fuelled. The heat of the afternoon is at its peak.

This is where your long training rides pay off. Your body knows what 6 hours in the saddle feels like. It knows the fatigue pattern. It has been here before. Ride by feel — if your power has dropped 10-15 percent from the morning, that is normal and expected. Do not chase the numbers. Keep eating. Keep drinking. Keep turning the pedals over each col.

The final 20km back to Saint-Félicien are rolling rather than mountainous. The finish line is in the town centre. The noise, the crowd, and the knowledge that you have ridden 260km through some of the most beautiful terrain in France will carry you the final few kilometres regardless of what your legs think about it.

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Starting Too Fast

The atmosphere at the Ardéchoise start is intoxicating — 15,000 riders, French cycling culture at its finest, early-morning excitement. Riders who push 80-85 percent FTP in the first two hours pay for it at kilometre 180 when the cumulative fatigue from those early watts combines with the heat and the relentless climbing. Start at 65-70 percent FTP. You will feel like you are going too easy. You are not.

### 2. Underfuelling

This is the most common reason for a bad day. A 260km ride in the heat requires 60-90g of carbohydrate per hour for 10-14 hours. That is 700-1,200g of total carbohydrate. Riders who eat 40g per hour because that is what they do on a 3-hour training ride hit the wall at kilometre 160 and spend the remaining 100km in survival mode. The village feed stations make this fixable — eat at every one. Carry gels and drink mix between stops. The gut must be trained in advance to handle this volume.

### 3. Ignoring the Heat

UK and northern European riders consistently underestimate 30-degree heat in the Ardèche. They ride at their normal intensity, sweat rates exceed their drinking rates, core temperature rises, and by hour 6 they are losing 10-20 percent of their power output purely from thermal stress. Heat acclimatisation in training, aggressive hydration from the start, and willingness to reduce intensity when the temperature spikes are the countermeasures. Use the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) to plan fluid intake at the forecast temperature — not at the temperature back home.

### 4. Lingering at Feed Stations

The village ravitaillements are wonderful. The local cheese is excellent. The shade is welcome. The chair feels like a miracle. But every minute sitting down is a minute your legs are cooling, stiffening, and forgetting how to pedal. Three or four stops of 10-15 minutes is the framework. Set a timer. When it goes off, stand up and leave. The cheese will be there again next year.

### 5. Wrong Gearing

Riders who bring race gearing to the Ardéchoise — a 52/36 with an 11-28 cassette — are the ones you see walking the climbs at kilometre 220. The individual gradients are not extreme. The cumulative effect of 5,700m of climbing over 12 hours makes every gradient feel extreme by the final third. Fit the lowest gearing available. This is not a point of pride. It is practical preparation.

## Travel and Logistics

### Getting There

Saint-Félicien is in the northern Ardèche, approximately 80km southwest of Lyon. The nearest airports are Lyon-Saint Exupéry (90 minutes drive) and Valence (45 minutes drive). Marseille and Geneva are viable at 2.5-3 hours.

**Driving from the UK:** Calais to Saint-Félicien is approximately 850km via the A6 motorway — an 8-9 hour drive, or a comfortable two-day trip with an overnight stop in Burgundy.

**Train:** Eurostar to Paris, TGV to Valence, then a local connection or taxi to Saint-Félicien. The TGV accepts bike bags. Book the bike space in advance.

### Accommodation

Saint-Félicien is a small town and accommodation is limited. Book as early as possible — ideally six months before the event. Options include hotels, gîtes (self-catering cottages), and chambres d'hôtes (bed and breakfasts) in the surrounding villages. Many riders rent a gîte with a group and split the cost.

Tournon-sur-Rhône and Lamastre are nearby towns with more accommodation options, both within 30 minutes of the start. The Ardèche is a holiday region — summer rentals are available but fill quickly once the event entries open.

### Registration and Entry

Entry typically costs EUR50-70 and opens several months before the event. The Ardéchoise rarely sells out quickly — unlike the Etape du Tour, registration remains open for weeks or months. Register through the official website. Collect your number and timing chip the evening before at the registration village in Saint-Félicien.

### Route Options

Choose deliberately. The Ardéchoise offers routes from approximately 70km to 260km, each looping through different parts of the Ardèche. All routes start and finish in Saint-Félicien. The shorter routes (70km, 110km) are genuine events — not consolation prizes — with beautiful riding and the same village-stop atmosphere. If your preparation suggests the 260km route is a stretch, the 220km or 170km route will deliver a better day than an undercooked attempt at the full distance.

## Recovery Plan

A 260km day with 5,700m of climbing in the heat generates profound fatigue. This is not a normal post-sportive recovery.

**Day 1 (post-event):** Nothing. Eat well — your body needs calories and it needs them now. Drink aggressively. If you finished in the heat, continue adding sodium to your fluids. Expect deep fatigue, possible nausea from the caloric deficit, and legs that refuse to walk downstairs normally.

**Days 2-3:** Light walking or a very gentle spin of 30-40 minutes. Focus on eating normally — your appetite may be suppressed or wildly elevated. Both are normal. Gentle stretching for the hip flexors, calves, and lower back.

**Days 4-5:** Easy Zone 1-2 rides of 60-90 minutes on flat terrain. No climbing. No intensity. The cardiovascular system recovers faster than the musculoskeletal system — your heart rate will normalise before your legs do.

**Days 6-7:** Return to normal training volume at reduced intensity. Full intensity can resume in week two. If you rode the 260km route, give yourself the full week before any quality sessions. The cumulative stress of ultra-distance riding lingers longer than shorter events and pushing back into hard training too early is a reliable path to illness or overtraining.

The FAQ section in the frontmatter addresses the specific questions that come up most for UK and international riders targeting the Ardéchoise — route choice, heat management, gearing, and the legendary village stops.

Two things worth adding:

**Is the Ardéchoise family-friendly?** Genuinely, yes. The shorter routes (70km, 110km) are achievable for fit recreational riders, and the event atmosphere is more village fête than competitive race. Many French families ride together. The organisation is excellent and the support is comprehensive. If you are looking to introduce someone to European sportives, the Ardéchoise at a shorter distance is one of the best first experiences available.

**How does this compare to the [Raid Pyrénéen](/blog/raid-pyreneen-training-guide)?** Different in almost every way. The Raid is a multi-day traverse of the Pyrenees — sustained mountain climbing, genuine cols, and the accumulated fatigue of consecutive days. The Ardéchoise is a single day of relentless, shorter climbing with a party atmosphere. The Raid is an expedition. The Ardéchoise is a celebration. Both are brilliant. The Raid demands more raw climbing fitness. The Ardéchoise demands more endurance and heat resilience.

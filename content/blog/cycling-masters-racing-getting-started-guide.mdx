You already know you could race. That is the thing. You are not unfit. You are not under-equipped. You are not too old. You are just not registered.

I talk to riders like you every week through the podcast. They have been riding seriously for years. They do structured training. They know their FTP to the watt. They can tell you the difference between sweet spot and tempo without looking at a chart. They will ride a hundred-mile sportive in the rain and barely mention it. But when you ask them if they have ever pinned a number on, the answer is always some version of the same thing: "I have been meaning to. I just haven't got around to it."

This is not a getting-around-to-it problem. This is a mental barrier dressed up as logistics.

So let me strip the logistics down to nothing and deal with the real reasons you have not signed up. Because masters racing — racing against riders your own age, with your own responsibilities, your own recovery limitations, your own creaking knees and mortgage payments — is more accessible, more social, and more rewarding than almost anything else you can do on two wheels. And the gap between where you are right now and where you need to be to pin on a number is almost certainly smaller than you think.

## How Masters Categories Actually Work

Masters racing exists because somebody in the 1970s had the reasonable thought that a 48-year-old schoolteacher should not have to race against a 23-year-old full-time athlete. The category structure is simple.

Most national federations — British Cycling, Cycling Ireland, USA Cycling, Cycling Australia — define masters as **40 and over**. From there, the sub-categories vary slightly by country but generally follow a pattern:

- **40-44**
- **45-49**
- **50-54**
- **55-59**
- **60-64**
- **65+** (sometimes broken down further)

Your age on January 1st of the racing year determines your category for the entire season. Turn 50 in November? You race the 45-49 category until 1 January.

At local and regional events, categories are often grouped more broadly — a single masters field for all 40+ riders, or a 40-49 and 50+ split. National championships tend to use the full five-year bands. The key point is this: you are not racing against the 25-year-old Cat 2 rider who trains fifteen hours a week and has no children. You are racing against people who, physiologically and logistically, are in the same decade of life as you.

That distinction changes everything.

## Masters Racing vs Open Racing: A Completely Different Experience

If you have ever watched an open Cat 3/4 race and thought "that looks terrifying," you are right — and it has almost nothing to do with what masters racing feels like.

Open amateur racing, particularly at the lower categories, is frequently chaotic. Fields are large, experience levels vary wildly, and there is a volatile mix of nervous beginners and aggressive riders trying to accumulate upgrade points. The pace is often irrational — attacks go from the gun, nobody wants to work, and the bunch-handling skills range from excellent to downright frightening.

Masters racing is different in three fundamental ways.

**The experience level is higher.** Most riders in a masters field have been cycling for ten, fifteen, twenty years. Many raced as juniors or seniors before stepping away. Even those who are completely new to racing have thousands of kilometres of group riding behind them. The bike handling is generally better. The bunch is generally calmer. Wheels overlap less frequently. Riders signal more consistently.

**The pace is hard but rational.** A masters field does not charge off from the gun and blow itself apart in the first five kilometres the way a Cat 4 field often does. The opening is firm but measured. Attacks are calculated. Riders know that a 40-year-old body cannot repeatedly spike to 600 watts the way a 22-year-old body can, so the racing rewards sustained effort and smart positioning rather than explosive violence.

**The atmosphere is unmistakably social.** Open racing can feel anonymous. Masters racing rarely does. You see the same faces week after week. You know names. You know stories. The bloke who attacked on the hill last Tuesday will hand you a coffee after the finish and ask about your daughter's GAA match. There is an intensity during the race and a warmth after it that you do not always get in open fields.

None of this means masters racing is easy. It is hard. It can be brutally hard. But it is hard in a way that rewards the things you are good at — consistency, patience, reading a race, managing your effort across 60 or 90 minutes — rather than demanding the one thing that age has actually taken from you, which is peak anaerobic power.

## The Mental Barrier Is the Only Real Barrier

Here is what I hear from fit, capable riders who do not race:

*"I am not fast enough."*
*"I do not want to come last."*
*"I would not know what to do in a bunch at that speed."*
*"I will look stupid."*
*"Everyone else will be better than me."*

Every single one of these is a story you are telling yourself, and every single one of them dissolves on contact with reality.

**You are faster than you think.** If you can hold 3.0 w/kg for an hour, you are competitive in most regional masters fields. Not winning — competitive. That is an FTP of around 225 watts for a 75kg rider. If you have been doing structured training for more than a year, you are probably already there or close to it. Run the numbers on the masters FTP benchmark tool if you want to see where you sit.

**Someone has to come last.** It is a race. There is a last place. Someone will finish there, and it might be you on your first outing. This does not matter in any meaningful way. Nobody in a masters field judges the rider who finishes at the back. They judge the rider who does not show up. The gap between "I have never raced" and "I have raced once and finished last" is enormous. The gap between last place and mid-pack is usually just a few more races of experience.

**Bunch riding at race speed is a learnable skill.** If you can hold a wheel on a club run, you can hold a wheel in a masters race. The pace is higher, yes. The proximity is closer, yes. But the fundamental skill — following a wheel, holding your line, reading the movements of riders around you — is the same skill you use every Saturday morning. It just matters more when there is a number on your back.

**You will not look stupid.** You will look like a first-timer, which is different. Other riders will see it — the hesitation on corners, the slightly too-big gap to the wheel in front, the nervousness at the start line — and most of them will be supportive because they remember being exactly where you are.

## Time Trials: The Perfect Starting Point

If the idea of riding in a bunch at race pace still makes you uncomfortable, start with a time trial.

A time trial removes every variable that makes bunch racing intimidating. There is no peloton. There are no corner-exit surges. There is no wheel overlap. There is no tactical positioning. It is you, the road, and a clock. You start at a set time — usually at one-minute intervals behind the previous rider — and you ride as hard as you can from the start to the finish.

The standard distance for a club time trial in the UK is 10 miles. That takes most masters riders somewhere between 22 and 30 minutes, depending on fitness, course, and conditions. If you can ride at threshold for 25 minutes on the turbo, you can race a 10-mile TT. You already have the fitness. You just have not pointed it at a finish line yet.

Here is why time trials are perfect for nervous first-timers:

**There is no drafting.** You ride alone, at your own pace, with nobody's wheel to follow and nobody following yours. The tactical complexity is zero. The bike-handling demands are minimal — you are riding in a straight line on an open road.

**You get an objective result.** A time, a distance, a speed. You can compare it to your training, to your previous efforts, to other riders in your age category. There is clarity in that. You know exactly where you stand, and you know exactly what you need to work on to go faster.

**The atmosphere is low-pressure.** Club TTs — the events run by local cycling clubs on weekday evenings or weekend mornings — are the most welcoming environment in competitive cycling. Marshals will talk you through the start procedure. Other riders will chat to you in the car park afterwards. The timekeeper will hand you your result with a smile and a "well done." It is racing, but it does not feel like being thrown into the deep end.

**You can race one this week.** Seriously. Most areas in the UK and Ireland have club TTs running from April through September, often on midweek evenings. Check your local club's website or the CTT (Cycling Time Trials) calendar. Entry is usually a few pounds at the line, no pre-registration needed. You can decide to race on Tuesday morning and be on the start line by Tuesday evening.

Once you have done two or three time trials and realised that racing is not the terrifying ordeal you imagined, the step to a road race or criterium is much smaller.

## Road Race, Criterium, or Time Trial: Which Suits You?

Different race formats suit different riders, and understanding the demands of each will help you pick the right first event.

### Time Trial

**Best for:** The rider who trains alone, who is strong at sustained efforts, who does not enjoy bunch dynamics, or who simply wants to start with the lowest-risk format.

**Demands:** Sustained threshold power, pacing discipline, aerodynamic position. Bike handling is minimal. Tactical demands are close to zero.

**Typical duration:** 20-35 minutes for a 10-mile TT, 50-70 minutes for a 25-mile TT.

**Masters angle:** Time trialling rewards consistency and pacing — two things that improve with age. Many of the strongest masters time triallists in the country did not start racing until their forties. The event is also forgiving of limited training hours: you can be competitive in a 10-mile TT on six hours of weekly riding.

### Road Race

**Best for:** The rider who enjoys group dynamics, who has good bike handling, who can read a race, and who has the endurance for 60-100km at racing pace.

**Demands:** Sustained power, repeated above-threshold efforts on climbs and in crosswinds, bunch-riding skills, tactical awareness.

**Typical duration:** 90 minutes to 3 hours for masters events, though many local masters road races are 60-90 minutes.

**Masters angle:** Road racing is where tactical intelligence compensates most for declining raw power. Knowing when to sit in, when to move up, when to let a break go, and when to commit — these are skills that take years to develop, and masters riders have those years. A 42-year-old with 3.2 w/kg who reads the race well will beat a 42-year-old with 3.5 w/kg who does not.

### Criterium

**Best for:** The rider who likes intensity, who can produce repeated short bursts, who has sharp cornering skills, and who thrives in fast, aggressive racing.

**Demands:** Repeated anaerobic efforts (15-30 seconds), cornering technique, positioning in a fast-moving bunch, the ability to recover between efforts while still riding at tempo.

**Typical duration:** 30-60 minutes.

**Masters angle:** Criteriums are arguably the hardest format for masters riders because they place the highest demand on anaerobic power — the energy system that declines most noticeably with age. That said, experienced masters crit racers compensate with superior positioning (saving energy by holding the right wheel), better cornering (carrying more speed through turns), and smarter effort management. If you enjoy the controlled chaos of a fast group ride, you will enjoy criteriums. But they are not the best starting point if you have never raced.

## Equipment: What You Need and What You Do Not

The equipment requirements for masters racing are the same as for any other road race. Here is the short version.

**You need:**

- A road bike in good mechanical condition. It does not need to be new, light, or expensive. It needs working brakes, good tyres, and a clean drivetrain.
- A certified helmet that fits properly. No cracks, no expired foam, chin strap done up.
- Bar-end plugs. Both sides. Non-negotiable.
- Front and rear lights if the event requires them. Check the event listing.
- Six safety pins for your race number.
- Appropriate clothing. A cycling jersey and shorts are fine. You do not need a skinsuit for your first race.

**You do not need:**

- A new bike. The bike you train on is the bike you race on. Full stop.
- A power meter (useful for TTs, not essential for anything).
- Aero wheels. They are nice. They are not necessary. The rider matters more than the wheels at this level.
- A team kit. Plenty of masters riders race in plain jerseys or club kit.
- A specific race bike versus a training bike. If you only have one bike, that is your race bike.

**Common masters mistakes:**

The most common equipment mistake I see in masters racing is not under-equipping — it is overthinking. Riders who have spent years reading reviews and comparing groupsets convince themselves they need a specific setup before they can race. You do not. If your bike passes a commissaire check — brakes work, tyres are good, bar ends plugged, no aero bars in a bunch race — you are ready.

The second most common mistake is showing up to a road race or criterium with clip-on aero bars still attached. They are banned in bunch races. Remove them. If you forget, the commissaire will not let you start, and you will have driven an hour for nothing.

## What Fitness Level Do You Actually Need?

Less than you think. That is the real answer.

For a **10-mile time trial**, you need to sustain a hard effort for 22-30 minutes. If you can do a 20-minute FTP test on the turbo without falling off, you have the fitness for a 10-mile TT.

For a **masters road race**, you need a solid aerobic base and the ability to respond to changes in pace. In practical terms, if you can ride a club chain gang for 60-90 minutes without being dropped, you have the fitness for a regional masters road race. You will not win it. But you will finish it, and finishing is all that matters the first time.

For a **criterium**, you need repeated short efforts above threshold. If you regularly do interval sessions with efforts in the 15-30 second range, and you can recover while riding at tempo, you have the fitness for a masters crit.

The benchmarks that matter:

- **3.0 w/kg FTP** puts you in the middle of most regional masters fields
- **3.5 w/kg FTP** makes you competitive at regional level and respectable at national level
- **4.0 w/kg FTP** makes you a serious contender at national masters level

If you do not know your w/kg, use the masters FTP benchmark tool to see where you sit relative to your age group. But do not use a number as a reason to delay. Plenty of riders race their first masters event at 2.5 w/kg and have the time of their lives.

## Finding Local Masters Races

This is the part that stops a surprising number of riders. They want to race but they do not know where to look.

**United Kingdom:** Start with the [British Cycling event calendar](https://www.britishcycling.org.uk/events). Filter by discipline (road, time trial, criterium) and by category. Masters-specific events are listed separately, but many open events also include masters categories within the same race. The CTT (Cycling Time Trials) website lists time trials across the country, and most local clubs run evening 10s through the summer.

**Ireland:** The [Cycling Ireland event calendar](https://www.cyclingireland.ie) lists all sanctioned events. Masters racing in Ireland is thriving — the national masters championships draw strong fields, and regional leagues run throughout the season. Your provincial cycling body (Leinster, Munster, Connacht, Ulster) will have local event listings.

**United States:** [USA Cycling](https://www.usacycling.org) has a comprehensive event finder. Masters racing in the US is structured differently — many events run combined fields with results split by age category. State and regional championships offer dedicated masters fields.

**Australia:** [AusCycling](https://www.auscycling.org.au) lists events by state. Masters racing is well-established, particularly in the state-level racing calendars.

Beyond the national federations, your local cycling club is the single best resource for finding races. Walk into any club spin and ask "where do the masters lads race?" You will leave with a calendar of events, three recommendations for your first race, and probably a lift to the venue.

## The Social Side: Why Masters Racing Is Really About Community

Here is something that the race results do not capture. Masters racing has a social dimension that open racing frequently lacks, and for many riders, this turns out to be the real reason they keep coming back.

The post-race coffee is not optional. It is part of the event. You will sit in a cafe or a village hall with riders you just spent an hour trying to beat, and you will talk about the race, about training, about work, about your kids, about the col you rode in Mallorca last spring. The bloke who attacked on the hill will buy you a flat white and explain exactly why he went at that moment. The woman who won the 50+ category will tell you about the interval session she did on Tuesday that gave her the legs to hold the break.

This is the tribe that most masters cyclists are looking for without knowing it. Not a generic online community. Not a Facebook group. An actual group of people who share your specific combination of competitive drive and ageing body, who understand that you cannot train on Friday because your back is in bits, who will text you the night before a race to confirm you are still coming.

Masters racing WhatsApp groups are legendary. Race information, training tips, mechanical advice, and an enormous amount of slagging. If you think your local club group chat is active, wait until you join a masters racing group.

Many masters racing communities also travel together — to national championships, to international masters events, to training camps. The racing is the anchor, but the friendships are the structure that builds around it. I have spoken to hundreds of masters racers through the podcast, and the pattern is consistent: they came for the racing and stayed for the people.

## Racing With Experience: Your Secret Advantage

The thing about being a 45-year-old in a bike race: you have advantages that no 25-year-old possesses, and they matter more than you realise.

**You know how to suffer efficiently.** Two decades of adult life — work stress, sleep deprivation, family demands — have taught you how to function when things are uncomfortable. You do not panic when the pace goes up. You do not catastrophise when your legs hurt. You know the difference between "this hurts" and "I am actually in trouble," and that distinction is worth minutes in a long race.

**You read situations better.** You can look at a group of riders and identify who is strong, who is bluffing, who is about to crack. You notice the rider who keeps checking over their shoulder — they are nervous, probably about to get dropped. You notice the rider who is sitting third wheel and has not taken a single pull — they are saving it for the finish. This kind of situational awareness takes years to develop. You have those years.

**You manage your effort.** A younger rider with more power will often burn matches early because they can. You cannot afford to, so you do not. You sit in when the pace is high. You move up when it eases. You use the draft. You choose your moments. This is not weakness — it is intelligence, and in a masters road race, it wins more often than raw watts.

**You know what matters.** You are not going to the Tour de France. Neither is anyone else in your masters field. The pressure is internal, the rewards are personal, and the consequences of losing are a slightly bruised ego and a second coffee. This perspective lets you race with a lightness that many younger riders lack. You can take risks because the stakes are low. You can enjoy the process because you are old enough to know that the process is the point.

## How to Actually Get Started: A Five-Step Process

Enough theory. Here is what to do this week.

**Step one: Pick a format.** If you have never raced, start with a time trial. If you are comfortable in a fast group and want bunch racing, pick a road race or criterium. But pick one.

**Step two: Find an event.** Go to your national federation's event calendar and find a masters event or a club TT within the next four weeks. Not the next six months. The next four weeks. If you give yourself too long, you will talk yourself out of it.

**Step three: Sort your licence.** Buy a day licence at the event or register for an annual licence online. This takes ten minutes.

**Step four: Tell someone.** Tell a riding mate, tell your partner, post it in the club WhatsApp group. Public commitment is the most effective motivational tool ever invented. Once you have told people you are racing on Saturday, the social cost of backing out is higher than the social cost of finishing last.

**Step five: Show up.** Arrive early. Sign on. Pin your number on. Ride the race. Do not overthink it. Do not wait until you are "ready." You are ready now. You have been ready for months. The only thing missing is the act of doing it.

No fluff, no motivational posters. Just riders who take this seriously and support each other in doing it.

## The First Race Is the Hardest. Everything After That Is Easier.

I have said this to hundreds of riders and I will say it to you: the first race is the hardest thing you will do in cycling. Not physically — physically it is just a hard ride. Mentally, it is enormous. You are stepping into an unfamiliar environment, with unfamiliar rules, surrounded by people who look like they know what they are doing when you feel like you do not.

But here is the other thing I have said to hundreds of riders: not a single one of them regretted doing it. Not one. Even the ones who got dropped in the first ten minutes. Even the ones who finished last. Even the ones who forgot to eat and bonked with five kilometres to go. Every single one of them came back and said some version of the same thing: "I wish I had done this years ago."

You have the fitness. You have the bike. You have the experience. The only thing standing between you and a race number is a decision.

Make it.

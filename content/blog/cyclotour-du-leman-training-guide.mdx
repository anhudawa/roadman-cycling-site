Here is the thing nobody tells you about the Cyclotour du Léman. It looks like a flat ride around a lake. The profile has no signature climb. There is no Col du Galibier waiting at kilometre 120, no Alpe d'Huez at the end of a long day. And because of that, riders show up undertrained. They assume that 180 kilometres of rolling terrain around Lake Geneva will be easier than whatever UK century they finished last autumn. It is not. The Cyclotour is a different kind of hard — the kind that arrives quietly at kilometre 140, when the French shore has rolled you over a dozen short climbs you were not counting and a westerly headwind is waiting for the final 50 kilometres back to Lausanne.

180 kilometres. Approximately 1,800-2,000 metres of climbing. Two countries. Six to eight hours in the saddle. The route circumnavigates Lac Léman starting and finishing in Lausanne, passing through Montreux, crossing into France at Évian-les-Bains, running along the southern shore to Geneva, then returning up the northern shore. Three to four thousand riders do it every late May or early June. The scenery is extraordinary — the Alps behind you, the Jura across the water, the Lavaux vineyard terraces as UNESCO World Heritage terrain. But the beauty hides a genuine test of endurance, and this is precisely what makes it the ideal intermediate European sportive. Here is how to prepare for it properly across 12 weeks.

## Key Takeaways

- The Cyclotour du Léman covers approximately 180km with 1,800-2,000m of rolling climbing — the challenge is distance and sustained effort, not individual climbs
- Target 2.5 W/kg for a comfortable finish, 3.0+ W/kg for a strong ride — this event rewards pacing discipline over climbing power
- Wind off Lake Geneva is the variable most riders underestimate — the prevailing westerly creates a headwind on the northern return leg that can add 30-60 minutes to the second half
- A 12-week build peaking at 10-12 hours per week with progressive long rides to 5-6 hours trains the endurance base 180km demands
- The route crosses between Switzerland and France — carry your passport or ID
- Fuelling for 6-8 hours is the make-or-break skill — riders who bonk at kilometre 140 almost always underfuelled in the first three hours
- The Lavaux vineyards are one of the most beautiful cycling stretches in Europe — enjoy it, because the French side is where the work begins

## Course Profile and Key Sections

### Lausanne to Montreux: The Lavaux Vineyards (km 0-30)

The first 30 kilometres run east along the northern shore through the Lavaux vineyard terraces — the section every photograph of the Cyclotour comes from. The UNESCO-listed vineyards cascade down to the lake, the Alps frame the horizon, and on a clear late-May morning it is one of the finest cycling stretches anywhere in Europe. The road rises and falls gently with a few short 2-3% ramps. The surface is excellent.

Here is where it gets interesting. This section is exposed. Wind funnels along the lake surface and the Lavaux stretch catches crosswinds depending on the day. In the morning start, conditions are usually calm. But the riders who come back along this northern shore six hours later will meet whatever the afternoon has built. Enjoy the opening. Eat early. The Lavaux is for photographs on a training camp, not for burning matches on race day.

### Montreux to Évian-les-Bains: The Eastern Shore and the Border (km 30-70)

Past Montreux the route curves around the eastern end of the lake. The terrain remains rolling — short kicks as the road moves away from the lakeshore through the villages above it. You cross from Switzerland into France, and the transition is worth noting: the road surface changes. Swiss roads are immaculate. French roads on the southern shore vary — some smooth, others with patching and rougher aggregate. Riders on 25mm tyres at 7 bar notice it. The border crossing also means a change of mobile network. Check your roaming settings before the start.

### Évian-les-Bains to Geneva: The French Southern Shore (km 70-140)

This is where the Cyclotour starts being honest. The French southern shore between Évian and Geneva looks flat on a map. It is not flat on a bike. The road rolls constantly — short punchy climbs of 1-2 kilometres at 4-6%, dropping into brief descents, then rising again. None of these would trouble you on fresh legs. At kilometre 100, they accumulate into something more significant than the profile suggests.

Let me break this down. The climbing comes in 20-30 individual efforts, each requiring you to increase power for 2-4 minutes, settle, then do it again. The riders who treat every rise as a small effort to dispatch quickly — standing, punching over the top — empty their legs before Geneva. The riders who stay seated, hold Zone 2, and let the gradient set the cadence arrive with something left for the return.

The feed stations on the French side differ from the Swiss ones. Expect bread, fruit, water. Carry your own gels and bars as insurance. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to map your hourly intake before the event, not during it.

### Geneva to Lausanne: The Northern Return (km 140-180)

The final 40-50 kilometres from Geneva back to Lausanne are where the event is decided, and it has almost nothing to do with fitness. It has to do with wind.

Lake Geneva sits in a natural corridor between the Alps and the Jura. The prevailing wind is westerly. In the morning, when you rode east through the Lavaux, you had it at your back. Now you are heading west, and the afternoon westerly is in your face. On a day when the wind has built — which happens more often than the event's reputation suggests — you are riding 40 kilometres into a sustained headwind at the end of an already long day.

The strategy is simple: find a group. Drafting at sportive speeds reduces your effort by 25-30%. If you have been solo, Geneva is the place to find wheels and commit to them. Hold 65-70% of your [FTP](/tools/ftp-zones), sit in, and let the group carry you home. Riders who try to ride the northern return alone into a headwind at threshold bonk at kilometre 160 and limp the final 20 kilometres.

## Fitness Benchmarks

Two numbers matter: your watts per kilogram at threshold and how long your body can sustain moderate effort without falling apart. Use the [FTP zone calculator](/tools/ftp-zones) to set your training targets.

| Capacity | FTP (W/kg) | Longest Training Ride | Weekly Volume | Average Speed Target |
|----------|-----------|----------------------|---------------|---------------------|
| Strong finish | 3.0+ | 5-6 hours | 10-12 hrs/wk | 28-32 km/h |
| Comfortable finish | 2.5-3.0 | 4-5 hours | 8-10 hrs/wk | 24-28 km/h |
| Completion | 2.0-2.5 | 4 hours | 7-9 hrs/wk | 20-24 km/h |

The good news is that the Cyclotour du Léman does not demand the FTP numbers that a mountain sportive requires. A rider at 2.5 W/kg who paces well, eats consistently, and rides the headwind section in a group will finish comfortably. A rider at 3.0 W/kg who goes out hard through the Lavaux and underfuels on the French shore will not. This event rewards discipline and endurance over raw power.

## 12-Week Training Plan Overview

Twelve weeks is the working minimum for a rider with a 6-8 hour weekly base. If you are starting from less than that, add four weeks of base riding at the front.

### Phase 1: Endurance Base (Weeks 1-3)

Volume: 7-9 hours per week. Four to five rides, 80% in Zone 2.

The foundation. This is where the aerobic engine that powers a 180km day gets built. Seiler's polarised-training research is clear on this: the easy riding has to be properly easy. Heart rate in the lower half of Zone 2, conversational pace, no surging on the short climbs in your local loop. The temptation in week 1 is to ride harder than the plan demands because the legs are fresh and the motivation is high. Resist it. Riders who hammer the base block are the same riders who plateau in week 6.

**Anchor session:** Long weekend ride building from 2.5 hours (week 1) to 3.5 hours (week 3). Include 400-600 metres of climbing per ride. If 3.5 hours at conversational pace feels difficult at the end of week 3, the later phases need adjusting.

**Off-the-bike:** Two sessions per week of core stability and hip mobility. Planks, side planks, dead bugs, single-leg glute bridges, and hip flexor stretches. A 180km day in the saddle is six to eight hours of sustained spinal loading. The riders whose lower backs seize at kilometre 130 are almost always the ones who skipped this work. Fifteen minutes, twice a week. It is not exciting. It is fixable.

### Phase 2: Distance Building (Weeks 4-7)

Volume: 9-11 hours per week. The long ride extends and one quality session per week enters the programme.

**Long ride progression:** 3.5 hours (week 4) building to 5 hours (week 7). By the end of this phase, you need one ride of 4.5-5 hours in the legs with 800-1,000 metres of climbing. This is the rehearsal for the distance. If your only long ride before the Cyclotour is 3 hours, you are guessing about what hours 5-7 feel like, and the guess is almost always wrong.

**Quality session:** One threshold effort per week. Start with 3x10 minutes at 90-95% FTP with 5 minutes recovery. Build to 3x15 minutes by week 7. This is not about raising FTP — it is about raising the ceiling so that 65-70% FTP on event day feels sustainable rather than effortful.

**Wind practice:** At least two rides in this block should be solo rides into a headwind at controlled effort. Find a route with an exposed section and ride the headwind at 65-70% FTP — lower position, steady cadence, controlled breathing. The point is to practise holding effort when speed drops. Riders who watch their speed fall from 30km/h to 22km/h into a headwind panic and push harder. Riders who have practised it hold the watts and accept the speed.

### Phase 3: Event-Specific (Weeks 8-10)

Volume: 10-12 hours per week. This is the peak of the programme.

**Long ride peak:** One ride of 5-6 hours with a full fuelling rehearsal. Carry the food you plan to carry on event day. Eat at 60-80 grams of carbohydrate per hour from the first 30 minutes. This ride tells you everything: whether your saddle works at hour 5, whether your stomach handles gels at hour 4, whether your pacing discipline holds when the legs start reporting fatigue. Every problem discovered in training is fixable. Every problem discovered at the Cyclotour is a problem you endure.

**Group riding:** At least two rides in this block should be in a group. The headwind section back to Lausanne rewards group-riding skills. If your club has a chain-gang session, this is the phase where it pays dividends.

**Sustained tempo:** One session per week of 2x30 minutes at 78-85% FTP on rolling terrain — mirroring what the French southern shore actually demands.

### Phase 4: Taper (Weeks 11-12)

Volume drops 30% in week 11, another 30-40% in week 12. Short intensity stays — 3x5 minutes at threshold, 4x90 seconds at VO2max — to keep the legs responsive without adding fatigue. The long ride in week 11 is 2.5 hours. The long ride in week 12 is 90 minutes. No rides longer than 2 hours in the final five days.

You will feel sluggish. Your legs will feel heavy. You will convince yourself that two weeks of reduced riding has cost you fitness. It has not. The fitness is banked. What is dropping is fatigue, and that is the entire point. Trust the taper.

## Key Sessions

**The five-hour endurance ride.** Zone 2, 800-1,000 metres of climbing, full fuelling practice. This is the most important single session in the 12-week programme. It proves that your body can handle the duration. Schedule it for week 9 or 10.

**Threshold blocks.** 3x12-15 minutes at 90-95% FTP. Builds the ceiling that makes event-day effort feel manageable. Once a week in the build and event-specific phases.

**Wind simulation.** Solo ride into a headwind at 65-70% FTP for 40-60 minutes. Practise holding watts when the speed drops. Practise staying low. Practise not caring about the number on the speed display. Once a fortnight in the build phase, once more in the event-specific phase.

## Nutrition Strategy

180 kilometres at 6-8 hours demands 60-80 grams of carbohydrate per hour from the gun. Not from kilometre 50 when you feel like it. From the start, when appetite is strong and the pace feels easy. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to map your hourly targets and the [calories calculator](/tools/calories) to estimate total expenditure.

| Section | Km | Hours | Carbs/Hour | Food Type |
|---------|-----|-------|-----------|-----------|
| Lausanne to Montreux | 0-30 | 0-1 | 60-70g | Bars, rice cakes, banana — solid food while appetite is strong |
| Montreux to Évian | 30-70 | 1-2.5 | 60-80g | Mix of gels and solids, drink mix in one bottle |
| Évian to Geneva | 70-140 | 2.5-5.5 | 60-80g | Gels, feed station food, flat cola at stops — eat to schedule |
| Geneva to Lausanne | 140-180 | 5.5-7.5 | 40-60g | Whatever your stomach accepts — liquid calories if solids refuse |

**Pre-event loading.** Carb-load 24-36 hours before: 8-10 grams per kilogram of body weight. Pasta, rice, bread, potatoes — plain, nothing new. Eat a substantial meal 3-4 hours before the start and a smaller snack 60-90 minutes before.

**Feed stations.** Do not rely on stations for your entire fuelling plan. Carry your own gels, bars, and drink mix. Use the stations to supplement. Check the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) for fluid targets based on the temperature forecast. Do not stop for more than 5-7 minutes at any station. Riders who sit down and chat for 15 minutes at every stop lose an hour across the day and restart on stiff legs every time.

## Equipment and Gear

This is not a mountain event. The bike setup should prioritise comfort for 6-8 hours over climbing weight.

**Gearing.** A compact chainset (50/34) with an 11-28 or 11-30 cassette handles everything the Cyclotour produces. The climbs are short — 2-4 kilometres at 4-6% — and nothing requires mountain gearing. If you are comfortable on hilly UK roads with a compact setup, the same gearing works here.

**Tyres.** 28mm or 30mm at sensible pressure. The French road surfaces vary, and wider tyres at lower pressure smooth out the rougher sections without any meaningful speed penalty at sportive pace. Run 5-6 bar for a 75kg rider — use a tyre pressure calculator if you have one. Carry two inner tubes, a mini-pump, tyre levers, and a multi-tool.

**Contact points.** Saddle, bar tape, bibs. If your saddle gives you trouble at four hours in training, it will be worse at six hours on event day. Sort it before the taper. Fresh bar tape. Good bibs. Chamois cream applied generously at the start.

**Wind gear.** A lightweight gilet for the exposed northern return. Late May at Lake Geneva can deliver rain from lake weather systems with little warning. A packable rain shell weighing 100 grams is cheap insurance. Arm warmers for the early start.

## Race Day Pacing

Split the 180 kilometres into quarters. Each quarter has a different character and demands a different approach.

**Km 0-45 (Lausanne through Lavaux to past Montreux): 60-65% FTP.** Scenic, rolling, fast groups forming. Sit in. Eat. Do not chase surges. Heart rate should feel boring. If it feels like an effort, you are riding too hard. The Lavaux is not where you make time. It is where you save it.

**Km 45-90 (Eastern shore and into France): 65-70% FTP.** The terrain picks up slightly. Hold your effort, not your speed. If a short climb pushes you to 75% FTP for two minutes, that is fine — settle immediately afterwards. Eat every 20-30 minutes. Drink every 15.

**Km 90-140 (French southern shore, Évian to Geneva): 65-70% FTP.** This is where the event lives. The rolling hills accumulate. Your legs start reporting. Hold your zones. Stay seated on the climbs unless you need to stretch. Every effort above 80% FTP here is an effort you will not have for the headwind section.

**Km 140-180 (Geneva to Lausanne, the headwind return): 65-70% FTP, in a group.** Find wheels. Sit in. Take short turns — 30 seconds to a minute — and rotate. If you are alone, drop your position, hold 65% FTP, and accept whatever speed that produces. Do not fight the wind. Ride through it. The final 10 kilometres into Lausanne will feel long. They are always long. Keep pedalling.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Treating it as a flat ride.** The profile looks gentle. It is not gentle. 1,800-2,000 metres of climbing spread across 180 kilometres means constant undulation, and the muscular cost of 20-30 short climbs is higher than the same elevation gained in two or three long ascents. Train on rolling terrain, not flat roads.

**2. Ignoring the wind.** Lake Geneva is 72 kilometres long and 13 kilometres wide. The thermal patterns create consistent afternoon westerlies. The northern return leg into this wind is the make-or-break section. Practise riding into headwinds at controlled effort.

**3. Underfuelling in the first three hours.** Appetite is strong early. The pace feels easy. Riders think they do not need to eat yet. By kilometre 100, when the French shore starts rolling and the effort rises, they reach for the food they should have eaten two hours ago and discover that the deficit cannot be closed. Eat from the first 30 minutes. Sixty grams of carbohydrate per hour, minimum.

**4. Stopping too long at feed stations.** Five minutes is a stop. Fifteen minutes is a rest. Rests cost more than the time they take — the legs stiffen, the heart rate drops, and the restart is harder than the riding. Fill bottles, eat something, leave.

**5. Skipping the long training ride.** If your longest ride before the Cyclotour is three hours, you do not know what five hours feels like. The 5-6 hour training ride in weeks 9-10 is not optional. It is the session that makes the event manageable rather than survivable.

## Travel and Logistics

**Getting there.** Lausanne is served by Geneva Airport (50 minutes by train) and Zürich Airport (2.5 hours by train). Swiss trains accept bike boxes. From the UK by car, Lausanne is roughly 10 hours from Calais.

**Accommodation.** Book early. The event attracts 3,000-4,000 riders and Lausanne hotels fill. Montreux, Vevey, and Nyon are alternatives along the lake with good train connections. Arrive a day early to ride the Lavaux section as a recce.

**Currency.** Switzerland uses Swiss francs (CHF), France uses euros (EUR). Carry a small amount of cash in both currencies. Card payments work almost everywhere on both sides.

**Border crossing.** Schengen crossings are normally open, but carry a valid passport or national ID card. UK passport holders post-Brexit should carry their passport.

**Entry.** Registration typically opens in January or February. Entry costs approximately CHF80-100. Popular start waves fill first — register early.

## Recovery Plan

The week after the Cyclotour follows a simple rule: move, do not train.

**Day 1.** Complete rest. Eat well. Hydrate. Sleep.

**Days 2-3.** A 30-45 minute spin at recovery pace. Below Zone 2, legs turning, no effort.

**Days 4-5.** A 60-90 minute easy ride. Zone 1-2. The fatigue from a 180km day takes 5-7 days to fully clear, even when you feel recovered.

**Days 6-7.** Return to normal easy riding. If the Cyclotour was the season goal, take a full recovery week at reduced volume before deciding what comes next.

## Where the Cyclotour Sits

The Cyclotour du Léman sits between a UK century and the true ultra-distance events. Harder than a hundred miles, less demanding than the [Mallorca 312](/blog/mallorca-312-training-guide) or the [Tour de Suisse Gran Fondo](/blog/tour-de-suisse-granfondo-training-guide). It has genuine climbing but it is not a mountain event. It crosses two countries, which makes it feel like an adventure rather than a loop.

For riders building towards longer European events — the 312, the Haute Route, a multi-day tour — the Cyclotour is the logical proving ground. 180 kilometres, rolling terrain, wind management, and the discipline of fuelling for 6-8 hours. Get those right here and the bigger events become the next conversation.

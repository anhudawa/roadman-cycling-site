There is no single “Dan Lorang plan” in the reviewed Roadman interviews. There is a coaching framework: decide what the rider is training for, understand the athlete and the life around them, choose a load they can repeat, and adjust it using recovery and response.

This page owns that practical framework. The [complete Dan Lorang podcast archive](/blog/every-roadman-episode-with-dan-lorang) owns the listening order. The [Dan Lorang entity page](/entity/dan-lorang) owns his current role and biography.

## Source boundary: what Lorang actually said

In the 2024 Roadman interview, Lorang described consistency as the foundation of endurance performance. He linked consistency to managing the balance between load and recovery so the athlete avoids recurring illness, injury and fatigue. He explicitly separated this from copying the risk profile of elite sport.

In the 2025 interview, he began season planning with priority races, then worked backwards while accounting for the athlete's starting point. He discussed different taper responses, total life stress, and the need for riders over 40 to look beyond training hours to sleep, work, family and social load.

The transcripts do not produce an exact eight-hour schedule. They produce the following planning sequence.

## Step 1: choose the repeatable load

Start with what you completed over the last four to six weeks without recurring illness, pain or persistent fatigue. That is evidence about current capacity. A proposed week that only works when work is quiet, sleep is perfect and family demands disappear is not yet repeatable.

Lorang's point is not that training should always feel easy. It is that the loading pattern must be sustainable enough to repeat and specific enough to create adaptation.

## Step 2: put life on the calendar first

Mark work deadlines, travel, childcare, late nights, appointments and important social commitments before placing the hardest or longest sessions. Lorang described professional coaches seeing the full calendar because non-training stress changes how much training an athlete can absorb.

For an amateur, this may mean moving a demanding ride away from a deadline week, reducing the load during travel, or protecting sleep instead of forcing a session into an already overloaded day.

## Step 3: define the goal and plan backwards

Choose priority events rather than treating every race as an A-race. Work backwards from the primary target, identify the demands of the event, and decide what must be developed before the final specific phase.

The length and content of each phase depend on the rider's current profile. Lorang does not support pretending that identical calendars produce identical adaptations.

## Step 4: give every session a job

Lorang described an inverse relationship between volume and intensity: high-intensity work is normally shorter, while easy aerobic endurance can run longer. That is a design principle, not a fixed intensity distribution.

Write the purpose beside each session—endurance, a specific high-intensity stimulus, strength, skill, recovery or event-specific practice. If a session has no distinct purpose, decide whether it belongs in the week.

## Step 5: read power, heart rate and perceived exertion together

Power shows external work. Heart rate shows a physiological response to that work. Perceived exertion adds information about how the effort feels. Heat, sleep, illness and life stress can separate those signals.

Do not react mechanically to one number. Compare familiar sessions, look for repeated patterns and ask why the signals disagree. Lorang said he uses calculated performance charts for within-athlete comparison, not to compare one athlete with another or to let an algorithm replace judgment.

## Step 6: adjust before the plan breaks

Persistent fatigue, recurring illness, disrupted sleep or pain are not proof of commitment. They are information that total load may exceed current recovery. Reduce, replace or skip a session when the evidence says the athlete is over the limit.

Lorang was especially clear that riders over 40 often feel external load more acutely. That is not a universal instruction to train less; it is an instruction to count more of the stress that exists outside the bike.

## A Roadman translation—not a quoted Lorang week

Use this decision order when building the next seven days:

1. Protect the sleep and recovery needed for the week to be repeatable.
2. Place the sessions most specific to the current goal on the lowest-stress days.
3. Add aerobic work at a duration the rider can recover from.
4. Place strength consistently across the season if it belongs in the athlete's programme.
5. Review heart rate, power, perceived exertion, pain, illness and sleep before progressing load.

This is deliberately not an 80/20 template or a promise of results in a fixed number of weeks. Those claims are not present as universal rules in the reviewed transcripts.

Cornering confidence is not a promise that the tyre has more grip. It is the
ability to read the bend early, choose a speed and line with room to change the
plan, and make smooth inputs on a bicycle that has passed its checks.

This guide is for road riding on firm surfaces. It cannot make blind bends,
traffic, gravel, diesel, ice or damaged equipment predictable.

## 1. Check the bicycle first

Before any handling session, inspect:

- wheel security and bearing play;
- tyre condition, measured width and pressure;
- tyre-rim compatibility and frame clearance;
- pad wear, braking surfaces and lever response;
- handlebar, stem and headset security;
- pedal and cleat engagement.

Stop if a tyre is damaged, a wheel moves unexpectedly, braking is inconsistent
or a component is outside its maker's limit. Technique cannot compensate for a
mechanical fault.

Use the [Roadman tyre-pressure calculator](/tools/tyre-pressure) as a starting
estimate from complete system weight and measured mounted width, then check the
exact tyre and rim documentation. A calculator never overrides a component limit.

## 2. Read the corner before steering

Look far enough ahead to identify the bend's direction, visible exit, surface,
camber, traffic and escape constraints. Keep scanning rather than fixing your
eyes on one pothole or the rider ahead.

The safe line remains inside your lane and the distance you can see. A wider arc
is not useful if it crosses the centre line, compromises sight distance or moves
into another rider's path. On an unfamiliar bend, enter slowly enough to tighten
or stop if the radius changes.

## 3. Set entry speed while upright

[British Cycling's descending guidance](https://www.britishcycling.org.uk/knowledge/training/article/izn20180117-Get-Started-Descending---Top-10-tips-0)
recommends controlling speed before the corner and using both brakes. Apply them
progressively, reach a conservative entry speed, then release pressure smoothly
as lean increases.

Braking and cornering share the tyre's available grip. That does not mean a rider
must choose to run wide rather than touch a brake; it means entry speed should be
reduced early enough that a rescue decision is unlikely. If the exit closes,
prioritise avoiding traffic, another rider or a barrier and reduce future entry speed.

Advanced trail braking is not a beginner public-road drill. If it is relevant to
racing, learn it with a qualified coach in a controlled closed setting.

## 4. Use a stable body position

There is no one professional pose to copy, but these cues are useful defaults:

- keep the inside pedal up for clearance;
- apply steady pressure through the outside foot;
- keep elbows relaxed rather than locked;
- maintain a secure hand position with immediate brake access;
- keep the head up and vision through the available path;
- let the bike move without abrupt handlebar or body input.

Do not force the inside knee outward or press the outside pedal as hard as possible.
The objective is a stable, repeatable position—not a claim that one pose creates
a quantified increase in grip.

## 5. Understand steering without overcorrecting

A bicycle initiates a turn through steering and lean, often described as
countersteering at speed. Riders do not need an aggressive handlebar jab to make
this work. Use small, smooth inputs and let the bike follow the chosen path.

Do not practise weaving at road speed in traffic. Steering awareness belongs in
a traffic-free skills area with broad lanes, low speed and clear run-off.

## 6. Wet-road cornering

Water, surface texture, tyre compound, tread condition, temperature and
contamination interact. No fixed 30-40 per cent grip reduction predicts the next
bend. [Shimano's road-brake guidance](https://si.shimano.com/en/pdfs/dm/RACBR01/DM-RACBR01-03-ENG.pdf)
and [SRAM's Road AXS manual](https://docs.sram.com/en-US/publications/6TmfV97fHWv8kvGXVegoTy/UM%20-%20Road%20AXS%20and%20XPLR%20AXS?1x-2x-or-xplr-drivetrain-=red-xplr-axs&installation-or-maintenance-=installation&models=rd-red-1e-e1)
both warn that wet stopping takes longer and skidding is easier.

Use a larger margin:

- lower entry speed and increase the gap;
- do most braking early while upright;
- cross unavoidable paint or metal upright and straight;
- avoid sudden line changes around leaves, standing water or oil;
- stop when visibility, flooding, wind or ice makes the route unsuitable.

Do not automatically drop pressure by 5-10 PSI. One [controlled three-tyre test](https://www.bicyclerollingresistance.com/specials/wet-grip-pressure-test)
measured more wet grip at lower test pressure, but it did not establish a safe
universal reduction. Use complete system inputs, obey the tyre-rim range and make
only 1-2 PSI comparisons on a familiar low-risk route.

## 7. A traffic-free practice progression

Choose a flat, clean, traffic-free area with broad turns and clear run-off. Work
with a qualified coach when returning after injury or when balance, vision or
braking confidence is limited.

### Drill 1: vision and line

At walking-to-jogging pace, follow a broad marked arc. Look through the path while
continuing to scan the surface. Hold the same line without increasing speed.

### Drill 2: pedal position

Repeat the broad arc with the inside pedal up and steady outside-foot support.
Keep both hands securely on the controls and elbows relaxed.

### Drill 3: brake-then-turn sequence

Use a wide stopping zone before the arc. Apply both brakes progressively while
upright, release them and make the gentle turn. Change only the braking marker,
not speed and line in the same repetition.

### Drill 4: left and right consistency

Build two broad arcs with ample separation. Repeat both directions at the same
low speed. A side that feels less stable gets more conservative repetition, not
more speed.

Stop if the surface changes, a wheel slips, braking becomes inconsistent, fatigue
reduces control or another person enters the practice area. Do not use a roundabout,
live descent, deliberate skid or maximum-lean test as a drill.

## 8. Common errors and safer corrections

**Looking too close.** Move vision through the available path while continuing to
scan for hazards.

**Late braking.** Start earlier and reduce speed while upright. Do not compensate
with an abrupt lever input.

**Crossing the centre line.** Reduce entry speed and keep the line inside your
lane and sight distance.

**Rigid arms or white-knuckle grip.** Relax the elbows and hands without losing
secure control or brake access.

**Copying another rider.** Their tyre, line of sight, mass, skill and risk margin
are different. Use your own visible stopping distance.

**Changing several variables.** Hold speed, line and equipment constant while
practising one cue.

## 9. Returning after a cornering incident

Inspect the bike and address injury first. When cleared to ride, begin in the
traffic-free low-speed setting and record what is predictable. A near miss is a
reason to step back a stage, not to force another attempt immediately.

Confidence may improve over days or take longer. The useful measure is repeatable
control with a margin, not whether you match a previous speed.

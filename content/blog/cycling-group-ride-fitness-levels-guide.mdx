You know how this goes. Saturday morning, five of you at the car park, bikes out, shoes on. Someone says "easy spin today, yeah?" Everyone nods. Forty minutes later, three riders are drilling it on the front, one is hanging on by a thread, and the fifth turned around ten minutes ago because they couldn't see the group any more.

Nobody wanted that ride. But it happens every single weekend on club runs and friend groups across the country. The fitness gap between the strongest and weakest rider in any casual group is usually enormous — 30, 40, sometimes 50 per cent in FTP terms — and if you don't actively manage it, the group splits.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the solution is not just "ride slower." That makes the strong riders miserable and doesn't actually keep the group together on anything with a gradient. The solution is structural. Route selection, pace management, role assignment, communication, and a set of rules everyone agrees to before the first pedal stroke.

Let me break this down.

## Why Mixed-Ability Groups Fall Apart

The core problem is simple physics. On a flat road, drafting reduces a rider's effort by 27 to 40 per cent. A rider with an FTP of 200 watts sitting in a group can hold 30 kph at maybe 140 watts. The rider with an FTP of 280 watts pulling on the front at the same speed might be doing 220 watts. Both riders are comfortable. The group stays together.

Now put that same group on a 6 per cent gradient. Drafting barely matters on a climb — the speed drops, the aerodynamic savings shrink to almost nothing, and suddenly everyone is riding on their own watts. The 200-watt rider is at threshold. The 280-watt rider is at endurance pace. The group splits in two minutes.

This is why the same group that rolls along happily on flat roads completely falls apart the moment the road goes uphill. It is not a willpower problem. It is not a "just try harder" problem. It is an aerodynamics-and-gradient problem, and you fix it with planning, not motivation.

The second reason groups fall apart is unspoken expectations. Half the group thinks it is a social ride. Half the group thinks it is training. Nobody said which one it was before they left, and those two ride types are fundamentally incompatible if nobody is managing the gap.

## Pace Management: Strong Riders Pull, Weaker Riders Sit In

This is the single most important structural fix for a mixed group, and once you understand it, everything else follows.

The strongest riders in the group should do the most work on the front. Longer pulls, more frequent turns at the front, more time in the wind. The weaker riders should sit in the draft as much as possible, taking shorter pulls or skipping their turn entirely.

This is not charity. Let me be really clear about this.

A strong rider pulling on the front of a group at a moderate pace is getting a seriously good workout. If the group is rolling at 32 kph on the flat, the rider on the front might be pushing 230-250 watts into a headwind while the rider sitting in fourth wheel is doing 160 watts. The strong rider is training. The weaker rider is surviving. Both are getting what they need.

### How to Structure the Rotation

In a mixed group of five or six, you do not need a formal through-and-off rotation. Instead, the two or three strongest riders rotate on the front while the weaker riders hold position in the second half of the group. When a strong rider finishes a pull, they drift back to third or fourth wheel, not to the back of the group. The weaker riders stay tucked in behind.

If a weaker rider wants to take a pull, brilliant — they should, but keep it short. Thirty seconds on the front is enough to contribute. The moment they feel the pace dropping, swing off and let a stronger rider come through. There is no shame in short pulls. The group stays together, and that is the whole point.

### The Half-Wheeling Problem

Here's where it gets really interesting. In mixed groups, half-wheeling is the silent killer. A strong rider moves to the front and unconsciously pushes the pace up by 2 or 3 kph. The rider next to them matches it. Thirty seconds later, the group is 4 kph above the agreed pace and the weakest rider is dangling.

The fix is dead simple: whoever is on the front watches their speed, not their power. Pick a speed — say 30 kph on the flat — and hold it. Not 32. Not 33. Thirty. Power meters and head units make this easy. If the speed agreed before the ride was 30 kph, and your Garmin says 32, you are the problem. Ease off.

## Route Selection: This Matters More Than You Think

I have seen groups spend twenty minutes arguing about pace and then ride a route with four Category 3 climbs. No amount of pace agreement survives a 2 km climb at 8 per cent. The group will split. It has to. The physics demand it.

### Flat and Rolling Routes Keep Groups Together

For a mixed-ability group ride, pick the flattest route you have. Gently rolling is fine — 1 to 2 per cent gradients that last a minute or two are manageable because the group can stay together in formation and the drafting benefit still helps the weaker riders.

What you want to avoid is anything sustained and steep. A 5-minute climb at 6 per cent will blow the group apart every single time. If your local roads are hilly, pick the valleys. Follow the river. Take the coast road. Save the lumpy stuff for training rides with matched groups.

### If You Must Ride Hills

Sometimes there is no flat option. Fair enough. In that case, accept that the group will split on the climbs and plan for it. The rule is straightforward: everyone rides the climb at their own pace, and the group regroups at the top. Not at the bottom of the next descent. At the top.

This is important. If the fast climbers regroup at the bottom of the descent, here is what happens: they crest the climb, bomb the descent, stop at the bottom, and wait. The slow climbers crest two minutes later, descend, and arrive at the bottom to find everyone rested, stretched, and ready to go. The slow climbers get zero recovery time. They arrive already on the back foot and the next flat section starts with them already in the red.

Regroup at the top. The fast climbers wait. Everyone gets a breather. Everyone rolls over the top together. The descent becomes social again. It is a small change that completely transforms the experience for the weaker riders.

## The No-Drop Protocol

A no-drop ride means exactly what it sounds like: nobody gets dropped. The group waits. Nobody is left behind. Nobody finishes the ride alone.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about no-drop rides: they do not enforce themselves. Saying "this is a no-drop ride" at the car park means nothing if nobody is tasked with making it happen. I have been on dozens of rides where "no-drop" was announced and then completely ignored thirty minutes in.

### Assign a Sweeper

The sweeper is a strong rider whose job is to stay with the last person. Not the second-to-last person. The last person. If someone drops off the back, the sweeper drops with them. They pace them back to the group, or they ride with them at whatever speed they can manage until the group regroups.

This has to be a named person. "We'll all keep an eye out" does not work. Everyone thinks someone else is watching. The weakest rider ends up alone on a road they might not know, which is the fastest way to make sure they never come back for another group ride.

The ideal sweeper is the strongest rider in the group, or close to it. They need enough fitness in reserve that riding at the back costs them nothing and they can still enjoy the ride. It is actually a brilliant role for someone who wants an easy day — you are socially engaged, you are helping someone, and you barely touch Zone 3.

### How No-Drop Works in Practice

The rules are simple:

1. **Climbs:** Everyone rides at their own pace. The group waits at the top. Nobody descends until the last rider crests.
2. **Junctions:** The front of the group stops at every junction and waits for the back. This also solves the navigation problem — nobody misses a turn.
3. **Flats:** The front riders control the pace. If a gap opens, they soft-pedal until the group reforms. Nobody is "off the back" for more than a minute.
4. **The sweeper stays at the back.** Always. If the sweeper is at the front, something has gone wrong.

## Communication: Before the Ride and During It

Communication before the ride is more important than communication during it. By the time you are on the road, it is too late to sort out mismatched expectations.

### The Pre-Ride Conversation

Before anyone clips in, three things need to be agreed:

**What kind of ride is this?** Social, training, or somewhere in between. This is not a trick question, but it needs a real answer. "Bit of both" is where problems start. If it is social, the weakest rider sets the upper limit. If it is training, the group needs to be fitness-matched.

**What is the target pace?** A number, not a feeling. "Easy" means different things to a 200-watt rider and a 300-watt rider. Thirty kph on the flat, 25 kph average, whatever it is — put a number on it.

**Where are the regroup points?** Name them. The top of Ditchling, the cafe at the crossroads, the layby after the dual carriageway. If everyone knows the regroup points in advance, the group can split temporarily on hills without anyone panicking about being lost.

### On-the-Road Communication

Once you are rolling, keep it simple:

- **"Ease up"** — someone is dropping off the back. The front riders need to soft-pedal.
- **"Regroup"** — the group is stretching. Stop and wait.
- **"Car back" / "car up"** — traffic warning. Standard stuff.
- **"Single out"** — narrow road, get into single file.
- **"On your right/left"** — a rider is coming past. Always announce.

The most important phrase in mixed-group riding is "ease up." It should be called by anyone who notices the group stretching, not just the sweeper. If you can see the last rider losing contact, call it. Do not wait until they are 200 metres back and cooked.

## When to Split Into Two Groups

Let me be really clear about this: sometimes one group does not work. And that is fine.

If the FTP gap between the strongest and weakest rider is larger than roughly 30 per cent, keeping everyone together on anything other than dead-flat roads becomes a full-time management exercise. The strong riders are bored. The weak riders are suffering. Nobody is having the ride they wanted.

The better option is to split into two groups for the ride and regroup at a cafe stop.

### How to Split Without Making It Weird

This is the delicate bit. Nobody wants to be in "the slow group." But the framing matters enormously.

Do not say: "Fast group and slow group." That is guaranteed to make someone feel rubbish.

Do say: "We'll split for the first hour and meet at the cafe at eleven. Group A is going left up the ridge, Group B is taking the valley road. Same distance, different terrain."

The second version is the same thing — one group is riding harder than the other — but it does not label anyone. Different routes, different terrain, same destination. Everyone gets a good ride.

Another option that works well for clubs: staggered starts. The slower group leaves ten minutes before the faster group on the same route. The faster group catches up organically, and the last 30 minutes are ridden together. This gives the slower riders a proper ride without the pressure of holding on, and the faster riders get to work hard without guilt.

### Group Size Matters

Even with well-matched fitness, groups larger than eight riders become hard to manage on public roads. You are too long for junctions, too wide for narrow lanes, and communication from front to back breaks down.

For mixed-ability rides, six is the sweet spot. Four strong riders and two weaker ones, or three and three. Any more than that and splitting into two groups is better for everyone regardless of fitness.

## The Organiser Sets the Tone

Whoever organises the ride owns the culture of that ride. Full stop.

If the organiser says "no-drop social ride" and then attacks the first hill at threshold, the message is clear: the words meant nothing. Every rider in the group just learned that pace agreements are optional and the strong riders will ride how they want.

The organiser needs to ride what they said. If it is a social ride, the organiser rides at social pace. If they want to train, they organise a training ride and invite appropriately.

This is especially true for club rides. The ride leader is not just navigating — they are setting the culture. A club where the Saturday ride consistently drops new riders is a club that stops getting new riders. A club where the ride leader actually holds the group together is a club that grows.

### Leading From the Middle

A good ride organiser does not ride on the front. They ride in the middle of the group where they can see both the front and the back. From the middle, you can call "ease up" to the front riders when the back is stretching, and you can drop back to check on a struggling rider without losing contact with the group.

If you are organising a mixed ride, put your strongest rider on the front to set the pace, put another strong rider as the sweeper on the back, and ride in the middle yourself. You are the connective tissue. You keep the group together.

## Integrating New Riders

New riders are the lifeblood of any cycling group. They are also the most likely to be dropped, to feel excluded, and to never come back. How you handle a new rider on their first group ride determines whether your group grows or stagnates.

### The First Ride Protocol

When someone new joins the group, assign a buddy. Not the sweeper — a buddy. Someone who rides next to the new rider, talks to them, explains what is coming up on the route, tells them about the regroup points, and makes sure they know the hand signals.

The buddy should be a competent but not intimidating rider. The goal is to make the new rider feel looked after, not chaperoned. Most new riders are anxious about holding the group up. Having someone next to them who says "you're doing great, we regroup at the top of this hill" makes an enormous difference.

Here is what kills a new rider's enthusiasm faster than anything: getting dropped on the first ride and riding the last 45 minutes alone. If your group lets that happen, you have lost that rider. They will not come back.

### Adjusting for Unknown Fitness

You do not know how fit a new rider is until you ride with them. Err on the side of easier. Pick the flattest route option. Keep the pace 2 or 3 kph below normal for the first 30 minutes. Once you can see where the new rider sits in the group, you can adjust.

If the new rider turns out to be strong — great. They will self-select into the front group next time. If they are at the back, you already have the sweeper and the no-drop protocol in place. Either way, their first ride was a good experience.

## Making It Work Every Week

The groups that get this right are the groups that treat it as a system, not a series of one-off decisions. Same structure every week. Same expectations. Same roles.

### The Weekly Template

- **Monday or Tuesday:** Someone posts the ride details. Route, distance, pace, meeting point, time. Not "we'll figure it out when we get there." Actual details.
- **Before the ride:** Quick reminder of the pace agreement and the regroup points. Assign the sweeper.
- **During the ride:** Ride the plan. Control the pace. Regroup where you said you would.
- **After the ride:** Coffee. This is non-negotiable. The cafe stop is where the social bonds form, and those bonds are what bring people back next week.

### When Someone Is Getting Stronger

The good news about mixed-ability group rides: they are temporary. Weaker riders get stronger. The rider who was clinging on at the back in March is pulling on the front by July. If you are that rider right now, sitting in the draft and wondering if you will ever be able to take a proper pull — you will. Fitness is trainable. The group ride itself is the training.

And if you are the strong rider doing all the pulling, remember that you were once the weakest rider in some group somewhere. Someone kept the pace down for you. Someone waited at the top of a climb. Now it is your turn.

## The Real Point

Mixed-ability group riding is a solved problem. It just requires someone to actually solve it instead of hoping it sorts itself out. Route selection, pace management, a sweeper, regroup rules, and one pre-ride conversation. That is it. Five things.

The ride where everyone finishes together, nobody got dropped, and the strong riders still got a workout — that is the best ride. Better than any solo effort, better than any Strava segment. Five mates arriving at the cafe together, everyone knackered in their own way, everyone happy.

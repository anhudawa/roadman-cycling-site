There are sportives that test your fitness. There are sportives that test your endurance. And then there is the Tour de Mont Blanc — a 330km loop around the highest peak in Western Europe that tests both at the same time, across three countries, over roughly 8,000 metres of climbing, in a single day that most riders finish in darkness.

The route circles the Mont Blanc massif. You start in France, cross into Italy over the Col du Petit Saint-Bernard, ride through the Aosta Valley, climb into Switzerland via the Col de la Forclaz, and return to France through the Col des Montets before the final push over the Col des Saisies and back to base. Three borders. Six or seven major cols depending on the year's route variation. Somewhere between 14 and 18 hours in the saddle for most finishers, with the strong riders coming home in 12-14 hours and the rest navigating the final climbs by headlamp.

This is not a long sportive. This is an ultra-distance alpine event. The distinction matters because it changes how you train, how you fuel, how you pace, and how you think about the day. A sportive asks you to ride hard for 4-7 hours. The Tour de Mont Blanc asks you to ride steadily for 12-18 hours across terrain that never lets up. The riders who finish well are not necessarily the fittest. They are the ones who paced the first 200km like it was a warm-up, ate before they were hungry, and arrived at the final cols with enough left to turn the pedals when everything in their body was asking them to stop.

Here is how to prepare for it across 16 weeks.

## Key Takeaways

- 330km and roughly 8,000m of climbing across France, Italy, and Switzerland — one of Europe's hardest single-day events
- 16-week build peaking at 12-15 hours/week with long rides of 7-8 hours
- 3.0 W/kg minimum for time-limit completion, 3.5+ W/kg for a strong ride
- 60-90g carbs per hour from the first kilometre, every kilometre
- Night riding is likely — train with lights at least twice before the event
- Sub-compact gearing (46/30 or 48/31) earns its place on the late cols
- One 7-8 hour over-distance ride with 4,000m+ of climbing before the taper

## Course Profile and Key Sections

The Tour de Mont Blanc is a loop. You leave and return to the same point, and in between you cross three national borders and climb everything the Mont Blanc massif puts in your way. Here is what the route looks like in sections.

**Col des Saisies (km 40-60).** 1,650m altitude. The opening climb. Around 17km at 5-6% average gradient — steady, not vicious, but long enough that you feel it if you ride it too hard. The temptation is to push here because the legs are fresh and everyone around you is riding at tempo. Resist it. This is a 330km day. The Col des Saisies is the warm-up. Ride it at low Zone 2 and let the nervous starters go.

**Cormet de Roselend (km 75-95).** 1,968m. One of the most beautiful climbs in the French Alps. Around 20km of climbing with sections of 7-8%. The road is narrow in places and the descent towards Bourg-Saint-Maurice is fast and technical. This is where the route character announces itself — proper alpine terrain, proper gradients, and the first real test of climbing legs.

**Col du Petit Saint-Bernard (km 110-140).** 2,188m. The border crossing into Italy. A long, grinding ascent of roughly 25km at 5-6% average with a few kicks of 8-9% in the steeper sections. The summit puts you in the Aosta Valley and the riding changes — Italian mountain roads, different signage, different rhythm. The descent into La Thuile is smooth and fast. Most riders reach the Petit Saint-Bernard around hours 5-7 depending on pace, and this is where the day starts separating the prepared from the hopeful.

**Aosta Valley transit (km 140-200).** The Italian valley section. Not flat — rolling terrain with 500-800m of climbing spread across 60km — but after the high cols it feels like respite. Here's where it gets really interesting: this section is a fuelling opportunity, not a rest stop. Eat aggressively, drink constantly, and ride the valley at genuine endurance pace. Riders who race the valley to "make time" arrive at the Swiss border empty.

**Grand Col du Saint-Bernard approach and Col de la Forclaz (km 200-250).** The Swiss section. The Col de la Forclaz sits at 1,527m — not the highest col of the day, but it arrives at km 230+ on legs that have already done 200km and 5,000-6,000m of climbing. Around 13km at 8% average. This gradient on fresh legs is routine. At this point in the day, 8% feels like a wall. Pacing here is about survival arithmetic — what wattage can you hold for 60-90 minutes without cracking? Use the [climb time calculator](/tools/climb-time) to model your projected ascent time at different power outputs and see what finishing inside the time limit actually requires.

**Col des Montets (km 255-270).** 1,461m. The re-entry into France through Chamonix. A shorter climb — around 4km at 5-6% — but by now the cumulative fatigue turns modest gradients into significant efforts. The Chamonix valley below is one of the most recognisable landscapes in European cycling, and most riders will pass through it in fading light or darkness.

**Final climb and return (km 270-330).** The closing section climbs back over the Col des Saisies or a variation route to return to the start. This final climb is the day's crux — not because of gradient (it is the same 5-6% you climbed 14 hours earlier) but because of what precedes it. 270km. 7,000m+ of climbing. 12-16 hours in the saddle. The legs that spun up the Col des Saisies at dawn now grind the same road at night. This is where the event is won or lost, and it is won by the riders who still have fuel in the tank and a pace plan they have not abandoned.

## Fitness Benchmarks

Three numbers tell you where you stand.

| Benchmark | Completion (time limit) | Comfortable finish | Strong ride |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FTP (W/kg)** | 3.0 | 3.3-3.5 | 3.8+ |
| **Finish time** | 16-18 hours | 14-16 hours | 12-14 hours |
| **Weekly training peak** | 10-12 hours | 12-15 hours | 14-16 hours |
| **Longest training ride** | 6-7 hours | 7-8 hours | 8-9 hours |
| **Climbing volume (peak week)** | 3,000m | 4,000m | 5,000m+ |

Use the [W/kg calculator](/tools/wkg) to set your number and the [race predictor](/tools/race-predictor) to see where your current fitness lands across a 330km day with 8,000m of elevation gain.

Here's the good news: 3.0 W/kg is achievable for most riders with 12-18 months of consistent training behind them. The Tour de Mont Blanc is not a race where you need elite numbers. It is a race where you need adequate numbers paired with flawless execution — pacing, fuelling, kit management, and the mental discipline to ride your own ride for 14-18 hours. For context on where it sits in the alpine landscape, compare the benchmarks for the [Otztaler Radmarathon](/blog/otztaler-radmarathon-training-guide) (3.4 W/kg floor, 238km, 5,500m) and [La Marmotte](/blog/la-marmotte-training-guide) (3.2 W/kg floor, 174km, 5,000m). The Tour de Mont Blanc demands less peak power than the Otztaler but significantly more endurance — the extra 100km and 2,500m of climbing change the nature of the event from a hard sportive to a genuine ultra.

## 16-Week Training Plan Overview

Four phases. Each builds on the last. Volumes assume a starting base of 8-10 hours per week with regular climbing.

### Weeks 1-4: Base

Volume: 8-10 hours/week.

Four to five rides per week, with 80% in Zone 2. The aerobic engine is the foundation for everything that follows. Stephen Seiler's polarised research is the framework — easy rides stay properly easy, which is harder than it sounds when you know what's coming in 12 weeks.

**Anchor session:** Long Zone 2 ride building from 3.5 hours in week 1 to 5 hours in week 4, with 1,000-1,500m of climbing in the back half. This is the ride that teaches your body to burn fat efficiently at low intensity, and that metabolic efficiency is what keeps you riding at hour 14 when the glycogen is long gone.

**Second session:** One tempo ride per week. 3x15 minutes at 76-82% FTP with 5-minute recoveries. Not hard. Not easy. The effort that sits just below where you start breathing heavy — the pace you will ride the valleys at on race day.

**Off-bike:** Two sessions per week. Core work — planks (front and side, 3x45 seconds), dead bugs (3x12 per side), bird dogs (3x10 per side). Single-leg strength — walking lunges (3x12 per leg), step-ups onto a bench (3x10 per leg), single-leg press (3x12). Build the hip and core stability that prevents your pedal stroke from falling apart at hour 12.

### Weeks 5-9: Build

Volume: 10-13 hours/week.

Two quality sessions per week now. The volume climbs and the specificity sharpens.

**Session one: Sweet spot.** 3x15 minutes at 88-93% FTP in week 5, building to 2x25 minutes by week 9. This is the bread-and-butter intensity for ultra-distance preparation — hard enough to raise threshold, easy enough to recover from in 48 hours. If you can hold 90% FTP for 25 minutes at the end of a 90-minute ride, you are building the engine the Tour de Mont Blanc demands.

**Session two: Sustained climbing.** On a real gradient if possible. 4x12 minutes at 82-88% FTP in week 5, building to 3x20 minutes by week 9. Cadence at 65-75 RPM — the cadence you will actually climb at after 8 hours, not the 90 RPM you use on fresh legs. Use the [VAM calculator](/tools/vam) to track your vertical metres per hour and see whether your climbing rate is on target for the major cols.

**Long ride:** Building from 5 hours in week 5 to 6.5 hours in week 9, with 2,000-3,000m of climbing. Include at least one sustained climb of 45+ minutes per long ride. Feed exactly as you will on race day — same products, same timing, same quantities. Gut training starts now, not in the taper.

**Off-bike:** Continue core and single-leg work. Add Bulgarian split squats (3x10 per leg) with dumbbells. The single-leg emphasis matters because cycling is a single-leg sport disguised as a bilateral one, and the hip stability from split squats and lunges prevents the lateral knee drift that causes pain after 6 hours.

### Weeks 10-13: Sharpen

Volume: 12-15 hours/week. This is the peak block.

**Session one: Threshold intervals on gradient.** 3x12 minutes at 95-100% FTP on a climb, with 5-minute descents as recovery. The specific stimulus here is holding threshold power on gradient when your heart rate is elevated and your breathing is heavy — the exact sensation of the Col de la Forclaz at km 230.

**Session two: Tempo endurance.** A 3-4 hour ride at 70-78% FTP with minimal stops. This is race-day intensity — the pace you will hold through the Aosta Valley and on the lower slopes of every col. It should feel boring. If it feels hard, the number is too high.

**The over-distance ride (week 11 or 12).** This is the session that separates riders who finish confidently from riders who finish guessing. One ride of 7-8 hours with 4,000m+ of climbing. Full fuelling rehearsal. Night riding practice in the final 60-90 minutes if possible. This ride answers the question "what happens to my body after hour 6?" — and the answer needs to come in training, not on the road between Chamonix and the Col des Saisies at 22:00.

**Night riding session (week 10 or 11).** Ride the last 2 hours of a long ride in darkness with your race lights. The handling is different. The perception of gradient changes. The cold arrives faster. And the mental state of riding a climb you cannot see the top of is something you should experience exactly once in training so it is not a surprise on race day.

**Alpine simulation weekend (week 12, if possible).** Back-to-back climbing days. Saturday 6-7 hours with 3,500m of climbing. Sunday 3-4 hours rolling. This weekend tests recovery, fuelling, and fatigue management across two days — not the same as the event, but the closest single-weekend analogue. If you are training for both the Tour de Mont Blanc and a multi-day event like the [Haute Route Alps](/blog/haute-route-alps-training-guide), this back-to-back weekend serves both targets.

### Weeks 14-16: Taper

Volume drops. Sharpness stays.

**Week 14:** 10-11 hours. Two rides with 10-minute openers at threshold. One long ride of 4-5 hours at Zone 2 with 1,500m of climbing. The fitness is in — this week protects it.

**Week 15:** 8-9 hours. One sweet-spot session (2x15 minutes at 90% FTP). One 3-hour Zone 2 ride. The rest is recovery, sleep, and carb-loading preparation.

**Week 16 (race week):** 4-5 hours total. Two short rides of 60-90 minutes with 5-minute openers at threshold. Legs should feel restless by Thursday. If they don't, you tapered too late. Use the [training load calculator](/tools/training-load) to confirm your CTL has held steady through the taper while ATL has dropped — that widening gap between the two is freshness.

Travel to the start 3-4 days before the event. Pre-ride 20-30km of the opening section at easy pace. Dial gearing under load on a local gradient. Check lights, charge batteries, lay out race-day kit. The preparation is done. Trust it.

## Key Sessions in Detail

**The sweet-spot block.** 2x25 minutes at 88-93% FTP with 8 minutes between efforts. This session builds the sustained-power capacity for the cols. The intensity sits just below threshold — hard enough to stress the system, recoverable enough to do twice a week during the build phase. Do it on a climb when you can. The neuromuscular pattern of holding 90% FTP at 65 RPM on gradient is different from holding it at 90 RPM on a flat road.

**The tempo endurance ride.** 3-4 hours at 70-78% FTP on rolling terrain. No stops except to refill bottles. This is the pace of the Aosta Valley, the pace of the false flats between cols, the pace you default to when the gradient is 3-4% and the legs are tired. It should feel controlled and sustainable. If it feels easy at hour 1 and hard at hour 3, you have found the right number. Track the session with the [TSS calculator](/tools/tss) to monitor training load across the build.

**Night riding practice.** Start a 4-5 hour ride at 16:00 in autumn or early spring so the final 90 minutes are in full darkness. Use the lights you will race with. Ride roads you know well, then ride a section you know less well. The point is not to simulate danger — it is to normalise the sensation of climbing and descending with limited visibility. The Tour de Mont Blanc's final 60-100km will happen in darkness for most finishers. That needs to feel familiar, not frightening.

**Alpine simulation.** If you can get to the Alps, Pyrenees, or Dolomites for a weekend during weeks 11-12, ride two back-to-back days with 3,000-4,000m of climbing on day one and 1,500-2,000m on day two. If you cannot travel, stack your local climbs — ride the biggest hill you have six times if necessary. The point is time on gradient with heavy legs, not altitude.

## Nutrition Strategy

The Tour de Mont Blanc is a 12-18 hour event. The fuelling plan is not an afterthought — it is the plan. Most DNFs on ultra-distance events are nutrition failures dressed up as fitness failures. The rider who bonks at km 250 does not lack fitness. They lack 4,000 calories they should have eaten in the preceding 10 hours.

| Time period | Carbs per hour | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Hours 1-4** | 60-80g | Drink mix (40g) + one gel or bar per hour. Establish rhythm. |
| **Hours 5-8** | 70-90g | Add solid food at aid stations — rice cakes, sandwiches, pastry. Keep drink mix constant. |
| **Hours 9-12** | 60-80g | Shift towards gels and liquid calories as gut slows. Solid food if tolerated. |
| **Hours 12+** | 50-70g | Gels, cola at aid stations, caffeine. Force-feed if necessary. Anything that goes down stays down. |

**Total intake across a 16-hour day:** 5,000-7,000 calories. 900-1,200g of carbohydrate. That is somewhere between 30 and 40 gels' worth of carbs — obviously not delivered as gels alone, but the number illustrates the scale of the task.

**Gut training.** Asker Jeukendrup's research on multiple transportable carbohydrates established that the gut adapts to higher intake rates with practice. A 2:1 glucose-fructose blend allows absorption rates above 60g/hour. But the gut only handles race-day intake if you have trained it during at least 4-5 long rides in the build phase. Start practising at 60g/hour in week 5 and build to 80-90g/hour by week 12.

**Solid food strategy.** After hour 6, pure gels start to taste like punishment. Plan for solid food at every aid station — French mountain aid stations typically offer bread, cheese, fruit, dried sausage, and cake. Italian aid stations add pasta and rice. Eat what appeals. Palatability matters at hour 10 in a way it does not at hour 2.

**Caffeine.** Save it for the second half. A caffeine gel at the base of the Col de la Forclaz (around hour 10-12) sharpens focus for the hardest climbing section. Another caffeine hit for the final climb. Do not front-load caffeine — it loses its edge if you have been dosing all day.

**Pre-race carb-loading.** 8-10g per kg of body weight in the 36 hours before the start. A 75kg rider eats 600-750g of carbs across the day and evening before. This is more food than feels comfortable. Eat it anyway. Use the [fuel planner](/tools/fuel-planner) to map your intake plan hour by hour and the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) to set fluid targets for the altitude and temperature conditions.

**Hydration.** 500-750ml per hour depending on temperature and altitude. Salt tabs in every bottle. Cold summit descents reduce thirst but not fluid loss — drink on schedule, not on thirst.

## Equipment and Gear

**Gearing.** Let me break this down. A standard compact (50/34) with an 11-28 cassette is fine for most sportives. The Tour de Mont Blanc is not most sportives. After 250km and 6,000m of climbing, your legs cannot produce the torque they produced at km 50. A 34x32 is the minimum. A 34x34 is sensible. And a sub-compact crankset — 48/31 or 46/30 — is what experienced ultra-distance riders choose for this event. The ability to spin at 65 RPM on a 7% gradient at hour 14 is worth more than any benefit from a larger chainring on the valley sections. Use the [gear ratio calculator](/tools/gear-ratio) to compare cassette and chainring combinations at different gradients and cadences.

**Lights.** Mandatory. Most finishers ride the final 2-4 hours in darkness. Front light: minimum 600 lumens with a 6+ hour battery life on medium setting. Carry a second smaller light as backup. Rear light: mandatory for safety on Alpine descents in darkness. Charge everything the night before. Do not rely on a single light source for a ride that may have you descending the Col des Montets at 23:00.

**Alpine kit layers.** The temperature range on this ride is extraordinary. The valley floor at midday might be 25-30°C. The Col du Petit Saint-Bernard summit at 2,188m might be 8-10°C. The Col de la Forclaz in late afternoon at 1,527m might be 12-15°C. The final climb at night might be 5-8°C. You need: arm warmers, a packable gilet, a lightweight rain jacket, and long-finger gloves stashed for the high-col descents. Use the [wind chill calculator](/tools/wind-chill) to understand what a 2,000m summit at 8°C feels like at 55 km/h descent speed — the answer is close to freezing.

**Tyres.** 28mm minimum, 30-32mm if your frame clears them. Tubeless with sealant. The route crosses three countries with varying road surfaces — French mountain roads can be rough, Italian valley roads are generally smooth, Swiss roads are immaculate. A wider tyre absorbs the rough sections without slowing you on the smooth ones, and at hour 14, comfort is speed.

**Saddle and chamois.** Test everything on a 7-8 hour ride before the event. Saddle sores that appear at hour 3 become open wounds at hour 14. Chamois cream applied at the start and reapplied at the halfway point. Carry a small sachet.

**Bags and storage.** A small top-tube bag for gels and a phone. A saddle bag for tools, tube, CO2. Jersey pockets for food rotation at aid stations. Keep the bike light but carry enough that a mechanical at km 280 is a 15-minute stop, not a race-ending phone call.

## Race Day Pacing Strategy

The Tour de Mont Blanc punishes early aggression more than almost any other event. 330km is long enough that a 10-watt overshoot in the first 100km costs you 30 watts of capacity in the final 100km. Here is how to split the day.

**Kilometres 0-100 (hours 1-4).** Ride at 60-68% FTP. This feels slow. It is supposed to feel slow. The Col des Saisies and Cormet de Roselend are the warm-up, not the race. Stay in the bottom half of Zone 2 on the climbs. Eat and drink on schedule. Let faster riders go — they are either genuinely faster than you (in which case they would go later anyway) or they are riding above sustainable pace (in which case you will pass them at km 250).

**Kilometres 100-200 (hours 4-8).** The Col du Petit Saint-Bernard and the Aosta Valley. Ride the Petit Saint-Bernard at 65-72% FTP — this is a 25km climb and it needs to feel controlled, not contested. The Aosta Valley transit is your fuelling window: eat aggressively, drink constantly, and hold 65-70% FTP on the rolling terrain. Do not race the valley.

**Kilometres 200-270 (hours 8-13).** The Col de la Forclaz and Col des Montets. This is where the event begins. Every watt you saved in the first 200km is available here. Ride the Forclaz at 70-78% FTP — the hardest sustainable effort of the day, not the hardest effort you can produce. The Col des Montets is shorter but arrives on destroyed legs. Pace it on power, not feeling. By now, your perceived effort will be 8/10 at what is actually 70% FTP. Trust the numbers.

**Kilometres 270-330 (hours 13-18).** The final climb and return. Ride at whatever wattage you can sustain without stopping. This is not a pacing problem — it is a willpower problem solved by fuelling. Eat a caffeine gel at the base. Take 200ml of cola at any aid station. Spin. Do not look at the summit. Look at the next kilometre marker.

## Common Mistakes

**Underfuelling the first 100km.** The most common mistake on ultra-distance events, and the least fixable. Riders skip eating in the first 3-4 hours because they "don't feel hungry yet." By the time they feel the deficit, they are 2,000 calories behind and no amount of gel-stuffing will close the gap. Eat from kilometre one. 60-80g of carbs per hour, every hour, no exceptions.

**Going too hard on the early cols.** The Col des Saisies and Cormet de Roselend are beautiful climbs on fresh legs in the morning light. The adrenaline of the start and the speed of the riders around you push the pace above sustainable. A 3.5 W/kg rider who climbs the Saisies at 3.2 W/kg has 0.3 W/kg in the bank for the Forclaz 10 hours later. A 3.5 W/kg rider who climbs the Saisies at 3.5 W/kg has nothing left. The arithmetic is that simple.

**Not training long enough rides.** If your longest ride before the Tour de Mont Blanc is 5 hours, you are guessing about hours 6-18. The guess is almost always wrong. One ride of 7-8 hours with 4,000m+ of climbing — with full fuelling rehearsal — is the minimum. Riders who skip it because they "don't have time for an 8-hour ride" are the riders who DNF at km 260 because they did not know what their body does after hour 10.

**Ignoring night riding preparation.** The final 60-100km will happen in darkness for most finishers. Descending an Alpine col at 50 km/h with a front light you have never used before is dangerous. Climbing a gradient you cannot see the top of, in silence, with fatigued legs, is psychologically brutal if you have never experienced it. Two training rides that finish in darkness — one including a descent — removes the surprise.

**Wrong gearing.** A 50/34 with an 11-28 cassette produces a lowest gear of 34/28. On a 7% gradient after 250km, that means grinding at 45-50 RPM unless you still have 3.5+ W/kg available. Most riders do not. A 34x32 or 34x34 adds 10-15 RPM at the same power, which is the difference between spinning to the summit and walking to it. Test your gearing on a steep local climb at the end of a long ride. If you are below 60 RPM in your easiest gear, change the cassette before you book flights.

## Travel and Logistics

**Getting there.** Geneva is the closest major airport, roughly 80km from the typical start location. Chamonix and the surrounding villages are also accessible via the Mont Blanc Tunnel from Italy. Most international riders fly into Geneva and hire a car.

**Bike transport.** Bike box or bike bag on the flight. Most local bike shops in the Chamonix valley offer assembly and basic mechanical support, but arrive 2-3 days early and build the bike yourself with time to ride 30-40km and check everything under load.

**Accommodation.** Book early. The event fills the local villages. Chamonix, Megève, and the villages around the Col des Saisies are the most common bases. A hotel with secure bike storage, breakfast included, and proximity to the start line is worth the premium. The night before the event, you need sleep — not logistics.

**Registration.** Typically opens 4-6 months before the event. Entry is around EUR 120-150 depending on the year. Registration usually includes feed stations, timing, and event support. Read the technical requirements carefully — lights are mandatory, minimum equipment lists are enforced, and medical certificates may be required.

**Pre-ride.** Ride 30-40km of the opening section 2-3 days before the event. Feel the gradients under your gearing. Check descents for road surface. Identify the first aid station location. This ride is reconnaissance, not training — ride at Zone 1 and be back for lunch.

## Recovery Plan

A 330km ride with 8,000m of climbing produces muscle damage, glycogen depletion, and systemic fatigue that takes 7-10 days to clear properly.

**Day of finish.** Recovery shake within 30 minutes of crossing the line — 30-40g protein, 60-80g carbs. Real meal within 90 minutes. Rehydrate with electrolytes. Elevate legs for 20-30 minutes. Sleep is the priority — ignore the post-event party if you have to. The body repairs during sleep, and the repair bill after 330km is significant.

**Days 1-3.** No riding. Walk for 20-30 minutes daily. Gentle stretching. Eat at maintenance calories with high carbs and protein. Compression garments if you use them. Do not test the legs on a bike — the delayed muscle soreness peaks at 48-72 hours and riding into it extends recovery.

**Days 4-7.** Easy spinning — 30-60 minutes at Zone 1. No climbing, no intensity. The legs will feel surprisingly good on day 5, and the temptation is to test them. Resist it. The muscular system recovers faster than the connective tissue, and a hard ride on day 6 risks the tendon and ligament niggles that turn a great achievement into a month off the bike.

**Days 8-10.** Resume normal easy riding. Introduce 10-15 minute tempo efforts on day 9-10 if the legs respond. If they do not, extend the recovery by another 3-4 days. There is no rush. You just rode 330km around Mont Blanc. The fitness is banked. Let the body absorb it.

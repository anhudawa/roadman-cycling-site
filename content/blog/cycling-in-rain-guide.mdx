Rain does not automatically make a ride unsafe, but it changes visibility, grip,
braking, temperature management and the consequences of poor line choice. Check
the forecast and route first. Flooding, ice, lightning, high wind or inadequate
visibility are valid reasons to move the session indoors or stop.

When conditions are suitable, the aim is not to reproduce dry speed. It is to
use compatible equipment, preserve a larger margin and return with a bike that
has been inspected after the wet ride.

## The Kit That Actually Matters

Let us start with what to wear, because getting cold and wet is miserable, but getting warm and wet is actually fine.

### Waterproof Jacket

Look for a jacket rated for the expected rain, with enough breathability and
ventilation for your effort, a fit that does not obstruct control and room for
the required layers. Test reach to the bars and rear-pocket access before a long
ride; a membrane label alone does not establish comfort for every temperature.

Use the lights and reflective equipment required by local rules and choose
contrasting, reflective details where practical. Visibility aids do not replace
lawful road position, speed and attention from every road user.

### Overshoes

Overshoes can reduce water and wind exposure, but fit, seams, temperature and
ride duration change the result. Check that they do not obstruct cleat engagement.

Carry a spare pair of socks in your back pocket for longer rides. Sometimes nothing keeps the water out entirely, and dry socks at the halfway point can transform your mood.

### Mudguards

Full-length mudguards can reduce road spray onto you, the bicycle and following
riders. Confirm tyre clearance, secure every fixing and recheck for rubbing or
movement after installation.

### Other Essentials

- **Cycling cap under your helmet**: The peak keeps rain out of your eyes. Simple and effective.
- **Full-finger gloves**: Wet hands get cold fast. Neoprene or waterproof gloves maintain grip and warmth.
- **Base layer**: Choose a moisture-managing layer appropriate to temperature and effort. Merino and suitable synthetics can both work; carry an extra layer when a mechanical stop could leave you exposed.
- **Clear or light-tinted glasses**: Dark lenses in rain are a visibility hazard. Clear lenses protect your eyes from spray without dimming your vision.

## Tyre Pressure Adjustments

Pressure can affect wet grip, comfort and tyre support, but rain does not create one correct PSI reduction. In a controlled test of three 28mm road tyres, [Bicycle Rolling Resistance measured slightly more wet grip at lower test pressures](https://www.bicyclerollingresistance.com/specials/wet-grip-pressure-test). That result does not prove that every rider should remove 5-10 PSI: the test used one wheel load, rim, surface and speed, while real systems differ in width, casing, load and permitted range.

Use the [Roadman road-bike tyre pressure calculator](/tools/tyre-pressure) to establish a reproducible starting point from complete rider-plus-bike weight, measured mounted width and surface. Check the exact tyre and rim documentation before riding: [SRAM's official guidance](https://www.sram.com/en/zipp/learn/how-to-calculate-tire-pressure), for example, asks for the riding condition as part of a complete setup rather than prescribing one blanket reduction.

If you want to compare a wet setting, change front and rear by only 1-2 PSI, repeat a familiar low-risk route and record confidence, harshness and support. Stay inside the higher minimum and lower maximum published for the tyre-rim pair. Stop lowering if the tyre squirms, bottoms, burps or feels unsupported. A grippier tyre compound, sound tread, slower entry speed and smooth steering or braking inputs can matter more than a pressure change.

## Cornering in the Wet

Wet grip varies with the tyre, surface, temperature and contamination. Preserve
enough margin to change the plan when a corner is worse than it first appears.

**Reduce speed before the corner.** Do most braking early and progressively while
upright, then reduce brake demand smoothly as lean increases.

**Reduce lean demand.** Enter more slowly, choose a smooth lawful line inside
your lane, keep the inside pedal up and weight the outside foot. Do not cross the
centre line or compromise sight distance to make a corner wider.

**Treat paint and metal cautiously.** Avoid them without swerving into traffic.
If one cannot be avoided, slow first and cross upright and straight with smooth inputs.

**Look where you want to go**. This applies in all conditions but is especially important in the wet when anxiety makes you fixate on hazards rather than your line through the corner.

**Give yourself more room.** Increase the gap, lower the entry speed and prioritise
the visible road over a segment or rider ahead.

## Braking Technique

Wet braking is fundamentally different from dry braking, and the difference matters whether you are on rim brakes or disc brakes.

**Disc brakes**: Moving the braking surface away from the rim can improve
consistency, but wet stopping still depends on tyre grip, brake condition, speed,
load and terrain. Start earlier and test the response gently at low speed.

**Rim brakes**: Water and contamination can delay the initial response. Follow
the wheel and brake makers' wet-weather instructions; light early contact can
help clear the braking surface, but continuous dragging can create heat and wear.

With either system, use both brakes progressively, keep the wheels rolling and
avoid abrupt lever input. No fixed front-to-rear split applies to every gradient,
load and surface.

## Visibility: Be Seen

Wet weather reduces driver visibility dramatically. You need to take active responsibility for being seen.

- **Front and rear lights on**, even during the day. Flashing mode is more attention-grabbing.
- **Bright clothing**. Fluorescent yellow, orange, or pink. Not black. Not dark blue.
- **Reflective elements**. Ankle reflectors are particularly effective because the up-and-down pedalling motion catches the eye of drivers.

## Post-Ride Bike Maintenance

Riding in the rain is fine. Leaving your bike wet and dirty afterwards is not. Water carries grit into every bearing, cable, and pivot point on your bike. Road spray contains salts, oils, and debris that accelerate wear.

After every wet ride:

1. **Wipe down the frame** with a damp cloth.
2. **Dry and lubricate the chain**. Wet lube after wet rides. Read our [chain lube guide](/blog/cycling-chain-lube-guide) for specifics.
3. **Check brake pads** for embedded grit.
4. **Bounce the wheels and listen for grinding** in the hubs.
5. **Leave the bike somewhere it can dry** rather than shutting it in a cold garage still dripping.

The useful routine and lubricant depend on the bicycle and products. Follow the
component manuals, and ask a workshop to inspect persistent noise, contamination,
rough bearings or reduced braking.

## Why You Should Actually Embrace Wet Riding

Suitable wet rides can add useful skill practice and keep an outdoor routine
flexible. They are not a toughness test. Use conservative progressions, review
what felt uncertain and choose an indoor session whenever the combined weather,
route, visibility or equipment risk is not acceptable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it safe to cycle in the rain?**

Yes, with the right precautions. Reduce speed on descents and corners, brake earlier, use lights for visibility, and cross painted road markings, manhole covers and metal surfaces upright. Check tyre and rim compatibility and use a pressure calculated for the complete system rather than making an automatic large pressure drop.

**What tyre pressure should I use in the rain?**

There is no universal wet-weather PSI or safe 5-10 PSI reduction. Start with the [Roadman tyre-pressure calculator](/tools/tyre-pressure), using complete system weight and measured mounted width, then check the exact tyre and rim limits. If you test a wet adjustment, change 1-2 PSI at a time within those limits and stop if the tyre squirms, bottoms or loses support.

**How do I stop my glasses fogging up in the rain?**

Use clear or light-tinted lenses rather than dark ones. Anti-fog treatments help but are not perfect. Many cyclists remove glasses entirely in heavy rain, as the droplets on the lenses can obscure vision more than the rain itself. A cycling cap with a peak under your helmet helps keep rain out of your eyes.

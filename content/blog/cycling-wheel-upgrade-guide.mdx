Your mate just turned up to the Saturday club run on a new set of carbon wheels. You noticed them before he even clipped in. They looked fast standing still — the deep profile, the glossy finish, the way the spokes caught the light. He spent the first fifteen minutes of the ride telling you about them. Fifty millimetre depth. Ceramic bearings. Internal nipples. He said the word "compliance" twice. He dropped you on the same hill he always drops you on, by roughly the same margin he always drops you by, and you spent the rest of the ride wondering if you need a pair.

You probably don't. But you might.

The wheel market is a strange place. It sits right at the intersection of genuine aerodynamic science and some of the most effective marketing in cycling. There are real gains to be had from a wheel upgrade — measurable, repeatable gains that show up in wind tunnels and on the road. There are also a lot of expensive purchases that amount to swapping one set of carbon hoops for another set of carbon hoops that are three percent lighter and fifteen hundred dollars more. The trick is knowing which situation you're in before you hand over the credit card.

## What Wheels Actually Do (And What They Don't)

Three things determine how much a wheel helps or hurts your speed: aerodynamics, rolling resistance, and weight. They matter in that order, and the gap between the first and the third is wider than most people realise.

**Aerodynamics** is the big one. On flat and rolling terrain at speeds above about 30 km/h, air resistance is the dominant force you're fighting. The wheel is the first thing that hits the air — it's spinning forward at the top of its rotation and backward at the bottom, creating a complicated mess of turbulence that either helps or hinders the airflow around the rest of the bike and your legs. A well-shaped rim manages that airflow. A poorly shaped one creates drag. The difference between a box-section aluminium rim and a properly designed 45mm carbon rim is not subtle. It's the single largest equipment-based drag reduction you can make below the handlebars.

**Rolling resistance** comes second, and it's mostly about the tyre, not the wheel itself. The rim's contribution to rolling resistance is indirect — it determines what tyres you can run, at what pressures, and whether you can go tubeless. We'll come back to this, because it turns out to be more important than most riders give it credit for.

**Weight** comes third, and this is where the marketing gets loudest and the science gets quietest. Wheel weight matters during acceleration and on climbs. The rotational component of wheel weight — the mass at the rim, where it's spinning fastest — has roughly 1.5 times the effect of the same mass sitting statically on the frame. That's real physics. But at sustained speed on anything that isn't a steep gradient, the aerodynamic profile of the wheel overwhelms the weight difference. A 1,600g wheelset with a deep, well-shaped rim will be faster on a rolling road than a 1,350g wheelset with a shallow, round profile. Every time.

Here's where it gets interesting. The industry spends enormous energy promoting weight, because weight is simple to measure and simple to compare. You can put a wheel on a scale and see a number. You can't put it on a scale and see its CdA. That's why weight dominates the conversation. Not because it matters most, but because it's the easiest story to tell.

## The First Upgrade: Where the Real Gains Live

If you're riding on the aluminium wheels that came with your bike — the ones with the shallow box-section rims and the heavy brass nipples and the hubs that sound like a coffee grinder — then a wheel upgrade is one of the smartest things you can spend money on. This is the one upgrade where the hype roughly matches reality.

Going from a typical stock aluminium wheelset to a quality 40-50mm carbon wheelset saves somewhere between 5 and 15 watts at speeds of 35-40 km/h. That's a huge range, and it depends on the specific wheels you're leaving and the ones you're moving to, the wind conditions, your speed, and your position on the bike. But even at the conservative end — 5 watts — that's a meaningful difference. Over a 40 km time trial, 5 watts is worth about 30 seconds. Over an event like an Etape stage, it adds up to minutes.

The weight savings help too. You're typically shedding 400-800g from the wheelset, which you'll feel on every acceleration out of a corner and every ramp on a climb. The ride quality changes as well — carbon rims tend to be stiffer laterally, which means less flex under load, and the better ones manage vibration differently from aluminium.

This first step is where the value lives. A $1,200-1,500 carbon wheelset from a brand like Hunt, Parcours, Scribe, Winspace, or Light Bicycle gives you the lion's share of the aerodynamic gains available from wheels. Not all of them. But most. Something in the region of 85% of what the best wheels in the world can deliver, for a quarter of the price.

Let me be really clear about this: the distance between stock aluminium and mid-range carbon is enormous. The distance between mid-range carbon and top-end carbon is small. That matters when you're deciding where to spend.

## Rim Depth: The Crosswind Trade-Off

Rim depth is the dimension everyone fixates on, and for good reason — it's the primary variable driving the aerodynamic performance of the wheel. But it isn't a case of deeper equals better. There's a trade-off, and it gets more aggressive as the rims get taller.

**30-35mm.** A shallow carbon rim. You get modest aerodynamic improvement over aluminium, good crosswind stability, low weight. This depth makes sense for pure climbers, riders who live in consistently windy areas, or riders under about 60 kg who struggle with handling in gusts. The aero gains are real but limited — you're leaving performance on the table compared to deeper options on flat and rolling roads.

**40-50mm.** The sweet spot for most riders, and the range I'd point almost everyone toward if they're buying one set of wheels. Deep enough to capture the bulk of the aerodynamic benefit — wind tunnel data consistently shows that most of the drag reduction from rim depth happens between 30mm and 50mm, with diminishing returns above that. Shallow enough that crosswind handling remains predictable for most riders in most conditions. A 45mm wheel at 35 km/h saves you roughly 8-12 watts over a shallow aluminium rim. That's the kind of gain you can feel.

**55-65mm.** Getting deep. The additional aerodynamic gains over 50mm are real but smaller — maybe 2-4 watts at race pace, depending on the rim shape. The crosswind story changes though. At this depth, a gust hitting you from the side produces noticeable steering force, especially at lower speeds and especially for lighter riders. If you weigh under 70 kg, a 60mm wheel in a strong crosswind is going to move you around. Some riders get used to it. Others never feel comfortable. And a wheel you don't trust is slower than a shallower one you ride with confidence, because you brake into crosswinds, tense up, and choose less efficient lines.

**80mm+ and disc.** Race-day specials for flat, calm courses. Track riders, time triallists, triathletes on calm-day courses. The marginal aero gains are there, but the crosswind penalty is severe, and the weight is back up. If you're reading this post, you almost certainly don't need these.

The honest recommendation: buy a 45-50mm wheelset and ride it every day, everywhere. A wheel that's fast in all conditions beats one that's marginally faster in perfect conditions. A whole season on a 50mm wheel will make you faster than half a season on a 65mm wheel and half a season reaching for your old ones because it's blowing a gale.

## Hub Quality: The Part Nobody Talks About

Here's the thing nobody tells you about wheels. When you buy a wheelset, you're staring at the rim. The carbon layup, the braking track, the width, the depth — that's what you see and that's what the brand photographs. But sitting quietly in the middle, doing all the work, is the hub. And the hub is where long-term performance, reliability, and ride quality are determined.

A good hub does three things well.

**It rolls freely.** The bearings — whether steel, ceramic, or hybrid — should be properly seated, adequately sealed against water and grit, and serviced on a sensible interval. The difference between a fresh, well-made steel bearing and a cheap one is far larger than the difference between ceramic and steel. Good steel bearings in a well-designed hub are perfectly adequate. Ceramic bearings reduce friction marginally, last longer between services, and cost significantly more. The watts saved are in the low single digits. It's real, but it's not transformative.

**It engages quickly.** Engagement refers to how fast the hub picks up when you start pedalling after coasting. A hub with 36 points of engagement takes up to 10 degrees of pedal rotation before it bites. A hub with 54 or 108 points catches almost instantly. On the road, this matters less than mountain bikers think but more than roadies admit — quick engagement means more responsive acceleration and more predictable power transfer, especially on steep, technical climbs where you're alternating between coasting and driving the pedals.

**It lasts.** The freehub mechanism, the axle, the endcaps, the bearing preload adjustment — this is the stuff that determines whether you're still riding these wheels happily in four years or looking for replacements after two. DT Swiss, for example, have built a reputation largely on freehub reliability. Their star ratchet system is simple, robust, and user-serviceable. Other brands use proprietary systems that work brilliantly until they don't, and then you're at the mercy of spare parts availability.

When you're comparing two wheelsets at similar price points, pay attention to the hub specification. The rim is the visible part. The hub is the functional part. A mid-range rim laced to a high-quality hub will outperform a flashy rim on a forgettable hub over the lifetime of the wheelset. Every time.

## Tubeless: The Hidden Upgrade

If your current wheels are clincher-only and the wheelset you're looking at runs tubeless, there's a second upgrade hiding inside the first one. And it might be worth more than the aero gains from the rim itself.

Tubeless tyres — run without an inner tube, sealed with liquid sealant — reduce rolling resistance compared to a standard clincher setup by roughly 2-5 watts per wheel at typical road pressures. The mechanism is straightforward: without an inner tube, there's no tube-to-tyre friction, the casing deforms more freely, and you can run lower pressures without risking pinch flats. Those lower pressures, in turn, improve grip and comfort, which means you corner with more confidence and absorb rough surfaces rather than bouncing over them.

The puncture protection is a practical bonus that doesn't show up on a watt chart but shows up in your season. A tubeless tyre with sealant will self-seal most small punctures — thorns, glass, small flints — while you're still riding. No stopping. No fumbling with CO2 cartridges in the rain. You carry on. Over the course of a year of riding, that reliability adds up to more ride time, less frustration, and — if you've ever been stranded fifteen miles from home in January — significantly better morale.

The setup has its quirks. Seating a tubeless tyre for the first time can test your patience, your track pump, and your vocabulary. Sealant needs topping up every three to four months. The initial mess when you first break a bead is exactly what you'd expect from a tyre full of white liquid. But once it's set up and rolling, it's better in almost every way. More speed, more grip, more resilience, less weight (the sealant weighs less than the tube it replaces in most setups).

If you're upgrading wheels anyway, make sure the new set is tubeless-ready. At this point, most quality wheelsets above about $800 are. If yours aren't, that's reason enough to consider the switch.

## Diminishing Returns: The $2,000 vs $5,000 Question

This is where you need to be honest with yourself, because the marketing won't be.

The performance curve of cycling wheels is steep at the bottom and nearly flat at the top. The gap between a $400 stock aluminium wheelset and a $1,500 carbon wheelset is massive — 5-15 watts, hundreds of grams, a completely different ride. The gap between a $1,500 wheelset and a $3,000 wheelset is smaller — maybe 2-3 watts and 100-150g. The gap between $3,000 and $5,000 is smaller still. Under controlled conditions in a wind tunnel, the very best wheels in the world might save you a watt or two over a strong mid-range option. On the road, with wind shifts, position changes, and the general chaos of real riding, that difference is unmeasurable.

What the extra money does buy at the top end is lighter weight, tighter manufacturing tolerances, fancier hub internals, and better warranty support. Those things are real, and if you have the budget and you've already addressed everything else on your bike and your body, they're worth having. Carbon layup techniques at the $4,000+ level often produce rims that are both wider and lighter simultaneously. The hubs tend to have better engagement, smoother bearings, and more refined sealing. The overall build quality feels different in a way that's hard to quantify but easy to appreciate.

But the watts? Mostly claimed in the first upgrade. If you're choosing between a $1,500 wheelset and putting the remaining $3,500 toward a power meter, a bike fit, and a year of structured coaching — it's not close. The cheaper wheels plus those three investments will make you minutes faster. The expensive wheels alone will make you seconds faster.

The industry has a strong incentive to blur this distinction, because the margins on premium wheelsets are much higher than on mid-range ones. The same carbon fibre, often from the same factories in China or Taiwan, gets laid up with slightly different processes and significantly different branding. That branding is what you're paying for once you cross the $2,500 threshold. Be clear-eyed about that.

## Should You Buy Wheels Yet? The Priority Checklist

Here's the honest order of operations. If you haven't done these things, do them before you think about wheels.

**1. Get a proper bike fit.** A professional bike fit — not the quick adjustment the shop did when you bought the bike, but a proper session with a qualified fitter — will typically save you more watts than any wheel upgrade. Correct saddle height, correct reach, correct cleat position. It fixes comfort, prevents injury, and puts your body in the position where it can produce the most power with the least effort. Cost: $200-350. Return: significant.

**2. Buy a power meter.** A power meter doesn't make you faster directly. What it does is give you the information you need to train intelligently. Without power, you're guessing. With power, you know your zones, you can pace your efforts, you can track your fitness over time, and you can follow a structured plan that targets specific energy systems. When I had Alex Dowsett on the podcast, one of the things he talked about was how precise power data changed the way Astana approached everything from position to fuelling. The same principle applies to you. Cost: $350-700 for a crank or pedal-based meter. Return: transformative if you train with it.

**3. Follow a structured training plan.** The fitness gains from structured training — proper periodisation, progressive overload, targeted intervals, adequate recovery — dwarf any equipment gain. A rider with good wheels and bad training will always be slower than a rider with average wheels and smart training. Always.

**4. Sort your tyres and pressures.** Running the right tyre at the right pressure on your current wheels might be worth as much as a new wheelset on the wrong tyres. Wider tyres at lower pressures roll faster on real roads than narrow tyres at high pressures. Use a [tyre pressure calculator](/tools/tyre-pressure) and get this dialled. It's cheap and it matters.

**5. Then buy wheels.** Once the big four are covered — fit, power, training, tyres — then a wheel upgrade is a smart purchase. At that point, you're adding speed to a system that's already working well, and you'll get the full benefit of what the wheels can deliver.

Skip the first four and buy wheels, and you'll have expensive wheels on a poorly fitted bike, ridden without data, on a programme that isn't going anywhere. You'll be the rider with the flashiest wheels and the slowest improvement rate in the group.

## What to Look For When You Do Buy

So you've ticked the boxes. You're fitted, metered, trained, and running good rubber. Time to buy wheels. Here's what to look for.

**Rim depth: 40-50mm** for most riders. Versatile, fast, manageable in wind.

**Rim width: 21-23mm internal** for road. This is the modern standard, and it matters because it determines tyre shape. A wider internal rim spreads the tyre, creating a rounder profile that rolls better, grips better, and supports lower pressures. If you're looking at a wheelset with a 17mm internal width, it's an older design.

**Hub specification.** Look for reputable hub brands — DT Swiss 240 or 350, CeramicSpeed, Industry Nine, White Industries. Or a brand's own hub with a clear service path and available spare parts. Ask the manufacturer: can I service this hub myself? What spares do I need? How long is the warranty? If those answers are vague, keep looking.

**Weight: under 1,500g for the pair** is a solid target for a mid-range carbon road wheelset. Under 1,350g gets you into the premium territory. Don't chase numbers below 1,200g unless you're a dedicated climber — at that point, you're often sacrificing durability and rim depth for weight savings that only matter on gradients above 7%.

**Tubeless compatibility.** Non-negotiable on a new wheelset in 2026. If a wheel doesn't support tubeless, it's behind the curve.

**Brake type.** Disc brakes are the clear standard now for most new bikes. If you're buying disc wheels, make sure the rotor mount matches your frame (centre-lock vs six-bolt, though centre-lock dominates). If you're on rim brakes, your options are narrowing but there are still strong choices — just be aware that the market is moving away from you.

Test-ride if you can. Some brands offer demo programmes or thirty-day returns. The numbers matter, but so does the feel. A wheel that inspires confidence in a crosswind, that responds when you accelerate, that tracks predictably into a corner — that's the one you want, even if it's three watts behind a rival on a spreadsheet. You ride wheels on the road, not in a wind tunnel.

Put the spreadsheet down. Go ride. If the wheels underneath you are holding you back, you'll know. If they're not — if the thing holding you back is fitness, or positioning, or pacing — then keep your money where it'll make the most difference and save the wheels for the day when they're the last piece of the puzzle.

That's the point where a wheel upgrade makes a real difference. Not before.

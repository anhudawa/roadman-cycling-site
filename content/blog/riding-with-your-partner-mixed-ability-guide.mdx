My wife and I have a very different relationship with cycling. I've spent years building an aerobic engine, talking to coaches, obsessing over training zones and fuelling strategies. She likes riding her bike. Those are not the same thing, and pretending they are nearly cost us every shared ride we had.

This is the thing nobody in cycling media wants to talk about honestly. We'll write endlessly about how cycling is great for couples — and it can be — but we skip over the part where one person is always waiting at the top of the hill trying not to look impatient, and the other person is always arriving at the top feeling like they've ruined the ride. That dynamic, left unaddressed, will quietly poison something that should be brilliant.

The good news: it's fixable. Completely fixable. Not by making your partner faster or by pretending you don't want to ride hard. But by being smarter about how you share the road. I've spoken to dozens of couples through the Not Done Yet community about this, and the ones who get it right all do roughly the same things. Here's where it gets really interesting — none of it requires anyone to change who they are as a rider.

## The problem nobody talks about — when one partner is fitter than the other

Every couple who cycles together knows this feeling. You just don't talk about it, because talking about it feels like admitting something is wrong with either the relationship or the riding. It's neither. It's physics.

If one partner rides ten hours a week and the other rides three, there will be a fitness gap. If one partner has been riding for fifteen years and the other started eighteen months ago, there will be a fitness gap. If one partner is 75 kg with a VO2 max of 55 and the other is 60 kg with a VO2 max of 42, there will be a fitness gap — even if they train the same amount. This isn't a flaw. It's how human physiology works.

The problem isn't the gap itself. The problem is what couples do about it, which is usually one of two things:

**The fitter rider pretends it's fine.** They ride at their partner's pace, tell themselves it's "active recovery," and slowly build up resentment because they never get to ride properly when they're together. They start finding excuses to ride alone more often. The partner notices. Nobody says anything.

**The less fit rider pushes too hard.** They try to match their partner's pace, blow up on every climb, feel awful about themselves, and eventually stop wanting to ride together at all. Or they keep pushing and get injured. The fitter rider feels guilty. Nobody says anything.

Both patterns end the same way: cycling goes from being the thing you share to the thing that divides you. And it happens so gradually that by the time either person notices, there's already a wall of unspoken frustration between you.

I watched this happen in my own riding. We'd plan a "nice easy ride" on a Saturday morning, and within twenty minutes I'd be soft-pedalling on the flat while my heart rate sat at 95 bpm, feeling like I was wasting precious training time. Meanwhile she was working at what felt like threshold just to keep up on a "gentle" ride. We were on the same road, turning the same pedals, and having completely different experiences. Neither of us was wrong. We were just pretending the gap wasn't there.

The couples who ride well together don't close the gap. They design around it.

## Why "just ride slower" doesn't work (and what to do instead)

This is the advice everyone gives. "Just ride at their pace." And on the surface it sounds reasonable and generous. In practice it's rubbish, and here's why.

When a strong rider "just rides slower," they're not actually riding. They're soft-pedalling. Their cadence is wrong, their position feels awkward at low power, and psychologically they're counting the minutes. Every slight incline becomes a conversation — do I ride ahead and wait, or do I stay and crawl? — and the less fit partner can feel that restlessness radiating off them like heat from a toaster. You know when someone's bored riding with you. You always know.

What most people do: the fitter rider grudgingly sits behind, occasionally surging ahead on a climb "just for a minute" and then waiting at the top looking at their phone. The less fit partner feels like a burden. Both people are miserable. The ride is "shared" in the sense that they're in the same postcode.

What actually works: structured asymmetry. You ride together, but you don't ride the same ride. The fitter rider has a plan within the shared ride — efforts, intervals, extra loops — that gives them training value while the less fit rider holds their own honest pace. This requires some route creativity and some honest conversation beforehand, but when it clicks it's transformative.

Dr Stephen Seiler, who's done more than anyone to shape how we think about endurance training, has made the point repeatedly that most of your training should be easy anyway. If you're a well-trained cyclist, 80 per cent of your weekly volume should be at low intensity. A partner ride fits perfectly into that easy bucket — but only if you frame it that way in your head before you clip in. If you tell yourself "this is zone 1-2 today, this is my easy ride, this counts as training" then you stop seeing it as wasted time. You start seeing it as smart programming.

The shift is mental, not physical. The fitter rider needs to actually decide — before the ride starts — whether this is a partner ride or a training ride. If it's a partner ride, it's their pace. Full stop. You chat. You stop for coffee. You point things out. You ride like a tourist in your own neighbourhood and you enjoy it, because the alternative is riding alone on a Tuesday evening wondering why your partner doesn't want to ride with you anymore.

And if you need training value from the ride? That's where route strategies and interval creativity come in.

## Route strategies — loops, out-and-backs, meeting points

The best tool for mixed-ability riding isn't a power meter or a heart rate strap. It's a well-chosen route. Get the route right and both riders can have a brilliant day on the bike without either person compromising.

**The loop route.** You design a route that's a loop — say 30 km — and you ride it together. The fitter rider does two laps, the less fit rider does one. You start together, ride the first lap side by side, and when you get back to the start the fitter rider heads out for lap two while the less fit rider cools down, gets the coffees in, stretches. The fitter rider comes back having done 60 km at their own pace for the second half. The less fit rider did 30 km at an honest effort. Both people had a good ride. Both people shared part of it.

This works especially well if the loop passes through a village or town with a decent cafe. The less fit rider's ride finishes at the cafe. The fitter rider's second lap starts and ends at the cafe. Everyone wins.

**The out-and-back.** You ride out together on the same road. When the less fit rider hits their halfway point — based on time, distance, or how they're feeling — they turn around and head back. The fitter rider keeps going for another twenty minutes, then turns. You meet somewhere on the return leg and ride the last section home together. The beauty of this is that you share the start and the finish, which are the best bits anyway.

**The meeting-point ride.** You take different routes to the same destination. The fitter rider takes the hilly route over the top road. The less fit rider takes the flatter valley route. You meet at the same cafe, the same viewpoint, the same pub. The fitter rider gets climbing, intensity, and a proper workout. The less fit rider gets a pleasant ride at their own pace without feeling chased. You share the destination and the ride home.

**The figure-of-eight.** This is my favourite. You design a route that's shaped like a figure of eight, with the cafe or meeting point at the centre where the loops cross. The less fit rider does the shorter loop. The fitter rider does both. You meet in the middle, refuel, and either ride the last section together or part ways for the second loop. It gives both riders independence and shared time.

All of these require something most couples skip: planning the route together beforehand. Not one person dictating and the other following, but actually sitting down with a map (Komoot is superb for this, and RideWithGPS if you prefer) and deciding together what you both want from the day. That ten-minute conversation over breakfast changes the entire dynamic.

One more thing about routes. Terrain matters enormously in mixed-ability riding. Hills exaggerate fitness gaps. A 5 per cent gradient that feels comfortable to a strong rider might put a less fit rider into the red. Flat or gently rolling routes keep both riders in a zone where conversation is possible, and conversation is really the point of riding together. Save the mountain passes for your solo rides or group rides. Partner rides work best on terrain that's forgiving.

## E-bikes as the great equaliser — removing the stigma

Right. Time to say this plainly: an e-bike is the single best investment a mixed-ability couple can make.

I can hear the objections already. "It's cheating." "It's not real cycling." "They'll never get fitter if they're on an e-bike." All of these are wrong, and I say that as someone who held exactly those opinions about three years ago before I actually spent time around modern e-bikes and the people who ride them.

Here's what an e-bike actually does. It fills the gap. Not all of it — the rider still pedals, still works, still gets a cardiovascular workout — but enough of it that two people with very different fitness levels can ride side by side, have a conversation, tackle real hills, and both arrive at the cafe feeling like they've had a ride. The assist is adjustable. On a flat road on a good day, the less fit rider might run it on eco mode and barely notice it's there. On a steep hill into a headwind on a tired day, they can dial it up and stay with their partner without going into the dark place.

The science has finally caught up on this. Research published in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* and the *Transportation Research Interdisciplinary Perspectives* journal shows that regular e-bike riders achieve meaningful improvements in cardiovascular fitness — not as much as purely acoustic-bike riders doing the same distance, but significantly more than people who aren't riding at all. And that's the comparison that matters. The alternative to an e-bike isn't usually "they ride a normal bike at the same intensity." The alternative is "they stop riding altogether because it's not fun anymore." An e-bike keeps people riding. That alone is worth it.

World Tour teams now use e-bikes for recovery rides. Coaches like Tim Kerrison at Ineos have incorporated them into rest-day protocols so riders can spin their legs without adding training load. If it's good enough for the best cyclists on the planet during the Tour de France, the "it's not real cycling" argument needs to go in the bin where it belongs.

The modern crop of e-bikes — the Specialized Turbo Creo, the Trek Domane+, the Cannondale Synapse Neo, the Ribble Endurance SL e — are remarkable machines. Light, responsive, and properly beautiful to ride. They don't feel like mopeds. They feel like bikes with a tailwind. The integration is so good that most people riding behind an e-bike on a group ride wouldn't even notice unless they looked at the down tube.

The stigma conversation matters because it's usually the less fit partner who has to overcome it, and they're already feeling self-conscious about the fitness gap. If the fitter rider frames it as "you should get an e-bike because you can't keep up" — even subtly — it lands badly. But if it's framed as "an e-bike means we actually get to ride together properly, and that's what I want" — that's a different conversation entirely. The emphasis shifts from deficit to shared experience. Language matters here. A lot.

One practical note on e-bikes for partner riding: battery range. Most modern road and gravel e-bikes give you 60-120 km of range depending on the assist level and terrain. Plan your routes so the battery comfortably covers the distance, and carry the charger if you're doing a cafe stop long enough to top up. Running out of battery mid-ride turns an equaliser into an anchor, and nobody wants that.

## Interval creativity — the fitter rider does efforts while the other holds pace

This is the strategy that changed everything for us, and it came from a conversation with a coach in the NDY community who works with a lot of masters riders.

The concept is simple. Both riders ride together, but the fitter rider adds structured efforts within the ride that the less fit rider doesn't do. It looks like this in practice:

**Hill repeats on a shared route.** You're riding along a rolling route together. You approach a hill. The less fit rider rides up it at their own pace. The fitter rider rides up it hard, descends, and rides up it again — rejoining the less fit rider at the top or on the other side. The less fit rider hasn't waited. The fitter rider has done a proper effort. Nobody's pace has been compromised.

**Tempo blocks between regroups.** Every twenty minutes or so, the fitter rider rides ahead at tempo or sweet spot for five to eight minutes, then soft-pedals or stops until the less fit rider catches up. The less fit rider holds their own steady pace throughout. The fitter rider accumulates solid training time. It looks a bit like a rolling interval session, and it works brilliantly on long flat roads.

**Sprint sign competitions.** This is more fun than structured. Every time you pass a town sign, speed-limit sign, or other marker, the fitter rider sprints to it from 200 metres out and then sits up and waits. It turns the ride into a game rather than a grind, and it gives the fitter rider genuine neuromuscular work — short, sharp efforts with full recovery.

**Extra climbing.** If your route goes near a short climb — a 1 km drag, a punchy ramp — the fitter rider can peel off, do the climb, and rejoin the main road while the less fit rider continues on the flat route. It requires a bit of route knowledge but it's the kind of thing that works perfectly once you know your local roads well.

**Wind work.** On an exposed, windy route the fitter rider rides on the front permanently, taking all the wind, while the less fit rider sits in the draft. The fitter rider gets a massive training stimulus from pulling into the wind. The less fit rider gets an easier ride thanks to the shelter. Both are working at their own level. This is essentially how professional cycling works — domestiques on the front, the protected rider in the wheels — and it's beautifully effective for couples.

The key to all of these is communication. The less fit rider needs to know what's happening so they don't feel abandoned every time their partner rides away. "I'm going to do three hill repeats here, I'll catch you on the other side" is completely different from just riding away without a word. One is training. The other is rude.

A Wahoo ELEMNT, Garmin Edge, or even a phone with a structured workout loaded gives the fitter rider something to follow during these efforts. You're not guessing. You ride to power or heart rate for the intervals and then dial it back for the shared sections. It turns one ride into two rides — a partner ride and a training ride happening at the same time on the same roads.

## Shared events — entering a sportive together (different goals, same day)

Here's where it gets really interesting. One of the best things a mixed-ability couple can do is enter an event together. Not the same event in the sense of the same distance at the same pace — but the same event weekend, the same atmosphere, the same start village, the same post-ride celebration.

Most well-organised sportives — the Dragon Ride in Wales, the Etape du Tour, the Marmotte, the Tour of Flanders sportive, the Wiggle events in the UK — offer multiple distances. A long route, a medium route, sometimes a short route. The fitter rider enters the gran fondo distance. The less fit rider enters the medio or piccolo fondo. You drive there together, pin your numbers on together, ride your respective routes, and meet at the finish line.

That shared experience is gold. The nervous energy in the morning. The ritual of laying out kit the night before. The coffee from the van at the start. The medal at the finish. The pint afterwards where you compare stories — "you should have seen the hill at 80 km" and "the headwind on the coast road was mental." You both did something hard that day. You both have stories to tell. The fitness gap becomes irrelevant because you're not comparing — you're sharing.

I've seen this transform couples in the Not Done Yet community. Partners who'd never entered an event because they assumed it was "for serious riders" sign up for a short-distance sportive, have a brilliant time, and come back wanting to do more. The fitter rider stops seeing the partner as someone who holds them back and starts seeing them as someone who shares the experience. That mental shift is enormous.

A few practical tips for shared event days:

**Book accommodation nearby if it's an early start.** Nothing kills event-day excitement faster than a three-hour drive at 4 am. Stay close, have a calm morning, ride well.

**Set separate goals.** The fitter rider might be chasing a time or a Strava segment. The less fit rider might be aiming to finish without stopping, or to ride the whole thing without walking a climb. Both goals are equally valid. Write them down. Celebrate both.

**Plan the post-ride.** Know where you're meeting, have a change of clothes, pick a restaurant or pub for afterwards. The post-ride is often the best bit, and it's the part you actually share regardless of what happened on the road.

**Consider cycling holidays.** Companies like Mellow Velo, Eat Sleep Cycle, and Love Velo run trips specifically designed for mixed-ability groups. They'll route you along different roads during the day and bring everyone back together for food and wine in the evening. It's partner riding scaled up to a week, and it's a brilliant way to share cycling in a place where neither person has any local-road ego.

The cycling holiday is worth expanding on because it removes so many of the friction points that exist at home. You're both on unfamiliar roads, so neither person has a "this is my route" claim. There's no training plan pressure because you're on holiday. The focus shifts naturally from performance to experience — the view from the col, the village bakery at the bottom of the descent, the surprise gravel track through the olive grove. Mixed-ability riding works best when the ride is about where you're going, not how fast you get there. Holidays force that perspective.

## Communication and expectations — having the conversation honestly

Most of the couples I speak to about this have never actually had the conversation. They've had versions of it — oblique, carefully worded, usually mid-ride when one person is frustrated and the other is defensive. But they haven't sat down over a cup of tea and said, plainly: what do we each actually want from riding together?

This matters because the answer is often different from what you assume. The fitter rider usually assumes their partner wants to get faster. The less fit rider often just wants to spend time outside together. Those are fundamentally different objectives, and trying to serve both at the same time on the same ride is where the tension comes from.

Here are the questions that matter. Ask them of each other, not rhetorically:

**What do you actually want from the rides we do together?** Not what you think you should want. What you actually want. Time together? Fresh air? Exercise? A specific training adaptation? A social thing? The answer shapes everything else.

**How often do you want to ride together?** Maybe it's every ride. Maybe it's once a week. Maybe it's once a month for a big day out. There's no correct answer. But if one person wants every Saturday and the other wants once a month, that mismatch will cause friction unless you name it.

**What pace feels good for you on a shared ride?** Not "I can keep up with whatever pace." What actually feels good? Where is your comfortable conversation pace? Because that's the pace for partner rides, and knowing it takes the guesswork out.

**What frustrates you about riding together?** Say it. Get it out. Chances are the other person already knows and is pretending they don't. "I get frustrated when you ride ahead on every hill" is useful information. "I feel guilty that you're always waiting for me" is useful information. Unspoken, these become resentment. Spoken, they become problems you can solve together.

**What kind of rides should we not do together?** This is the most liberating question. Maybe the answer is "not hills" or "not longer than two hours" or "not on days when you have intervals planned." Defining what doesn't work protects the rides that do.

If this sounds like relationship counselling rather than cycling advice, it's because the problem was never really about cycling. Cycling just made it visible. The fitness gap is a proxy for a communication gap, and closing the communication gap makes the fitness gap manageable.

One more point on expectations. The less fit rider's improvement trajectory — if they want to improve — is something to talk about honestly. New cyclists improve rapidly. Someone riding consistently for six months will be dramatically fitter than when they started. That gap might narrow on its own if the less fit partner is riding regularly. But it might not, especially if the fitter partner is also training and improving. Managing expectations about the gap — whether it will close, stay the same, or even widen — prevents the quiet disappointment that comes from hoping and not seeing.

## When cycling improves a relationship (and warning signs it's becoming friction)

When it works, cycling together is one of the best things you can share. I don't say that lightly. There's something about being outside, moving through the countryside under your own power, side by side, that creates a quality of time together that's hard to replicate anywhere else. You talk differently on a bike. The conversations are better — maybe because you're not facing each other, maybe because the rhythm of pedalling loosens something. The shared suffering on a headwind day bonds you. The shared joy on a tailwind day lifts you. The cafe stops are better when you've earned them.

Couples who ride well together report — and research supports this — that shared physical activity strengthens relationship satisfaction. A study published in the *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* found that couples who engage in novel, arousing activities together (and cycling absolutely qualifies, especially on a descent) experience greater relationship quality. The shared accomplishment of finishing a ride, entering an event, or tackling a route you've both been nervous about creates a bank of positive shared experiences that you draw on when other parts of life get difficult.

The practical benefits stack up too. You share a hobby, which means shared kit purchases feel less like extravagance. You understand each other's training time. You don't have the "you're always out on your bike" argument because you're both out on the bike. Holidays can be cycling holidays. Rest days can be properly shared rest days. The logistics of life with a cyclist partner are dramatically easier when you're both cyclists.

But. And this is where I need to be honest, because I've seen the other side too.

**Warning sign one: score-keeping.** If either partner is tracking who rides more, who rides further, who's faster, who gets more solo rides — that's not cycling any more. That's competition within the relationship, and it's corrosive. Cycling should add to the relationship. The moment it becomes a ledger, something needs to change.

**Warning sign two: one partner's cycling absorbs all the shared time.** If every weekend morning is a five-hour training ride and the partner who doesn't ride (or rides less) is always the one managing the house, the kids, the life admin — cycling isn't improving the relationship. It's extracting from it. The fitter rider needs to earn their training time by being present in other ways, not assume it's owed.

**Warning sign three: identity gatekeeping.** "You're not really a cyclist." "You only ride an e-bike." "You couldn't even do the short route." If either partner is gatekeeping who gets to call themselves a cyclist or whose riding counts, that's not a cycling problem. That's a respect problem, and no amount of route planning will fix it.

**Warning sign four: the less fit partner has stopped enjoying it.** If they're riding out of obligation, guilt, or fear of being left behind — literally or metaphorically — the whole thing needs a reset. Cycling should be chosen, not endured. If the shared rides have become something one partner dreads, stop doing them for a while and figure out why.

The test I use is simple. After a shared ride, do both of you feel good? Not just the fitter rider who got their intervals done. Not just the less fit rider who's relieved it's over. Both of you. If both people get off the bike feeling like that was time well spent, the format is working. If one person consistently feels bad afterwards — frustrated, guilty, inadequate, resentful — the format needs to change.

And changing the format is not failure. Riding separately most of the time and together once a fortnight for a cafe spin is a perfectly valid arrangement. So is one person riding a road bike and the other riding an e-bike. So is one person racing and the other never entering an event. There's no template for how cycling couples should look. There's only what works for the two people involved.

The fitness gap between you and your partner is real. Ignoring it doesn't make it go away. It just makes both people quietly unhappy on rides that should be brilliant. But designing around it — with smart routes, honest conversations, the occasional e-bike, and a willingness to let each person ride their own ride within the shared experience — turns the gap from a problem into a feature. Different speeds, different distances, same cafe. Different routes, different goals, same finish line. Different fitness, different needs, same relationship.

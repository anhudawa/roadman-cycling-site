## The short answer

A cycling recovery week is **5-7 days in which total training stress falls clearly**. For many riders, a sensible starting point is roughly **40-60% less ride time than a build week**, no demanding intervals and one to three complete rest days. Keep easy spins if they leave you feeling better. A few brief openers are optional when you are healthy; they are not a requirement.

That range is a coaching starting point, not a universal prescription. Direct trials have not established one perfect recovery-week percentage, TSS target or 3:1 calendar for cyclists.

## A practical 7-day cycling recovery-week plan

This example is for a rider who normally trains five or six days and has finished a demanding but successful block. “Easy” means conversational, low strain and easier than your usual endurance ride.

| Day       | Session                                                                       | Purpose                                          |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Monday    | Complete rest                                                                 | Remove training stress                           |
| Tuesday   | 45-60 min easy spin                                                           | Gentle movement; finish fresher than you started |
| Wednesday | Complete rest or 20-30 min walk                                               | Recovery outside the bike                        |
| Thursday  | 45-60 min easy; optionally 3-4 × 10-15 sec relaxed openers with full recovery | Keep routine without accumulating fatigue        |
| Friday    | Complete rest                                                                 | Protect the load reduction                       |
| Saturday  | 60-90 min easy endurance                                                      | Shorter than the normal weekend ride             |
| Sunday    | 30-60 min easy or rest                                                        | Choose from response, not guilt                  |

If you usually ride ten hours, this might total four to six hours. If you arrived with unusually poor sleep, illness symptoms, persistent soreness or declining performance, use the more conservative version: remove the openers, shorten the rides and take more complete rest.

## What should change: volume, intensity and frequency

### Volume

Volume is the clearest lever. A cut of roughly 40-60% is defensible as a starting range because endurance taper research repeatedly finds benefit from substantial volume reductions. A [2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 taper studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37163550/) found improved time-trial performance across tapers that reduced volume by 41-60% while maintaining intensity and frequency.

But a taper aims to peak for competition. A recurring recovery week aims to manage training fatigue and prepare for the next block. The taper evidence informs this template; it does **not** prove an exact recovery-week formula.

### Intensity

Remove meaningful threshold, VO2 max and long tempo work when accumulated fatigue is the reason for the week. If you are healthy and simply unloading after a planned block, three or four relaxed 10-15 second openers—with long, easy recovery—may help the legs feel familiar without becoming a workout.

Openers are optional. If you turn them into sprints, chase peak power or add “just one” interval set, the session no longer serves the week.

### Frequency

You can preserve the habit of riding while shortening sessions, or replace rides with rest days. There is no universal need for two rest days, three rides or daily spins. Choose the version that reduces stress and improves your response.

## What should recovery-week TSS be?

There is no useful universal TSS target. TSS rises non-linearly with intensity and depends on an accurate FTP setting, so a five-hour week containing hard intervals can create more stress than a longer week of truly easy riding.

Use TSS as a comparison with **your own** previous two or three weeks:

1. Make total ride time clearly lower.
2. Make minutes at threshold and above close to zero when fatigue is the issue.
3. Confirm that weekly TSS falls rather than forcing a predetermined number.
4. Check whether easy rides feel easier and normal wellbeing trends stabilise.

The [TSS calculator](/tools/tss) can quantify sessions, while the [training-load tracker](/tools/training-load) shows the change across the block. Neither number overrides illness symptoms or a persistently worsening response.

## How often should cyclists schedule one?

Three build weeks followed by one recovery week is a common planning convention. Two build weeks followed by one recovery week is another. Direct evidence does not show that one schedule is universally superior, including for every rider over 40.

The [sport-recovery consensus statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29345524/) emphasises the balance between training, competition, other life demands and recovery, as well as large differences between and within athletes. Start with a calendar template, then move the week when your response says it belongs.

Consider bringing it forward when several of these change together relative to your normal baseline:

- easy rides require unusually high effort;
- power or pace falls across more than one key session;
- sleep quality and motivation worsen;
- soreness or fatigue persists instead of resolving;
- work, travel or family stress rises sharply;
- early illness symptoms appear.

A [systematic review of 56 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26423706/) found that subjective wellbeing measures responded to acute and chronic training load with greater sensitivity and consistency than commonly used objective measures. That supports tracking fatigue, sleep, soreness, stress and mood. It does not create a diagnostic cutoff or make every bad score a reason to stop.

## Masters cyclists: adjust the person, not the birthday

Some masters riders do well with two loading weeks followed by an easier week. Others tolerate longer blocks. A [review of recovery kinetics in masters athletes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25880787/) concluded that the research was limited; any age effect may be smaller than often assumed and needs further study.

Use age as context, then look harder at:

- training history and current volume;
- strength-training load;
- sleep and life stress;
- health and medication changes;
- how long it takes to reproduce a high-quality session;
- whether the planned work is still improving performance.

The [masters recovery score](/tools/masters-recovery-score) can organise those inputs. Treat it as a conversation aid, not a medical test.

## Food, sleep and recovery gadgets

Eat enough to support health and repair, and scale carbohydrate to the work you are actually doing. Do not turn the week into an aggressive diet. Keep normal protein-rich meals and use the [post-ride recovery food guide](/blog/best-recovery-foods-after-cycling) when another demanding session follows soon.

Prioritise a consistent sleep opportunity. You do not need to manufacture an exact extra 30 or 60 minutes, but the easier schedule is a chance to protect sleep rather than fill the space with more work.

Recovery products are secondary. A [2024 umbrella review covering 22 reviews and 1,100 endurance athletes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38753045/) found no single recovery strategy with consistent benefits. Massage, cold water, compression or a supplement cannot rescue a week that never reduced the training stress.

## Why heavy legs are not proof that it is working

Legs can feel flat early in an easier week, but there is no validated “day two feels awful, day five feels amazing” sequence. Do not use heaviness as proof that repair is happening, and do not panic after one dull spin.

Watch the direction across the week. If easy-ride effort, mood, sleep or performance continues to worsen, extend recovery and investigate other causes. The joint [ECSS/ACSM overtraining consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/) notes that prolonged performance decline and fatigue require exclusion of illness, infection, low energy availability and nutrient deficiencies; no single biomarker diagnoses overtraining syndrome.

Seek medical advice promptly for chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, a racing or irregular heartbeat, fever, or symptoms that are severe or persistent.

## How to know the week was useful

Do not grade the week by one wearable score or by whether you set a personal best on Saturday. Ask:

- Did the planned training stress actually fall?
- Are easy rides returning to their normal effort?
- Are sleep, soreness, fatigue and motivation stabilising?
- Can the next key session be completed at the intended quality?

If those answers remain poor, the solution may be more recovery, a lighter next block or investigation of a non-training cause—not a harder “test” ride.

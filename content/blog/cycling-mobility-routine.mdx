## The 15-minute cycling mobility routine

Use comfortable range, slow breathing and no forcing. A mild stretch or effort is acceptable; sharp pain, joint pinching, numbness or symptoms travelling down a limb are reasons to stop.

| Exercise                          | Dose                        | Main job                                         |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Cat-cow                           | 6-8 slow repetitions        | Explore comfortable spinal flexion and extension |
| Open-book rotation                | 5 slow repetitions per side | Thoracic rotation                                |
| 90/90 hip switches                | 6 controlled switches       | Hip internal and external rotation               |
| Half-kneeling hip-flexor position | 2 × 30 seconds per side     | Explore hip extension without arching the back   |
| Controlled hamstring hinge        | 6 repetitions per side      | Hip hinge and hamstring range tolerance          |
| Knee-to-wall ankle drill          | 8 repetitions per side      | Ankle dorsiflexion with the heel down            |
| Glute bridge                      | 2 × 8 repetitions           | Use hip extension after exploring range          |

Work through the list without rushing. It will take roughly 12-15 minutes. If time is limited, choose the two movements linked to a range you have actually measured rather than doing one hurried repetition of everything.

## 1. Cat-cow

Start on hands and knees. Slowly round the spine, then reverse the movement within a comfortable range. Do not chase the largest possible arch. Use six to eight repetitions to notice whether one direction feels different, not to diagnose the cause.

## 2. Open-book thoracic rotation

Lie on your side with hips and knees bent and arms together in front. Rotate the top arm and upper trunk away while keeping the knees comfortably stacked. Follow the hand with your eyes if that feels natural. Complete five slow repetitions per side.

This movement targets upper- and mid-back rotation. It does not prove that the thoracic spine is responsible for neck or low-back pain.

## 3. 90/90 hip switches

Sit with both knees bent and feet a little wider than hip width. Let both knees rotate gently from one side to the other while keeping the movement controlled. Use your hands behind you for support if needed. The goal is repeatable hip rotation, not forcing both knees to the floor.

## 4. Half-kneeling hip-flexor position

Kneel with one foot in front. Gently tuck the pelvis and shift forward until you feel a tolerable stretch at the front of the rear hip. Keep the ribs stacked over the pelvis rather than creating the appearance of range by arching the low back. Hold for about 30 seconds, twice per side.

Cycling and desk work place the hip in flexion for long periods, but that observation does not prove that a cyclist's hip flexors have permanently “shortened” or stopped the glutes firing.

## 5. Controlled hamstring hinge

Place one heel slightly forward with a soft knee. Push the hips back while keeping the trunk long until you feel a tolerable hamstring stretch, then return. Complete six slow repetitions per side. This is a movement drill, not a contest to touch the toes.

## 6. Knee-to-wall ankle drill

Face a wall in a short split stance. Keep the front heel down and move the knee toward the wall in line with the foot, then return. Complete eight repetitions per side. Adjust the foot distance so the heel stays planted and the movement remains comfortable.

If you want a repeatable measurement rather than a diagnosis, the [ankle mobility and calf-strength guide](/blog/cycling-ankle-mobility-calf-strength-guide) explains the knee-to-wall test and its limits.

## 7. Glute bridge

Lie on your back with feet on the floor. Lift the hips through a comfortable range, pause briefly and lower with control. Complete two sets of eight. The bridge adds light capacity work after the range drills; it is not a test of whether the glutes are “switched off.”

## What the evidence supports

A [2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 77 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37301370/) found that chronic stretch training improved range of motion in healthy participants. Static stretching and proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation produced larger ROM gains than ballistic or dynamic stretching in its subgroup analysis, while volume, intensity and frequency did not show clear moderating relationships.

A larger [dose-focused review of 189 studies and 6,654 adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39614059/) also found acute and chronic flexibility improvements. The authors estimated that gains were maximised around ten minutes of static stretching per week for chronic programmes, but this was pooled across body regions, populations and protocols—not a validated dose for this mixed cycling routine.

The boundary matters. An [evidence-gap map of 300 athlete stretching trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38457105/) reported that fewer than 15% measured sport-specific outcomes and only five investigated injury outcomes. The reasonable claim is that stretch training can improve ROM. The unreasonable leap is that this exact routine prevents cycling injury, fixes pain or creates watts.

## Mobility, stretching and strength

“Mobility” and “flexibility” do not have one consistently applied distinction across coaching and research. Coaches often use flexibility for available range and mobility for the ability to move or control it. In practice, the routine includes both held positions and controlled movement.

Strength through a useful range may be part of the answer too. The bridge is included so this is not purely passive range work, and a full [cycling strength programme](/blog/cycling-strength-training-guide) can build capacity that a mobility drill does not.

## Before or after cycling?

Before a hard ride, the priority is a progressive on-bike warm-up that prepares you for the actual task. Dynamic hip and trunk movements can be included if they help you feel ready.

The blanket claim that any static stretch before exercise “kills power” is too strong. A [2024 meta-analysis of 83 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38735533/) found a small loss in isolated maximal strength overall and a larger effect with static-stretch bouts of at least 60 seconds, but it did not find a negative effect on athletic performance in general. Keep long range-focused holds for after the ride or a separate session when they are not part of your preferred warm-up.

## Does mobility prevent injury or fix pain?

Do not sell it as insurance. A [systematic review of controlled sports studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15076777/) did not find a significant association between stretching and fewer total injuries. Cycling-specific factors such as training load, previous injury, strength and bike setup still need attention.

For back pain, mobility may be only one piece. A [review of eight cycling-biomechanics studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27784817/) found associations involving lumbosacral flexion, back-extensor endurance and muscle-activation patterns in cyclists with low-back pain. A newer [bike-fitting review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41705012/) included only three intervention studies; all reported improvement, but varied protocols and short follow-up limit generalisation.

That means a generic “tight hip flexors caused your back pain” diagnosis is not justified. Check position, recent load and capacity. Seek assessment when pain persists or has neurological or other red-flag features.

## How to use the routine for four weeks

1. Choose one or two relevant observations: knee-to-wall distance, comfortable hip rotation or the ease of holding your riding position.
2. Perform the routine two to four times per week for four weeks.
3. Keep the range comfortable and the exercise setup consistent.
4. Recheck the same observation—not a different test every session.
5. Keep movements that helped; replace or remove those that did not.

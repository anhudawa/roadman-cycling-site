You're rolling along a busy road, tucked tight against the kerb, thinking you're being polite. You're leaving plenty of room for cars. You're not in anyone's way. You're doing the right thing.

You're not. You're doing the dangerous thing.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about cycling on the road: your position is your biggest safety tool. Not your helmet. Not your hi-vis vest. Not even your lights, though those matter too. Where you place yourself on the tarmac determines whether drivers see you, how much space they give you, and whether they even register you as a road user at all.

Most cyclists default to the gutter. They ride as far left as physically possible, hugging the kerb, threading between drain covers and debris, squeezing past parked cars with inches to spare. They think they're being considerate. What they're actually doing is making themselves invisible, unpredictable, and vulnerable. They're inviting drivers to squeeze past in spaces that aren't wide enough. They're putting themselves in the door zone. They're surrendering every advantage that road position gives them.

This is fixable. All of it. And it starts with understanding two positions that every cyclist should know.

## Primary and secondary position — the fundamentals

Every road situation you encounter on a bike comes down to one decision: where in my lane should I be right now? The answer is always one of two positions.

### Secondary position — your default

Secondary position means riding roughly one metre from the kerb. This is where you spend most of your time on open, straight roads with good sight lines and enough width for drivers to pass safely. You're visible, you're predictable, and you're giving faster traffic room to overtake when conditions allow it.

One metre, not half a metre. Not six inches. Not brushing the gutter. One metre gives you a buffer from drain covers, debris, and uneven road edges. It gives you room to manoeuvre if you hit a pothole or need to avoid broken glass. And it keeps you far enough from the kerb that drivers register you as being on the road, not on the pavement.

Here's where most people get it wrong: they treat secondary position as their permanent home. They never leave it. They stay glued to the left in situations where the left is exactly where they should not be.

### Primary position — when you need to own the lane

Primary position means riding in the centre of your lane. Not the centre of the road. The centre of the lane you're in. On a typical single-carriageway road, that puts you roughly where the left-side tyres of a car would be.

This feels aggressive the first few times you do it. It is not. It is assertive. There is an enormous difference.

You use primary position in specific situations:

- **Approaching junctions.** A driver waiting to pull out from a side road is looking for traffic in the middle of the lane, not six inches from the kerb. In secondary position, you're in their blind spot. In primary, you're exactly where they're looking.
- **Passing through pinch points.** Road narrowings, traffic islands, width restrictions — any place where the lane is too narrow for a car and a bike side by side. Take the lane. If there isn't room for both of you, there's room for one of you, and you were there first.
- **On roundabouts.** We'll get to this in detail, because roundabouts are where positioning matters most.
- **When you need to prevent an unsafe overtake.** If the road ahead is blind, narrow, or has oncoming traffic, moving to primary tells the driver behind you: not here. Wait for a safe place. This is not being obstructive. This is keeping both of you alive.
- **When passing side roads.** A driver turning left into a side road may not see you if you're buried in the gutter. In primary, you're in their mirror line.

The skill is not picking one position and staying there. The skill is reading the road and moving smoothly between the two.

## Moving between positions

This is the bit that separates a competent road cyclist from someone who just happens to be on a bike.

Moving from secondary to primary should be smooth, deliberate, and preceded by a shoulder check. Glance behind. Check for traffic. If it's clear, move across. If a vehicle is close, hold your position and wait for a gap. No sudden swerves. No lurching into the lane. Smooth and purposeful.

Moving from primary back to secondary is simpler — you're moving left, away from the traffic flow. Still do a shoulder check, because cyclists and motorcyclists may be passing on your left, but the movement itself is straightforward.

The key principle: your position changes should look intentional. A driver who sees a cyclist drift randomly across the lane doesn't know what you're doing. A driver who sees a cyclist smoothly take the lane before a junction understands exactly what you're doing, even if they've never read a word about cycling road positioning. You're doing what every other vehicle on the road does.

That's the point. On the road, you are traffic. Position yourself like it.

## The door zone — the most predictable danger on any road

Let me break this down with simple maths. A car door, fully opened, extends roughly one metre from the side of the vehicle. A car parked at the kerb sits maybe 15 centimetres from the edge. That means the outer edge of an open door reaches about 1.15 metres from the kerb.

If you're riding half a metre from a parked car, you are inside the door zone. If someone opens that door, you have somewhere between zero and half a second to react at 20 mph. That is not enough time to brake. It is not enough time to swerve. It is enough time to hit the door, go over it, or swerve into traffic.

Getting doored is one of the most common urban cycling accidents. It is also one of the most avoidable.

The fix is simple and absolute: ride at least one metre from any parked car. Not from the kerb — from the car itself. That means your line along a row of parked vehicles will typically be 1.2 to 1.5 metres from the kerb. Yes, that means you're further into the lane than feels comfortable. Yes, drivers behind you may feel you're taking up too much room. That's fine. They can wait two seconds and pass when there's space. You can't undo a broken collarbone.

### Reading parked cars

Not every parked car is equally dangerous. Learn to read the clues:

- **Occupied vehicles.** Look through rear windows for heads in the driver's seat. Brake lights that have just gone off. Exhaust fumes from an idling engine. Indicator lights. Any of these mean a door could open at any moment.
- **Recently parked vehicles.** A car that pulled in 10 seconds ago is far more dangerous than one that's been sitting for hours. The driver is about to get out.
- **Delivery vehicles.** Vans stopped on a busy road. The driver is on a schedule. They will open that door the second they stop, and they will not check for cyclists. Give them more room, not less.
- **Passenger side.** Passengers check mirrors even less frequently than drivers. On a road with parked cars, the nearside passenger door is the one most likely to swing open without warning.

Build the habit of scanning two to three cars ahead. Your eyes should be on the parked vehicles 30 metres up the road, not the one you're currently passing. By the time you're alongside a car, it's too late to adjust your line.

## Roundabouts — where road position is life or death

I'm not being dramatic. Roundabouts are the single most dangerous piece of road infrastructure for cyclists, and poor positioning is the main reason.

Here's what happens when you get it wrong: you approach a roundabout in secondary position, tucked against the kerb. A car arrives at the same time or slightly behind you. The driver doesn't register you — you're in their peripheral vision at best, invisible in their blind spot at worst. They enter the roundabout. You enter the roundabout. They turn left across your path on the exit. You're underneath their front wheel.

This is called a left-hook, and it is the most common serious accident involving cyclists and motor vehicles at roundabouts. It happens because the cyclist was invisible. And they were invisible because they were in the wrong position.

### How to ride a roundabout

**On approach:** Move into primary position early — at least 30 metres before the roundabout. This gives drivers behind you time to register your position and adjust. Check behind you. Signal if you're turning.

**Entering the roundabout:** Hold primary position. You are in the centre of the left-hand lane. You are exactly where a car would be. Drivers approaching from your right can see you clearly. Drivers behind you cannot squeeze past.

**In the roundabout:** Maintain your lane position. Look at the traffic entering from each arm as you pass. Keep your speed consistent. Do not slow down unnecessarily unless you need to give way.

**Exiting:** Signal left before your exit, check your left shoulder for anyone who might be there, and exit smoothly. Move back to secondary position once you're clear of the roundabout and on the open road.

**Multi-lane roundabouts:** Choose the correct lane exactly as a car would. First exit or straight on — left lane. Right turn or beyond halfway — right lane. Take the lane position within your chosen lane and hold it. On exit, check your mirrors (or shoulder), signal, and move across.

The fundamental principle: you are a vehicle. Position yourself where vehicles go. Drivers are trained to look for vehicles in certain places on a roundabout. Be in those places.

### Small roundabouts and mini-roundabouts

Mini-roundabouts present a specific problem because drivers often treat them as speed bumps rather than junctions. The same principles apply but compressed: take primary position early, hold it through, signal on exit. The reduced size actually makes primary position more important, because there is even less room for a driver to pass you safely within the roundabout.

## Junctions — left turns, right turns, and side roads

### Turning left

A left turn from a main road into a side road is the simplest manoeuvre, but it still catches people out. The danger is a vehicle behind you that is also turning left and tries to cut inside you, or a vehicle overtaking you that doesn't realise you're turning.

Signal in good time. Check behind. Move to a position slightly further from the kerb than you might expect — this prevents anyone from passing you on the inside during the turn. Make the turn wide enough to avoid the kerb, and be ready for pedestrians crossing the side road.

### Turning right

Right turns are the manoeuvre that terrifies new road cyclists. You need to cross lanes of traffic to get into the right-turn position, and that means occupying the centre or right-hand side of the road.

Here's the good news: this is more straightforward than it feels.

Well before the junction — 50 metres at least on a busy road — check behind, signal right, and move into the centre of the road or the right-turn lane if there is one. Hold your position. When the junction is clear or the lights change, make the turn.

If the road is too busy or too fast to move across, there is an alternative that is completely legitimate: use a two-stage turn. Stay on the left, pass the junction, stop on the far side, reposition yourself to face the new direction, and rejoin the traffic flow when it's clear. This is not a failure. This is a practical decision based on conditions. The pros do this too.

### Passing side roads

When riding along a main road past a junction on your left, move toward primary position as you pass. A driver waiting to pull out of that side road is looking for traffic in the middle of the carriageway. If you're in the gutter, you don't exist. In primary, they see you.

Watch for vehicles turning left into side roads from the opposite direction, too. An oncoming driver turning across your path may not have seen you if you're tucked into the kerb.

## Assertive riding is not aggressive riding

This distinction matters. Aggressive riding is weaving through traffic, running lights, cutting across lanes, and generally behaving as though the rules don't apply to you. That's dangerous and stupid.

Assertive riding is something entirely different. It means:

- **Being where drivers expect to see road users.** In the lane, not in the gutter.
- **Communicating your intentions clearly.** Through position first, signals second.
- **Controlling space when you need to.** Taking the lane at pinch points, junctions, and roundabouts.
- **Being predictable.** Holding your line. Signalling your moves. Not swerving in and out of parked cars.
- **Making eye contact.** At junctions, look at the driver. If they've seen you, you'll know. If they haven't, you'll know that too.

The research backs this up. A study from the University of Leeds found that drivers give more passing distance to cyclists riding further from the kerb. Counter-intuitively, riding closer to the centre of the lane resulted in wider, safer overtakes. When you ride in the gutter, drivers try to squeeze past without changing lane. When you take a visible position, they move out properly.

Position communicates presence. And presence commands respect. Not because drivers are generous. Because your position triggers the same spatial awareness that another car would.

## What the Highway Code actually says

The 2022 Highway Code update changed the language around cycling road positioning significantly, and most drivers — and plenty of cyclists — don't know the details.

**Rule 72** now explicitly states that cyclists may ride in the centre of their lane on quiet roads or streets, in slower-moving traffic, and when approaching junctions or road narrowings where it would be unsafe for a driver to overtake.

**Rule H3** requires drivers to give cyclists at least 1.5 metres when overtaking at speeds up to 30 mph, and more at higher speeds. It also instructs drivers not to cut across cyclists when turning at a junction.

**The hierarchy of road users** now places cyclists above drivers in terms of responsibility. Drivers are expected to take the greater share of responsibility for the safety of cyclists, not the other way around.

None of this means you should ride in primary position on the A1 during rush hour. Common sense still applies. But it does mean that your right to take a safe road position is explicitly protected by the rules of the road, and anyone who tells you to get in the gutter is wrong — legally, practically, and morally.

## Putting it together — reading the road

Road positioning is not a set of static rules. It's a continuous conversation between you, the road, and the traffic around you. Here's how that works in practice on a typical ride:

You leave your house and join a residential street. Secondary position. The road is wide, traffic is light, sight lines are good. You ride about a metre from the kerb.

You approach a line of parked cars. You move out smoothly, giving them a full metre of clearance, checking for occupied vehicles as you go. Your line is now 1.5 metres from the kerb. A car behind you waits, because there isn't room to pass with oncoming traffic. That's fine.

You reach a junction. You move into primary position 30 metres before the turn. The car behind you now sees you in the centre of the lane and understands you're at a junction. You signal, you turn.

You join a busier road. You settle into secondary position, but you're already scanning ahead. A bus stop with a bus pulling in. You check behind, move to primary to pass the bus, then return to secondary once you're clear.

A roundabout. You take primary 40 metres out. You hold it through. You signal your exit. You return to secondary.

This is the rhythm of competent road cycling. Secondary, primary, secondary, primary. Reading the road. Anticipating hazards. Moving smoothly. Always visible. Always predictable. Always assertive.

## The position-first principle

Here's where it gets really interesting: most cycling safety advice focuses on gear. Better lights. Brighter clothing. Louder horns. Mirrors. Cameras. And those things have value — I've written about [how to set up lights properly for night riding](/blog/cycling-night-riding-safety-guide) and why [reflective gear outperforms hi-vis after dark](/blog/cycling-night-riding-safety-guide).

But position comes first. Before a driver sees your jacket colour, they see where you are on the road. Before they hear your bell, they've already decided whether you're a road user or an obstacle based on your lane position. Before any piece of equipment does anything, your position has already communicated: I am here. I am traffic. I am not moving.

A cyclist in primary position wearing a dark jersey is treated as a road user. A cyclist in the gutter wearing head-to-toe fluorescent yellow is treated as a hazard to be squeezed past.

Position is your first language on the road. Everything else is a footnote.

## Common mistakes and how to fix them

**Swerving in and out of parked cars.** This is the classic beginner error. You ride in secondary position, then swerve out around a parked car, then tuck back in, then swerve out again for the next one. To a driver behind you, your line is erratic and unpredictable. Instead, pick a line that clears all the parked cars and hold it. If that means riding in primary for 200 metres, ride in primary for 200 metres.

**Filtering on the left at junctions.** Passing a queue of stationary traffic on the inside at a junction is one of the most dangerous things a cyclist can do. The vehicle at the front of the queue may turn left without seeing you. Stay behind the lead vehicle, in primary position, where the driver can see you in their mirrors. If you must filter, do it on the right, where you're visible.

**Hugging the kerb on fast roads.** On a fast road, riding in the gutter invites close passes at speed. Moving to a more visible position — further from the kerb — actually increases your safety because drivers are forced to change lanes to pass rather than squeezing through.

**Not checking behind before moving.** Every position change needs a shoulder check. Every single one. Build the habit until it's automatic. Glance, assess, move. It takes less than a second.

**Riding in the gutter through junctions.** Junctions are where most cycling accidents happen. If you're in the gutter at a junction, you are in the most dangerous place on the most dangerous piece of road. Take the lane. Every time.

## Build these habits on quiet roads first

If you've been riding in the gutter for years, switching to assertive road positioning feels deeply uncomfortable at first. That's normal. You're overwriting years of instinct that told you the safest place was as far from cars as possible.

Start on quiet residential streets. Practise taking primary position at junctions with no traffic behind you. Get comfortable with the lane position. Then add busier roads. Then roundabouts. Within a few rides, primary position will feel natural. Within a few weeks, you won't understand how you ever rode any other way.

The single most important thing you can do for your safety on a bike is not buy better equipment. It's ride in the right place on the road.

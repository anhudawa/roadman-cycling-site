You know the moment. It is October, maybe November. The event calendars for next year start dropping. Sportive entries open. Your club announces its racing programme. And suddenly you have signed up for six events across five months because they all looked good and the early-bird pricing was right.

By June you are cooked. You raced well in March — way too well, as it turned out — and you have been slowly fading ever since. The one event you actually cared about, the one in August that you circled in January, arrives and you are running on fumes.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about season planning: the number of events you enter is not the problem. The order in which you make decisions is. Most riders plan forwards — pick events as they appear, train for whatever is coming next, hope it all works out. The riders who perform when it matters plan backwards. They start with the race that matters most and let that single date dictate everything else.

This is not about periodisation mechanics. It is not about goal-setting frameworks. It is about the calendar architecture of your season — the decisions you make before a single training session gets written.

## The A/B/C Race Hierarchy (And Why Most People Get It Wrong)

The idea of categorising your events into A, B, and C races has been around forever. Joe Friel formalised it in *The Cyclist's Training Bible*, and it is the foundation of every sensible season plan. But there is a critical distinction that gets lost: this hierarchy is not about how hard the event is. It is about how you treat it.

**A-race:** The event your entire season is built around. You taper for it. You peak for it. You sacrifice training consistency in the week before and recovery capacity in the week after. You measure your season by how this one goes.

**B-race:** A serious effort, but not a peak. You race hard, you care about the result, but you do not taper for it and you do not disrupt your training block to accommodate it. B-races are high-quality race simulations.

**C-race:** A training ride with a number pinned to your jersey. You enter for race practice, for the experience of riding in a bunch, for the stimulus. Zero expectations about the result.

Here is where masters riders mess this up. They treat every event like a B-race. They put in a hard effort every time, take a few days easy afterwards, and never commit fully in either direction — never properly peaking, never properly training through. It is a recipe for being permanently at 85 percent.

### How Many A-Races Can You Actually Handle?

If you are over 40, the answer is probably fewer than you think.

Friel has been coaching masters athletes for decades, and his recommendation is clear: one or two A-races per season, separated by at least eight to ten weeks. That is not a suggestion. That is what the recovery data supports. Each genuine peak — the kind where you taper properly, race at full capacity, and squeeze out everything you have — takes a toll. And the recovery from that toll is longer after 40 than it was at 25.

Dan Lorang talks about this in the context of WorldTour riders, but the principle scales down. Even the best-prepared athletes cannot sustain peak form for more than two or three weeks. For age-group riders with full-time jobs and families, that window is narrower still. You get a week, maybe ten days, where everything clicks. Then it starts to fade.

Two A-races per season gives you two genuine shots at your best. Three is pushing it. Four is a fantasy.

### B-Race Selection Is Where Most of the Skill Lives

Your B-races are not random. They serve two specific purposes: race-specific preparation and fitness benchmarking.

Pick B-races that simulate the demands of your A-race. If your A-race is a hilly sportive with 3,000 metres of climbing, your B-races should involve sustained climbing, not flat crits. If your A-race is a time trial, find B-race time trials or hilly events where you will spend long stretches riding alone at threshold.

The second purpose is assessment. A B-race four to six weeks before your A-race tells you where your fitness actually is — not what your power meter says on a Wednesday evening, but how you perform under race conditions with adrenaline and fatigue and other riders around you. That data is worth more than any lab test.

Three to four B-races per season is a solid number. Enough to get race-fit without disrupting training blocks.

C-races? As many as you enjoy and can fit in without compromising recovery. Just make sure you actually treat them as training. No heroics. No going to the well because someone attacked on the last lap and your ego could not let it go.

## Working Backwards: The Single Calculation That Drives Everything

This is the part that changes everything if you have never done it properly. You take your A-race date and you count backwards.

Say your A-race is August 16th. Here is how the calendar unfolds in reverse.

**Taper:** 10-14 days before the event. Volume drops by 40-60 percent. Intensity stays sharp but sessions get shorter. You feel restless and slightly sluggish by the end — that is normal and it means it is working. Taper starts around August 2nd.

**Build phase:** 6-8 weeks of race-specific intensity. This is where threshold, VO2max, and race-simulation work lives. If your taper starts August 2nd, your build phase runs roughly from mid-June to early August.

**Base phase:** 10-14 weeks of aerobic foundation and strength work. This is the big block. If your build starts mid-June, your base phase kicks off somewhere around mid-March.

**Transition/off-season:** Everything before base. Unstructured riding, recovery, cross-training, and mental reset.

That single calculation — counting backwards from August 16th — just told you that your base training needs to begin in March, which means your off-season runs from November to February, which means you need to have your events chosen and your calendar mapped by November at the latest.

Plan in November for an August race? Plenty of time. Plan in April? You are already scrambling.

### The Maths for Two A-Races

If you want two peaks, the calculation gets more interesting. You need enough time between the first A-race and the second to properly recover, rebuild, and peak again. Friel recommends a minimum of eight weeks between peaks, ideally ten to twelve.

Say A-race one is in June and A-race two is in September. You would need:

- Taper one: late May into June
- A-race one: early June
- Recovery and rebuild: June through July (6-8 weeks)
- Short build two: late July into August (4-6 weeks — shorter because the aerobic base is already established)
- Taper two: late August
- A-race two: early September

That is tight but workable. Push those A-races closer together — say June and August — and you are trying to recover, rebuild, and peak again in six weeks. It can be done, but the second peak will almost certainly be lower than the first.

## The Most Common Mistake: Peaking Too Early

If your A-race is in August and you are doing threshold intervals in January, something has gone badly wrong.

This is the single most destructive pattern in amateur season planning. Riders start intensity work too early, feel brilliant in March, race well in April, and then spend the next four months watching their form slowly drain away. By the time their actual target event arrives, they have accumulated seven months of training stress with no structured recovery, and they are running on determination alone.

The fix is boring but effective: respect the timeline. If your build phase does not need to start until June, then January through May is base training. Aerobic work. Strength. Skills. The stuff that feels like you are going nowhere until you realise you have built the engine that everything else runs on.

I see this every season. Riders who were patient through January and February, who trusted the plan when their mates were posting Zwift race results, absolutely flying in July and August while the early-starters are booking sports massages and wondering where it all went wrong.

## Reverse Periodisation and Where It Fits in Your Calendar

For masters riders who are time-crunched — and let's be honest, that is most of us — the traditional model of grinding out long base miles through the winter does not always make sense. You have got 60-90 minutes before work, it is dark at 4pm, and the turbo trainer is right there.

Reverse periodisation flips the order. You maintain intensity through winter — threshold sessions, VO2max intervals, structured work on the turbo — and build volume in spring and summer when the daylight and weather allow longer rides outdoors.

For season architecture, this changes the calendar shape but not the principle. You are still counting backwards from your A-race. The difference is what each block contains.

A reverse-periodised season for an August A-race might look like:

- **November-February:** Indoor intensity block. Three to four structured sessions per week, 60-90 minutes each. Threshold, sweet spot, VO2max work. Total weekly volume: 6-8 hours.
- **March-May:** Volume builds as outdoor riding becomes practical. Long weekend rides. Total volume climbs towards 10-12 hours. Intensity stays in the programme but becomes a smaller proportion of total work.
- **June-July:** Race-specific build. This looks similar to the traditional model — threshold and race-simulation work — but you arrive here with a strong top-end that never fully decayed over winter.
- **August:** Taper and A-race.

The key advantage for masters riders: the high-end fitness that is hardest to rebuild after 40 stays preserved through winter. You are not spending 14 weeks in spring trying to find your VO2max again. It never left.

## Building the Calendar Around Real Life

Here is where it gets really interesting, and where most season plans fall apart.

Your plan cannot exist in a vacuum. It has to survive contact with your actual life — the family holiday in half-term, the work conference in March, the week your kids have exams and you are on school-run duty, the wedding in June.

These are not disruptions to your plan. They are constraints the plan must be built around.

### Step 1: Map the Non-Negotiables First

Before you open a single event website, sit down with a calendar and block out every week where training will be reduced or impossible. Be honest. If the family holiday is a week of eating pasta and swimming in the hotel pool, that is a recovery week. Mark it.

Common non-negotiables for masters riders:

- School half-terms and holidays
- Work travel or conference periods
- Family events (weddings, birthdays, visiting relatives)
- Partner's schedule demands
- End-of-year work deadlines

Get these on the calendar first. They are fixed. Training works around them, not the other way around.

### Step 2: Place Your A-Race

With the non-negotiables mapped, you can see the gaps. Your A-race needs to sit in a period where you have consistent training availability in the 8-10 weeks beforehand. If the only clear run you have is May through July, then an August A-race makes sense. If work goes quiet in autumn, a late-September or October event might work better.

Do not pick an A-race where the build phase collides with a two-week family holiday. You can recover a lost week of base training. You cannot recover a lost fortnight of build phase.

### Step 3: Count Backwards and See What Fits

Now run the backwards calculation. Does the timeline actually work? If your A-race is in September but you have a conference in July and a holiday in August, you have just lost three of your eight build-phase weeks. That is a problem. Either move the A-race or accept that this year, the September event is a B-race and your A-race is somewhere else.

This is the discipline most riders lack. They pick the event they want, hope the calendar cooperates, and then try to cram training into the gaps. The riders who consistently perform are the ones who let the calendar tell them what is realistic.

### Step 4: Place Your B-Races Strategically

B-races slot into the build phase and early peak period. You want them in positions where:

- They simulate A-race demands
- They fall on natural training weekends (not disrupting key sessions)
- They are early enough to provide useful fitness data (4-8 weeks before the A-race is ideal)
- Recovery from them does not derail the following week's training

A B-race that requires travel, a hotel stay, and two days off the bike is not a B-race. It is an A-race pretending to be something smaller.

### Step 5: Use Non-Negotiables as Recovery Weeks

Here is where clever planning turns constraints into advantages. That half-term holiday? Perfect recovery week at the end of a three-week build block. The work conference where you are stuck in hotels? Easy spin on the hotel gym bike, some stretching, mental reset. The week your partner is away and you are doing all the school runs? Reduce volume, maintain one quality session, use the lower training stress as adaptation time.

Friel calls this periodising life around training. I would flip it — periodise your training around life. Same outcome, but the second version actually happens.

## The Masters-Specific Differences That Change Everything

Season planning for a 45-year-old is not the same as for a 25-year-old, and it is not just about recovery time. There are structural differences that should change how you map your year.

### Recovery Between Peaks Takes Longer

A 25-year-old can race hard on Saturday, recover by Tuesday, and be back in quality training by Wednesday. At 45, that timeline stretches. Hard racing might need four or five days before you can hit quality sessions again. Over a season, that adds up. Every B-race costs you more training days than it costs a younger rider. Which means you need fewer of them, spaced further apart.

### Consistency Matters More Than Any Single Block

This is something Friel hammers home in his masters-specific work. The single biggest predictor of performance for older athletes is not the quality of any individual training block. It is how many weeks of consistent training you string together across the season. A masters rider who trains four days a week for 40 weeks will outperform one who trains six days a week for 20 weeks and spends the other 20 weeks injured or burnt out.

Your season architecture should prioritise consistency above all else. If that means one fewer B-race because the travel disrupts your routine, skip it. If it means a slightly longer base phase because you are only training four days a week, extend it. The plan that you actually follow beats the plan that looks perfect on paper.

### Strength Work Runs Year-Round

Younger riders can afford to let strength maintenance slide during race season. Masters riders cannot. Muscle mass decline accelerates after 40, and the research — Ronnestad's work is particularly good here — shows that in-season strength maintenance prevents the power losses that many older riders accept as inevitable.

What this means for your calendar: you need to keep two strength sessions per week through base and build, dropping to one during taper and race weeks. That is time you need to account for. If you are already tight on training hours, those strength sessions replace ride volume, not get bolted on top.

### The Off-Season Is Not Optional

Masters riders who skip the transition period — the unstructured two to four weeks between seasons — tend to break down by mid-season. The off-season is where the body consolidates a full year of training adaptations, where niggles get treated before they become injuries, where the mental side resets.

Plan it in. Two weeks minimum of unstructured activity. Ride if you feel like it, do not if you do not. No power meter, no TrainingPeaks, no structured anything. Then ease back into base training. Your body will thank you by April.

## A Sample Season Architecture

Here is a practical example. A masters rider with an A-race on August 16th, a family holiday the last two weeks of July, and a work conference in early March.

**November 1 - November 14:** Transition. Off-season. Unstructured.

**November 15 - February 28:** Base phase (15 weeks). Aerobic work, strength building, skills. If using reverse periodisation: structured intensity indoors, three to four sessions per week, 6-8 hours total. Recovery weeks every third week. Work conference in early March is noted — the last week of February becomes a recovery week, sliding naturally into the disrupted conference week.

**March 1 - March 7:** Conference week. Reduced training. Hotel gym sessions. Treat as additional recovery.

**March 8 - June 7:** Continued base / early build (13 weeks). Volume builds as outdoor riding becomes practical. B-race one in late April (fitness check). B-race two in late May (race-specific simulation). Recovery weeks built in.

**June 8 - July 12:** Build phase (5 weeks). Race-specific intensity. Threshold, race-simulation, and targeted sessions.

**July 13 - July 26:** Family holiday. Recovery and maintenance. Easy rides if available. This is not a disaster — it is a planned recovery block between build and peak.

**July 27 - August 2:** Sharpening week. Short, sharp sessions to remind the body what race effort feels like after the holiday. Not a panic block. Two or three quality sessions, plenty of rest.

**August 3 - August 15:** Taper. Volume drops. One or two openers. Trust the training.

**August 16:** A-race day.

Notice how the holiday is not a problem. It is a feature. It sits exactly where a recovery block would naturally go. That is not luck — that is architecture.

## What to Do When the Plan Needs to Change

It will need to change. Everyone's does.

Illness knocks out a week of training. A B-race gets cancelled. Your knee starts complaining during the build phase. The A-race changes its date. Life happens.

The principle is simple: protect the A-race date and work backwards from it again. If you lose two weeks of base to flu in January, you do not try to cram those weeks in later. You accept a slightly shorter base phase and start your build on schedule. The build phase timing matters more than the base phase length.

If you lose time during the build phase, that is more serious. You have two options: compress the build (risky — more intensity in less time means higher fatigue accumulation) or accept a lower peak (safer — you arrive at your A-race slightly underprepared but fresh, which is usually better than overtrained and exhausted).

The one thing you never sacrifice is the taper. Riders who skip the taper because they feel behind on training always regret it. The taper is where the fitness you have built actually becomes accessible on race day. Cut it short and you race on fatigued legs, no matter how fit the underlying engine is.

## The November Decision That Shapes Your Whole Year

Season planning is not something you do in January when training starts. It is something you do in November when the event calendars drop.

One evening. A cup of coffee. Your calendar app and a list of potential events. Block out the non-negotiables. Pick one A-race — two at the absolute maximum. Count backwards. See what fits. Choose B-races that serve a purpose. Treat everything else as a C-race or skip it entirely.

That is it. That is the whole process. The discipline is not in the planning — it is in sticking to the hierarchy when your mate asks you to target the same sportive in April and you know it falls right in the middle of your base phase.

The good news? Once you have done this once, you will never plan a season any other way. The riders in [our Skool community](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling) who switched to backwards planning consistently report the same thing: they race fewer events, they feel sharper at the ones that matter, and they stop wondering why their form disappears every July.

Fewer races. Better architecture. Faster when it counts.

That is the whole trick.

You spent the winter doing the right things. Base miles in the cold. Threshold blocks indoors. Sweet spot sessions that made you question your life choices. The fitness is there. The engine works.

Then you land in Mallorca. Or Girona. Or the start village of the Etape somewhere in southern France. It is 34°C at nine in the morning. Humidity thick enough to chew. You clip in and within twenty minutes your heart rate is fifteen beats higher than it should be at this power. Your legs feel fine but your body is in crisis.

The problem is not fitness. The problem is thermoregulation. Your cooling system was calibrated for 12°C and drizzle, and you have just asked it to work in conditions it has never seen.

This is fixable. Completely fixable. And the fix takes less time than most riders assume.

We have covered [what heat does to your body](/blog/cycling-heat-performance-adaptation-guide) and the [performance case for heat training](/blog/heat-training-cyclists-30-watts-ftp-protocol) elsewhere on this site. This piece is the practical playbook. Three protocols, a timeline, hydration adjustments, and race-day execution. The stuff you actually need to do.

## What Heat Acclimation Does to Your Body

Your body is remarkably good at adapting to heat stress, but only if you give it repeated exposure over consecutive days. The adaptations are specific and measurable.

**Plasma volume expansion.** Your blood volume increases by 10-15 per cent, primarily through expansion of the plasma (fluid) component. More blood volume means more cardiac output at the same heart rate. The heart fills more completely with each beat and ejects more blood per stroke. This is the single biggest adaptation and the reason heat acclimation works even in cool conditions.

**Earlier onset of sweating.** An acclimated body starts sweating sooner and at a lower core temperature. Your thermostat gets recalibrated. Instead of waiting until you are overheating to begin cooling, the system kicks in earlier and runs more efficiently.

**More dilute sweat.** You lose less sodium per litre of sweat. This matters more than most people realise. Sodium loss is a driver of fatigue and cramping in long hot events. An acclimated body conserves sodium better, which extends how long you can sustain the effort.

**Lower core temperature at rest and during exercise.** Your baseline drops slightly, and at any given power output your core temperature will be lower than before acclimation. More headroom before you reach critical levels.

**Reduced heart rate at a given power.** This is what riders notice first. The same 200 watts that was pushing your heart rate to 155 before acclimation now sits at 145. Cardiac drift — that gradual heart rate creep during sustained effort — is attenuated. You can hold your target power for longer before the cardiovascular system starts losing the battle.

These adaptations begin within five days of consistent heat exposure. They develop rapidly through days five to ten and plateau somewhere around day fourteen. The message for the time-poor rider: you do not need a month. You need a focused block.

## Lorenzo's Research and Why It Changed Everything

Santiago Lorenzo's 2010 study at the University of Oregon is the paper that put heat acclimation on the map for endurance athletes. The study took trained cyclists, ran them through a ten-day heat acclimation protocol, and tested performance before and after.

The expected result: they got better in the heat. They did. Time-trial performance in hot conditions improved by roughly five per cent.

The unexpected result: they also got better in cool conditions. Time-trial performance in 13°C improved by six per cent. That was the number that made coaches pay attention.

The mechanism was the plasma volume expansion. More blood volume meant better stroke volume, better oxygen delivery, and better thermoregulation across all conditions. Lorenzo had shown that heat acclimation was not just a survival strategy for hot races. It was a legitimate performance tool.

This is why you now see World Tour teams building deliberate heat blocks into their programmes even when the target race is not particularly hot. UAE Team Emirates, Jumbo-Visma (now Visma-Lease a Bike), and others have incorporated heat exposure as part of Grand Tour preparation. It sits alongside altitude camps as a legal, ethical, proven method of expanding blood volume and improving oxygen transport.

For the amateur rider preparing for a summer sportive, the practical implication is clear: heat acclimation is not just about surviving the heat. It makes you a better rider, full stop. The gains transfer.

## Protocol 1: Indoor Trainer Heat Sessions

This is the most common and most controllable method. You create a hot environment, ride in it, and force your body to adapt.

### What You Need

- An indoor trainer (smart trainer, wheel-on, rollers — does not matter)
- A room you can close off and heat up
- A small space heater or fan heater (pointed toward you, not away)
- An extra base layer or lightweight jacket to wear over your normal kit
- A core temperature monitor if you want to be precise (CORE sensor, or an ingestible thermometer pill for the committed)
- A heart rate monitor
- Plenty of fluid

### The Session

**Intensity:** Zone 2. Low and steady. This is not the session for threshold work. You want your body producing heat from sustained aerobic effort, not from high-intensity anaerobic bursts. Think endurance pace. Conversational. If you cannot talk in complete sentences, you are going too hard.

**Duration:** 60-90 minutes. Longer is not better here. The adaptation comes from reaching and sustaining a core temperature of approximately 38.5°C. Once you are there, the clock is ticking on the adaptation. Going to two hours does not double the benefit but does dramatically increase fatigue and recovery cost.

**Environment:** Close the windows. Close the door. Turn off any fans. Position the space heater so it adds warmth to the room. You want the room to reach 30-35°C. This is profoundly uncomfortable and that is the point. Wear a long-sleeve base layer or a light rain jacket over your normal kit to trap more heat against your skin.

**Heart rate:** Expect your heart rate to be 10-20 beats higher than normal at the same power. This is not a problem — it is the stimulus. Ride to power, not heart rate. If heart rate exceeds Zone 4 at Zone 2 power, you have overdone the heat. Open a window, remove a layer, and back off.

### The Schedule

Run this on five to seven consecutive days, ideally in the final two weeks before your event. Every day is better than every other day because the adaptations build on each other cumulatively. Miss a day and you lose momentum. Miss three days and you are essentially restarting.

**Day 1-2:** Start with 60 minutes. The room can be moderately warm (28-30°C). You are letting your body get a first taste. These sessions will feel terrible. Your heart rate will spike. You will produce an extraordinary amount of sweat. This is normal.

**Day 3-5:** Extend to 75 minutes. Increase the room temperature slightly or add another layer. By day three, you will notice the first signs of acclimation: sweating starts sooner, heart rate is slightly lower than day one at the same power and temperature.

**Day 6-7:** Full 90-minute sessions if you have the time. By now the room that felt unbearable on day one feels merely unpleasant. Heart rate at a given power will have dropped measurably. You are acclimated.

### Practical Tips

Lay down towels. The sweat volume during indoor heat sessions is extraordinary — easily double what you produce in a normal indoor session. Your trainer, floor, and any electronics in the splash zone will thank you.

Keep fluid within arm's reach. Drink 750ml-1 litre per hour during these sessions. Pre-mix with electrolytes. You are losing significant sodium and this is one area where standard water is not enough. See the [hydration section below](#hydration-adjustments-during-acclimation) for specifics.

Do these sessions as your last training block of the day. They are fatiguing and they will affect the quality of any subsequent training. Follow each session with normal cooling — cold shower, cold drink — and prioritise recovery nutrition.

## Protocol 2: Post-Exercise Sauna

If you cannot add extra training hours — and most of us cannot — the post-exercise sauna protocol is the most time-efficient route to heat acclimation. The research here comes from several groups, including work by Mike Zurawlew at Coventry University.

### How It Works

You complete your normal training ride — whatever is on the plan — and then immediately sit in a sauna for 30 minutes. The exercise has already elevated your core temperature. The sauna sustains and extends that elevation. Your body gets the heat stimulus without any additional training load.

### The Protocol

**Sauna temperature:** 80-90°C. This is a standard Finnish-style dry sauna at most gyms. Infrared saunas run cooler (typically 45-65°C) and can work, but you will need longer exposure — 40-45 minutes — to get the same stimulus.

**Duration:** 30 minutes. Sit still. Do not pour cold water on yourself during the session. The whole point is sustained core temperature elevation, and cooling yourself mid-session defeats the purpose. You can pour water on the sauna rocks to increase humidity and perceived heat if you are comfortable doing so.

**Timing:** Immediately after exercise. Within 30 minutes of finishing your ride. The gap matters because your core temperature is already elevated from the exercise, and the sauna capitalises on that elevation. A sauna session three hours later is a pleasant experience but a less effective heat stimulus.

**Frequency:** Every day for six to ten days. As with the trainer protocol, consecutive days produce better cumulative adaptation than spaced sessions.

### The Schedule

**Sessions 1-3:** You will likely need to leave the sauna before 30 minutes. This is fine. Sit for as long as you can tolerate, leave, cool down for two to three minutes, and return. Aim for a total of 30 minutes of exposure across the session. Do not push through dizziness, nausea, or a pounding headache. These are signals to stop and cool.

**Sessions 4-6:** By now, 30 continuous minutes at 80-90°C will feel manageable. Uncomfortable, but manageable. Sweat onset will be faster. Heart rate during the sauna will be lower than in early sessions.

**Sessions 7-10:** The sauna feels almost routine. This is the acclimation working. Your body has recalibrated its thermostat.

### Post-Sauna Recovery

Cool down gradually. A cold shower is fine after the sauna but avoid ice baths in the first 30 minutes — the extreme temperature swing can cause cardiovascular stress in some individuals.

Drink 500-750ml of fluid with electrolytes within the first 30 minutes after leaving the sauna. Weigh yourself before the ride and after the sauna. The difference is fluid loss, and every kilogram of weight lost is roughly a litre of fluid you need to replace.

### Sauna vs Trainer Heat: Which Is Better?

They are roughly equivalent for plasma volume expansion, which is the primary adaptation. The trainer method also trains your body to exercise in the heat, which adds a neuromuscular and psychological component. If your event is in the heat, trainer heat sessions are slightly more specific. If time is the constraint, sauna is the pragmatic choice.

You can also combine them. Three or four trainer heat sessions in week one, then daily post-ride sauna sessions in week two. This gives you both the exercise-in-heat specificity and the time efficiency.

## Protocol 3: Overdressing Outdoors

This is the simplest protocol and the one with the lowest barrier to entry. You ride outside wearing more clothing than conditions require, forcing your body to retain heat.

### How To Do It

On a 15-20°C day, wear a long-sleeve thermal base layer under your jersey. Add arm warmers. Consider a gilet. You are not trying to replicate Saharan conditions — you are trying to prevent your body from cooling efficiently so that core temperature stays elevated during a normal ride.

Ride at your normal endurance pace. Zone 2. The extra clothing will push heart rate up and force your thermoregulatory system to work harder. This is the adaptation trigger.

### Limitations

This method is the least controllable of the three. Outdoor temperature, wind, and sun exposure all vary and you cannot fine-tune them. On a properly warm day (25°C+), overdressing can push you into dangerous territory faster than you realise. On a cool day (10°C), even full winter kit may not raise your core temperature enough to trigger acclimation.

Overdressing works best as a supplement to the trainer or sauna protocols, not as a standalone. Use it to extend the number of heat-exposure sessions in a block without adding more indoor training hours.

### Safety Note

If you are overdressing outdoors and you begin to feel dizzy, confused, or nauseous, stop riding immediately. Remove layers. Pour water over your head and neck. These are signs that your core temperature has risen too high. Outdoors, you do not have the option of simply opening a door and stepping into air conditioning.

## The Timeline: When to Start, How Long It Lasts

### When to Start

The ideal window is 10-14 days before your event, finishing three to five days before the start. This gives you enough sessions for full acclimation and enough recovery time to arrive at the event rested and adapted.

If you have less time, a five-day block finishing two days before the event will still produce meaningful acclimation. You will not reach full adaptation, but you will get 60-70 per cent of the benefit. That is the difference between a manageable day and a survival march.

If you have more time, there is no advantage to starting a heat protocol four weeks out. The adaptations plateau at around 14 days and then you are maintaining, not improving. Use those earlier weeks for your normal training. Start the heat block when it matters.

### How Long the Adaptations Last

This is the question everyone asks, and the answer is encouraging. Heat acclimation decays at roughly 2.5 per cent per day without continued heat exposure. That sounds fast until you do the maths.

After a full 14-day protocol, you are at 100 per cent of your acclimation ceiling. Seven days later, without any heat exposure, you have lost about 17 per cent. You still have 83 per cent of the adaptation. Ten days later, you are at roughly 75 per cent. Even two weeks after completing the protocol, you retain the majority of the benefit.

This means you have a comfortable window. Finish your heat block a week before the event. Taper normally. Travel. The acclimation will be there when you need it.

### Maintenance

If your event is more than two weeks after your acclimation block, throw in a short top-up session — 30-minute sauna or 45-minute overdressed ride — every three to four days. This is enough to maintain the adaptation without adding meaningful fatigue.

## Hydration Adjustments During Acclimation

Heat acclimation changes your fluid requirements, and most riders do not adjust enough.

As your body acclimates, your sweat rate increases. This is one of the key adaptations — earlier and more profuse sweating for better evaporative cooling. But more sweat means more fluid loss and more sodium loss.

### During Acclimation Sessions

**Fluid:** Drink 750ml-1 litre per hour during heat sessions. This is higher than your normal training intake because sweat rates during deliberate heat exposure are dramatically higher than during normal riding.

**Sodium:** Add 500-1000mg of sodium per litre of fluid during heat sessions. Standard electrolyte tablets often contain only 200-350mg of sodium per serving, which is not enough. Look at the label. Supplement with additional sodium if needed — a quarter teaspoon of table salt in a 750ml bottle adds approximately 500mg of sodium.

**Post-session:** Drink 150 per cent of the fluid you lost during the session. Weigh yourself before and after. If you lost 1kg, drink 1.5 litres over the next two to three hours. Include sodium in this recovery fluid.

For a deeper look at sweat rate testing and sodium replacement, the [sweat rate guide](/blog/cycling-hydration-sweat-rate-guide) covers the full method. The [sodium loading guide](/blog/cycling-sodium-loading-hydration-guide) covers pre-event sodium strategy.

### During the Event

Your increased sweat rate persists after acclimation — that is part of the benefit. But it means your event-day hydration needs to be higher than it was before you acclimated.

Plan for 700-1000ml per hour in hot conditions, depending on your body size, intensity, and the actual temperature. Use the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) to dial this in with your specific numbers.

Pre-load sodium in the 24 hours before the event. Three to five grams of additional sodium spread across the day before, taken with meals and fluid. This tops up your sodium stores and supports the expanded plasma volume you worked to build.

## Race-Day Heat Management

You have done the acclimation. Your cooling system is ready. Now you need to execute on the day.

### Pre-Cooling

Lower your core temperature before the start and you buy yourself extra time before you reach critical levels. The research on pre-cooling is robust and the methods are simple.

**Ice vest or cooling vest:** Wear one during your warm-up and remove it at the start line. This can reduce pre-start core temperature by 0.3-0.5°C.

**Slushy drink:** Crushed ice mixed with a sports drink, consumed in the 30 minutes before the start. The phase change from ice to liquid absorbs significant heat from your core. More effective than drinking cold water because the ice itself absorbs energy as it melts.

**Cold water on the skin:** Pour cold water over your head, neck, and forearms in the start area. Evaporative cooling begins immediately.

These methods stack. Use all three if conditions are truly hot. A pre-cooled, heat-acclimated rider starting at a lower core temperature has a meaningful physiological advantage over a rider who trained the same but showed up warm.

### Pacing

Drop your power target by 5-8 per cent for the first 30-60 minutes. Even with full acclimation, your body will work harder in the heat than in cool conditions. Starting conservatively gives your thermoregulation time to settle into its rhythm before you ask for peak performance.

After the first hour, reassess. If heart rate has stabilised and RPE feels manageable, you can gradually close the gap to your normal targets. If heart rate is still creeping and RPE is high, hold the reduced power. The race is long. Patience in the first hour pays dividends in the final two.

### On-Course Cooling

Every opportunity to cool yourself is worth taking. This is not time wasted — it is performance preserved.

Pour water over your head and neck at every feed station. Soak your jersey. Stuff ice in your pockets and down the back of your jersey. Unzip on descents to maximise airflow across wet skin. These are not marginal gains. In 35°C+ conditions, active cooling is the difference between finishing strong and crawling to the line.

### Fluid Strategy on Course

Start drinking early. Do not wait until you are thirsty — thirst lags behind actual dehydration by 30-45 minutes. Aim for 200-250ml every 15 minutes. Set a timer on your head unit if you need to.

Alternate between plain water (for pouring and drinking) and electrolyte fluid (for drinking). You need both. Use the [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) alongside the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) to set your carbohydrate and fluid targets together — in the heat, both need to increase.

## Safety: Who Should Not Do This

Heat acclimation is a controlled physiological stress. For most healthy, trained cyclists, it is safe. But it is not appropriate for everyone.

**Do not begin a heat acclimation protocol if:**

- You have a known cardiovascular condition, particularly arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension, or a history of cardiac events
- You have had a previous episode of heat stroke or exertional heat illness
- You are currently unwell — even a mild cold or infection compromises thermoregulation
- You are taking medications that affect heart rate, blood pressure, or thermoregulation (beta-blockers, diuretics, antihistamines, and some antidepressants can all alter heat response)
- You are significantly dehydrated or have been unwell with a stomach bug in the previous 48 hours

If you are unsure, speak to your GP before starting. This is not overcaution. Heat illness is one of the few things in endurance sport that can become life-threatening within minutes.

### Warning Signs During Sessions

Stop the session immediately if you experience any of the following:

- **Dizziness or lightheadedness** that does not resolve within 30 seconds of easing off
- **Nausea or vomiting**
- **A throbbing headache** that worsens despite reducing intensity
- **Confusion or disorientation** — this is a red flag for heat stroke
- **Cessation of sweating** while core temperature is still elevated — this means your thermoregulatory system has failed and you need to cool immediately
- **Heart rate that will not come down** even at very low power

If any of these occur, stop. Remove layers. Get into a cool environment. Pour cold water over your neck, head, and wrists. Drink cold fluid. If confusion or cessation of sweating is involved, seek medical attention.

### Building Exposure Gradually

Start conservative. First session shorter, room slightly less hot, extra layer optional. Let your body demonstrate that it is coping before you increase the stimulus. Acclimation is a cumulative process. You do not need to simulate Death Valley on day one.

The riders who get into trouble are the ones who treat heat exposure like an interval session — maximum stimulus from the start, ego driving the duration, ignoring the signals that say stop. Treat it like base training. Steady. Patient. Progressive.

## Putting It All Together

Pick your protocol based on your schedule. If you have flexibility, the indoor trainer method gives you the most control and the most event-specific adaptation. If time is tight, post-exercise sauna is remarkably effective for the investment. If neither is practical, overdressing outdoors is better than nothing.

Start 10-14 days before your event. Run five to seven consecutive sessions. Adjust hydration upward throughout the block. Finish three to five days before the event and taper normally.

On race day, pre-cool, pace conservatively, and drink more than you think you need. The acclimation has given your body a better cooling system. Your job is to support it with fluid, sodium, and sensible pacing.

You trained all winter for the fitness. A focused heat block makes sure that fitness is available when the thermometer reads 35°C.

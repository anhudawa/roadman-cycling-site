> **This is education, not medical advice.** Persistent shoulder pain — particularly with weakness, radiating symptoms into the arm or hand, or pain that wakes you at night — is a reason to see a physiotherapist or sports medicine doctor. The guidance below covers the mechanical, postural, and strength-related causes of cycling shoulder pain, which account for the majority of cases. If pain does not respond to systematic changes within four to six weeks, get a professional involved.

You know the feeling. Ninety minutes into a weekend ride, the burn starts between the shoulder blades. By two hours it has crept up into the trapezius, and by three hours you are hunching your shoulders toward your ears like you are trying to block the wind with your neck. You shake your arms out at every traffic light. You roll your head side to side. It helps for about forty-five seconds, then the tension rebuilds.

Most riders blame the bike. The bike might be part of the problem. But if you spend your working week sitting at a desk with your head pushed forward and your shoulders rounded in — and then you clip in on Saturday morning and load those same structures for another three or four hours — the bike is finishing what the desk started.

Shoulder pain in cycling is a three-part problem: fit, strength, and posture. Fixing one without the others is why it keeps coming back.

## Why Cycling Loads the Shoulders Differently

Running loads the shoulders dynamically. Swimming loads them through range. Cycling does neither. Cycling loads the shoulders *statically* — a sustained, low-grade contraction that holds the upper body in position while the legs do the work.

Your hands sit on the bars. Your arms support a percentage of your body weight. Your shoulders stabilise the entire upper body against the force your legs produce through the pedals. Every pedal stroke generates a small amount of rotational force through the torso, and the shoulder girdle absorbs it.

The problem is not the load itself. The problem is the duration. A two-kilogram static hold is nothing for the first fifteen minutes. By hour three, those same muscles are fatigued, the stabilisers have switched off, and the upper trapezius is doing the work of six or seven smaller muscles that have given up.

Three factors determine whether your shoulders survive a long ride or fold: how much weight your arms are carrying (bike fit), how well the shoulder girdle distributes that load (strength), and what state those muscles were in before you clipped in (posture and thoracic mobility).

## The Bike Fit Side: Reach, Drop, and Width

### Reach — the most common culprit

A bike that is too long for the rider forces the shoulders into protraction — rounded forward, internally rotated, stretched out toward the bars. This is the default position of someone reaching for something just slightly too far away. Comfortable for a few minutes. Punishing over hours.

When the reach is excessive, the mid-back muscles (rhomboids, mid and lower trapezius) cannot hold the shoulder blades in a stable position. The upper trapezius takes over. The rotator cuff works overtime to stabilise a joint that is sitting at the edge of its comfortable range. The result is that familiar burning tightness that starts between the shoulder blades and radiates upward into the neck.

The fix is often surprisingly small. A 10 to 20 millimetre reduction in stem length can transform shoulder comfort on long rides. Phil Burt, who ran bike fitting at British Cycling for over a decade, has talked extensively about how amateur riders consistently ride bikes that are too long — partly because of sizing conventions, partly because a longer bike looks faster, and partly because nobody told them otherwise.

If your shoulders are consistently sore after rides longer than ninety minutes, reach is the first thing to investigate. A shorter stem is cheap. A set of bars with a shorter reach measurement is the next step up. Neither requires a full bike fit to trial.

### Drop — how low is too low

The relationship between saddle height and handlebar height — the drop — determines how much your torso tilts forward. More drop means more forward tilt, which means more weight through the hands and arms, which means more shoulder loading.

For a 25-year-old WorldTour rider with perfect thoracic mobility and a strength programme designed by a sports science team, aggressive drop is manageable. For a 45-year-old with a desk job and a thoracic spine that has not extended properly since 2014, aggressive drop is a recipe for shoulder and neck pain.

Raising the bars by 10 to 20 millimetres — a couple of spacers under the stem — reduces the forward weight bias and takes load off the shoulders. You lose a small amount of aerodynamic advantage. You gain the ability to ride for four hours without your shoulders seizing up. That trade is worth making for most riders.

### Handlebar width — the overlooked dimension

Handlebar width affects how the shoulder joint sits in its socket during the ride. Too wide, and the arms splay outward, stretching the anterior shoulder and loading the rear deltoid awkwardly. Too narrow, and the load concentrates through a smaller arc, increasing the strain on the rotator cuff and compressing the chest.

The old rule of thumb — bar width should match shoulder width, measured from the bony points of the acromion — is a reasonable starting point. But "shoulder width" varies by where you measure, and many riders are on bars that are 20 to 40 millimetres wider than they need.

The trend toward narrower bars in the professional peloton (many WorldTour riders are now on 38cm or even 36cm bars) partly reflects aerodynamics, but it also reflects a better understanding of shoulder mechanics. A bar that is slightly narrower than your shoulder width keeps the arms closer to the body's centre of mass and reduces the lever arm that the shoulder must stabilise.

If you have persistent shoulder pain and you are on 42cm or 44cm bars, try 40cm. The change in shoulder loading is noticeable within the first ride.

## The Desk Job Connection

This is where it gets interesting — and where most cyclists miss the actual cause of their shoulder pain.

You sit at a desk for eight hours. Your head drifts forward. Your shoulders round. Your pectorals shorten. Your mid-back muscles lengthen and weaken. Your thoracic spine stiffens into flexion because you have not asked it to extend all day.

Then you get on the bike. The cycling position asks you to support weight through arms and shoulders that have spent the entire day in a compromised pattern. The muscles that should stabilise the shoulder blades — the lower trapezius, the serratus anterior, the rhomboids — are already fatigued and lengthened from eight hours of postural loading. The pectorals are short and tight, pulling the shoulders forward even further.

The desk and the bike amplify each other. The desk creates the dysfunction. The bike loads the dysfunction. The rider feels the pain on the bike and blames the bike, when the real intervention needs to happen at 2pm on a Tuesday, not at 8am on a Saturday.

If you work at a desk and you ride, your shoulder pain programme needs to address both environments. Standing desk intervals (30 minutes sitting, 30 minutes standing), deliberate posture resets during the working day, and a thoracic mobility practice are not optional extras. They are the foundation.

## The Hidden Driver: Thoracic Spine Stiffness

The thoracic spine — the twelve vertebrae between your neck and your lower back — should be capable of significant extension, rotation, and lateral flexion. In most desk-bound adults over 35, it is stiff as a plank in all three directions.

When the thoracic spine cannot extend, the shoulder girdle compensates. The scapulae elevate. The shoulders round forward. The head pushes forward to maintain line of sight. Every movement that should come from thoracic rotation instead gets forced through the shoulder joint or the lumbar spine.

On the bike, this means the shoulders are working against a locked upper back. They cannot settle into a relaxed, stable position because the structure they sit on will not allow it. The rotator cuff picks up the slack. The upper trapezius fires constantly. The result is burning pain between the shoulder blades and up into the neck — pain that no amount of stem-shortening will fully resolve because the problem is not the bike. The problem is the twelve vertebrae that will not move.

### Thoracic mobility work

Two exercises, done daily, produce measurable change within three to four weeks:

**Foam roller thoracic extensions.** Lie on a foam roller positioned across the upper back (not the lower back). Support the head with the hands, elbows pointing forward. Let the upper back extend over the roller. Hold for five seconds. Roll the roller one segment lower and repeat. Work through five or six positions from the upper to mid thoracic spine. Three sets of five repetitions at each position.

**Open book rotations.** Lie on your side with knees bent to 90 degrees, arms extended in front of the chest, hands stacked. Rotate the top arm up and over, opening the chest toward the ceiling, following the hand with the eyes. The knees stay stacked. Hold the end position for three seconds. Ten repetitions per side, two sets. This targets thoracic rotation specifically — the movement most desk workers have lost entirely.

## Rotator Cuff and Scapular Strengthening

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, which also makes it the least inherently stable. Stability comes from the muscles around it — the four rotator cuff muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) and the scapular stabilisers (serratus anterior, lower and mid trapezius, rhomboids).

Cyclists neglect these muscles almost universally. The cycling position does not develop them. Riding more does not strengthen them. They require targeted, off-bike work — and the good news is that it requires almost no equipment and very little time.

### Rotator cuff exercises

These are high-repetition, low-resistance movements. A light resistance band or a pair of 1 to 3 kilogram dumbbells is all you need.

**Banded external rotation.** Attach a resistance band to a door handle or a post at elbow height. Stand sideways to the anchor. Hold the band with the outside hand, elbow bent to 90 degrees, elbow pinned to the side. Rotate the forearm outward against the band's resistance, keeping the elbow still. Three sets of 15 to 20 repetitions per side. This targets the infraspinatus and teres minor — the two rotator cuff muscles that are weakest in cyclists because the riding position never asks them to work.

**Face pulls with a resistance band.** Anchor the band at face height. Hold both ends with palms facing down. Pull the band toward the face, elbows high and wide, squeezing the shoulder blades together at the end range. Three sets of 15 repetitions. This is one of the best exercises for countering the forward-shoulder pattern from desk work and cycling combined.

**Prone Y-T-W raises.** Lie face down on the floor or a bench. Arms hanging down. Raise the arms into a Y position (thumbs up, arms at roughly 45 degrees from the body), hold for two seconds, lower. Repeat in a T position (arms straight out to the sides). Repeat in a W position (elbows bent, arms pulled back as if squeezing the shoulder blades together). Five repetitions of each letter, two to three sets. No weight or very light dumbbells (1 to 2 kg maximum). These target the lower trapezius and the posterior shoulder — the muscles that cycling completely ignores.

### Scapular stability exercises

**Wall slides.** Stand with your back, head, and arms against a wall. Arms in a "goalpost" position — elbows at 90 degrees, backs of the hands touching the wall. Slide the arms up the wall toward full extension, keeping contact with the wall the entire time. Slide back down. If you cannot maintain wall contact through the full range, that itself is diagnostic — your thoracic spine and shoulder mobility need work. Three sets of 10 repetitions.

**Serratus push-ups.** Start in a regular push-up position (or on the knees for a lighter variation). Without bending the elbows, push the upper back toward the ceiling by protracting the shoulder blades — the movement is small, just a few centimetres of additional elevation at the top. Hold for two seconds. Let the shoulder blades retract and come together. Three sets of 12 repetitions. This isolates the serratus anterior, the muscle that holds the scapula flat against the rib cage and prevents the "winging" pattern that destabilises the shoulder.

**Band pull-aparts.** Hold a resistance band at arm's length in front of the chest, hands shoulder-width apart. Pull the band apart by squeezing the shoulder blades together, stretching the band until it touches the chest. Control the return. Three sets of 15 to 20 repetitions. Simple, effective, and you can do them between meetings if you keep a band at your desk.

The entire programme — rotator cuff and scapular work combined — takes twelve to fifteen minutes. Three sessions per week is the minimum effective dose. Results typically appear within three to four weeks.

## On-Bike Adjustments That Make an Immediate Difference

While the strengthening work builds over weeks, these adjustments produce relief within a single ride.

**Elbow bend.** Locked elbows transmit every bump, ripple, and vibration in the road surface directly through the wrist and into the shoulder joint. A slight bend in the elbows — even five to ten degrees — turns the arms into suspension. The difference is dramatic. If you catch yourself riding with straight arms, consciously soften the elbows. It should feel like your arms could absorb a pothole rather than transfer it into your collarbone.

**Grip pressure.** Most riders grip the bars far too tightly. A death grip transmits road vibration into the forearms and shoulders before the muscles have any chance to dampen it. Hold the bars as if you are holding two eggs. Firm enough not to drop them. Light enough not to crack them. On smooth roads, you should be able to lift individual fingers off the bar without changing your position.

**Hand position rotation.** Vary your hand position regularly — tops, hoods, drops, each for 10 to 15 minutes in rotation on long rides. Each position shifts the load angle on the shoulder by a few degrees. That small change prevents any single group of muscles from reaching the fatigue threshold where pain begins.

**Brake lever positioning.** Brake levers that sit too low on the bar force the wrists into excessive extension when riding on the hoods — the position most riders spend 70 to 80 per cent of their time in. That wrist extension pushes the forearm into pronation, which rotates the shoulder internally. Moving the levers slightly higher on the bar can reduce this chain reaction. A few millimetres of lever rotation translates directly into shoulder comfort over hours.

## When to Self-Manage and When to See a Physio

Most cycling shoulder pain is mechanical. Fit adjustments and strengthening resolve the majority of cases. But not all of them.

**Self-manage when:**
- Pain is diffuse (across the upper back and shoulders, not a single sharp point)
- Pain arrives after an hour or more and resolves within an hour of stopping
- Pain decreases with position changes during the ride
- No weakness, no radiating symptoms, no night pain
- Pain is bilateral (both sides roughly equal)

**See a physiotherapist when:**
- Pain is sharp and localised to one point in the shoulder
- Pain persists more than two hours after the ride
- Pain radiates down the arm or into the hand
- You notice weakness — difficulty lifting the arm overhead, difficulty reaching behind the back
- Pain wakes you at night
- Pain does not respond to four to six weeks of fit changes and strengthening
- Pain is markedly one-sided

The concern with persistent, one-sided shoulder pain is that it may be structural rather than mechanical. Labral tears, rotator cuff impingement, and subacromial bursitis all present as shoulder pain that worsens with cycling. Referred pain from the cervical spine can mimic shoulder pain almost exactly. A physiotherapist experienced with overhead athletes and cyclists can distinguish between these conditions — and the treatment for each is different.

## Massage and Trigger Point Work

Chronic shoulder tension creates trigger points — hyperirritable knots in the muscle that refer pain to predictable locations. The upper trapezius, levator scapulae, and infraspinatus are the three muscles most commonly affected in cyclists.

A monthly sports massage focused on the upper back and shoulders can help manage chronic tension that strengthening alone does not fully resolve. Between sessions, a lacrosse ball against the wall provides effective self-treatment for trigger points in the infraspinatus (the flat muscle on the back of the shoulder blade) and the rhomboids (between the shoulder blade and the spine).

Pin the ball against the wall with your back. Find the tender point. Apply sustained pressure — uncomfortable but not sharp — for 60 to 90 seconds until you feel the tissue soften. Move to the next point. Five minutes of targeted work after rides goes a long way toward preventing the cumulative tension that builds week to week.

Foam rolling the thoracic spine after rides serves double duty — it addresses thoracic mobility and releases the paraspinal muscles that tighten during sustained time on the bike.

## Recovery Timeline: What to Expect

**Acute shoulder pain** — discomfort that started with a recent bike change, a crash, or a sudden increase in ride duration — typically responds to fit adjustments within one to two rides and resolves fully within two to four weeks with targeted strengthening.

**Chronic shoulder pain** — discomfort that has been building for months, worsening gradually, present to some degree on every ride — takes longer. The muscles involved are small, they respond to high-repetition low-load work rather than heavy resistance, and the postural patterns that created the problem have been reinforced by thousands of hours at a desk and on the bike.

A realistic timeline for chronic cases: noticeable improvement in three to four weeks, significant improvement in six to eight weeks, full resolution in eight to twelve weeks. That assumes consistent strengthening three times per week, thoracic mobility work daily, and at least one meaningful fit change (usually stem length or handlebar height).

The riders who do not improve within this timeframe almost always fall into one of two categories: they are inconsistent with the off-bike work (doing it once a week instead of three times), or the pain is structural rather than mechanical and needs clinical assessment.

## Putting It Together

Shoulder pain on the bike is fixable. Almost always. But it requires you to address three things simultaneously, not one at a time.

First, the bike. Check your reach. Check your drop. Check your bar width. A 10 to 20 millimetre stem change is the cheapest and fastest intervention in cycling, and it resolves or reduces shoulder pain more often than any other single adjustment.

Second, the body. Twelve to fifteen minutes of rotator cuff and scapular stability work, three times per week. Thoracic mobility work daily. This is not a large time commitment. It is a smaller time commitment than the riding you will miss if the shoulder pain eventually stops you.

Third, the desk. Your working posture is either part of the solution or part of the problem. Standing desk intervals, posture resets, and a resistance band at your desk for pull-aparts between meetings. The shoulder pain you feel at mile sixty started at hour four of your working day. Fix both environments or keep treating symptoms.

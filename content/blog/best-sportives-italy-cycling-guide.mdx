Italy did not just produce great sportives. It produced the idea of the sportive. The gran fondo — literally "great endurance" — was born in this country, and the culture around it remains distinctly Italian in ways that matter when you are on the start line at 6am in Bormio or Cesenatico or Gaiole in Chianti. The organisation is meticulous. The feed stations serve espresso. The pasta dinner the night before is not a carb-loading obligation — it is an event in itself, three courses with wine, at a restaurant that takes the cooking as seriously as you take the riding.

I have ridden sportives across Europe — in France, Spain, Belgium, Switzerland, the UK. The Italian ones are different. Not necessarily harder (though some of them are brutally hard), but more complete as experiences. The roads are better maintained than their reputation suggests. The drivers are more respectful of cyclists than in almost any other country I have ridden in. And the scenery — Dolomite towers, Tuscan white roads, Romagna hills rolling into the Adriatic — makes you forget, for a few seconds at a time, that your legs are screaming.

What follows is a breakdown of the four Italian sportives I would recommend above any others, plus a handful worth considering if you are building a multi-event calendar. For each of the big four, we have published a full training guide with week-by-week plans — those links are in each section.

## Granfondo Stelvio Santini

**Distance:** 151km | **Climbing:** ~4,000m | **When:** Early June | **Start:** Bormio

The Granfondo Stelvio Santini is defined by its final act. After 130-odd kilometres of climbing through the valleys around Bormio — beautiful but largely preparatory — the route turns onto the Stelvio from the south side. Twenty-four kilometres at 7.1% average. Forty-eight hairpins scratched into the mountainside. A summit at 2,758 metres — the highest paved pass in the Eastern Alps and roughly 25% less oxygen than the valley floor.

I remember the moment the gradient kicked between kilometre eight and fourteen from Bormio — sustained sections above 10%, pitches hitting 14%. You can see the hairpins stacking above you like a staircase painted onto the rock. The riders who go too hard through that section arrive at the exposed upper slopes with their legs gone and their breathing ragged in the thin air. The riders who pace it properly still have enough left to appreciate the view from the top — and to manage the technical descent with 48 freezing switchbacks back to the valley.

The Stelvio is an event for experienced climbers. If you are comfortable holding 3.0 W/kg on long climbs and you have spent time above 2,000m (or at least understand what altitude does to your power output), this is one of the most rewarding days you will ever spend on a bike. If you are new to mountain sportives, start elsewhere — this is not a forgiving introduction.

**What to know before you enter:** Registration sells out within hours of opening, usually in autumn. The summit can be 5-10°C in early June while Bormio sits at 25-30°C — pack a gilet and arm warmers as non-negotiable. Run a compact crankset with at least a 34-tooth cassette. Use the [climb time calculator](/tools/climb-time) to estimate your Stelvio time based on your current W/kg, and the [VAM calculator](/tools/vam) to set realistic pacing targets for the steeper sections.

Read [our full Stelvio training guide](/blog/granfondo-stelvio-training-guide) for a 16-week build covering altitude pacing, gearing, fuelling, and race-day execution.

## Maratona dles Dolomites

**Distance:** 138km (long route) | **Climbing:** ~4,230m | **When:** First Sunday in July | **Start:** La Villa, Alta Badia

The Maratona might be the most beautiful day on a bike available to an amateur rider anywhere in the world. Seven Dolomite passes. UNESCO World Heritage scenery that looks like it was painted rather than formed by geology. Pale rock towers rising vertically from green meadows. Light that changes the colour of the mountains every twenty minutes.

The long route covers 138km with approximately 4,230m of climbing — seven passes including Campolongo, Pordoi, Sella, Gardena, Giau, Falzarego, and Valparola. The medium route drops two passes and takes 106km. The short route is 55km. All three share the same extraordinary roads and the same extraordinary organisation.

Getting in is the hard part. Over 40,000 riders apply each year for approximately 9,000 places. Entry is by lottery — you submit your application (typically between January and March), and the draw happens in April. Loyalty points for previous finishes improve your odds marginally, but for a first-timer, the acceptance rate sits around 20-25%. Apply every year. When the email arrives saying you are in, clear your calendar immediately.

The climbing is relentless but rarely vicious. Gradients tend to sit between 6-9% rather than the double-digit walls of the Stelvio. What accumulates is volume — 4,230m of ascending spread across seven efforts with descents between them that are fast, technical, and cold enough to need a gilet on the summit of the Pordoi (2,239m) even in July. The riders who have a strong day are the ones who respected the first three passes as warm-up, ate consistently from the start, and kept their [FTP zones](/tools/ftp-zones) honest on every climb rather than surging to match faster wheels around them.

Read [our Maratona training guide](/blog/maratona-dles-dolomites-training-guide) for a 14-week plan covering all seven passes, pacing across the full day, and how to handle the lottery process.

If you want to ride the Dolomite passes outside of the Maratona event, our [cycling in the Dolomites guide](/blog/cycling-in-the-dolomites-guide) covers the region in full.

## Nove Colli

**Distance:** 200km | **Climbing:** ~3,900m | **When:** Mid-May | **Start:** Cesenatico

Nove Colli — nine hills — is Italy's most iconic gran fondo, and the numbers explain why. Two hundred kilometres. Nine categorised climbs through the hills of Romagna. A start and finish in Cesenatico, the small Adriatic port town where Marco Pantani grew up and where cycling is not a hobby but a civic identity. Twelve thousand riders on the start line. A festival atmosphere that begins at the pasta party the night before and does not end until the last rider crosses the finish line well after dark.

The terrain is different from the Alpine events. There are no above-treeline moonscapes or altitude-related power losses. The Romagna hills are steep, punchy, wooded, and beautiful in May — gradients that ramp to 15-18% for short sections before easing, then ramping again. The cumulative effect across nine of them, spread over 200km, is a day that tests durability and fuelling discipline more than raw climbing power. You do not need 4.0 W/kg to finish Nove Colli. You need the ability to keep eating, keep drinking, keep pedalling steadily for eight to twelve hours while the May sun heats the tarmac and the road keeps tilting upward.

Pantani's presence is everywhere. The memorial statue on the seafront. The climb named after him. The locals who watched him train on these roads and who now cheer amateur riders up the same gradients with the same intensity. If you care about cycling history — and if you are reading this, you probably do — Nove Colli connects you to it in a way that a museum or a documentary cannot.

**What to know before you enter:** Registration opens in autumn and fills quickly — often within a day. The event runs in mid-May, which means warm-to-hot conditions (25-32°C) in the valleys. Run the [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) and the [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) for a 200km day in those temperatures. The nine climbs are individually manageable; it is the accumulation across a 200km day that catches riders who started too fast or ate too little. Treat the first 80km as a long warm-up and you will have a far better final 50km.

Read [our Nove Colli training guide](/blog/nove-colli-training-guide) for a 12-week plan covering pacing, fuelling, and how to manage nine climbs across a full-length gran fondo.

## L'Eroica

**Distance:** 46km to 209km (multiple routes) | **Climbing:** Varies by route | **When:** First weekend in October | **Start:** Gaiole in Chianti

L'Eroica is not like the other events on this list. It is not a test of fitness. It is not about power-to-weight ratios or optimal fuelling strategies or W/kg targets. It is about riding a pre-1987 steel bicycle with downtube shifters and toe clips along the white gravel roads of Tuscany, surrounded by vineyards and olive groves, while locals hand you wine and salami at unofficial feed stations set up in their farmyards.

The rules are specific: your bike must have been manufactured before 1987, with period-appropriate components. No clip-in pedals. No carbon. No electronic shifting. Downtube shifters and friction. The strade bianche — white roads — are unpaved, loose, and occasionally steep. They are the same roads that the professional Strade Bianche race uses in March, though L'Eroica covers different sections. The dust coats everything. The bike rattles. The scenery is absurdly beautiful.

Multiple routes range from 46km (accessible to almost anyone with basic fitness) up to 209km (a serious day on any bike, let alone a steel frame with six-speed friction shifting). Most riders choose the medium routes of 75km or 135km. The pace is social rather than competitive — although the long route does attract some quick riders who are also quick on vintage equipment.

I would recommend L'Eroica to any cyclist, regardless of whether they consider themselves competitive or fast. It strips the sport back to what drew most of us to it in the first place: a beautiful road, a simple machine, good company, and the feeling of getting somewhere under your own power. The fact that the "somewhere" is the Chianti hills in October, with the vineyards turning gold and the light going soft across the valleys, is a considerable bonus.

**What to know before you enter:** You need a qualifying bike. If you do not own a pre-1987 steel frame, Gaiole has rental options and local shops specialising in vintage builds — book early because demand outstrips supply. Registration opens in stages and sells out. October in Tuscany is mild (15-22°C) with occasional rain — dress in layers and accept that the gravel sections will be slower than you expect.

Read [our L'Eroica training guide](/blog/leroica-training-guide) for a 14-week preparation plan covering bike setup, strade bianche technique, and how to ride 100km+ on a bike that weighs 11kg and has no indexed shifting.

## Also Worth Considering

Italy's sportive calendar runs deep beyond the four flagship events. These are worth a look if you are building a season around Italian riding or if the big four are sold out.

**[Granfondo Campagnolo Roma](/blog/granfondo-campagnolo-roma-training-guide)** covers 145km with 2,200m of climbing, starting on Rome's historic roads before heading south into the Castelli Romani — the ring of volcanic hills built on an ancient caldera. The queen climb to Rocca di Papa (4.5km, 7.5% average, 14% max) separates the field after 40km of deceptively fast flat riding through the suburbs. October timing (18-24°C) makes it one of the most accessible Italian gran fondos, and the combination of Roman monuments and volcanic countryside is hard to match. Read our [full Roma training guide](/blog/granfondo-campagnolo-roma-training-guide) for a 12-week plan covering Rocca di Papa preparation, flat-to-steep transitions, and pacing strategy.

**Milan-San Remo Randonnee** lets you ride the Monument route — nearly 300km from Milan to the Ligurian coast, crossing the Turchino Pass and finishing on the Via Roma in San Remo. It is long rather than mountainous. The appeal is following the exact road book of one of cycling's oldest and most prestigious one-day races. A proper ultra-distance challenge for riders who have the endurance base.

**Granfondo Felice Gimondi** starts and finishes in Bergamo, with three significant climbs through the pre-Alpine foothills. Named after the Italian champion, it draws a loyal field and offers a well-organised event with a mid-tier difficulty that suits riders who are not yet ready for 4,000m of climbing but want more than a flat sportive.

**Granfondo Pinarello** is based in Treviso, in the Veneto flatlands north of Venice. The terrain is gentler — flat to rolling — and the event leans into the heritage of one of Italy's most famous bike brands. It suits riders who want an Italian gran fondo atmosphere without the mountain climbing, and it works well as a first Italian sportive for riders building toward harder events in subsequent years.

## Registration: Plan Early, Act Fast

Italian sportives do not wait. Registration patterns vary by event but share a common trait: demand exceeds supply, often dramatically.

**Maratona dles Dolomites** uses a lottery system. Applications open in January or February, close in March, and the draw happens in April. Over 40,000 people apply for roughly 9,000 places. Apply every year. Join cycling clubs that have group allocations. Accept that you may not get in on the first attempt — or the second.

**Granfondo Stelvio Santini** and **Nove Colli** open registration in the autumn preceding the event and typically sell out within hours. Set a phone alarm for the exact opening time. Have your details pre-filled and your payment method ready. Treat it like buying concert tickets for a band that will not tour again.

**L'Eroica** releases entries in waves across several months. Earlier waves sell fastest. The bike requirement creates a natural filter — fewer casual entrants — but the event's reputation means demand grows each year.

A practical approach: decide in September which events you want to target the following year. Note their registration dates. Enter everything you qualify for. Build your training plan around whatever you get into.

## Travel Logistics

**Airports:** Milan Malpensa and Milan Bergamo serve the Stelvio (3 hours by car to Bormio) and the Gimondi. Venice Marco Polo and Treviso serve the Maratona (2.5 hours to La Villa via the A22 autostrada) and the Pinarello. Bologna serves Nove Colli (1.5 hours to Cesenatico). Florence or Pisa serve L'Eroica (1.5 hours to Gaiole in Chianti). Rome Fiumicino for the Roma gran fondo.

**Bike transport:** Hard bike boxes offer the best protection on flights but are awkward on Italian trains and in small hire cars. Soft bike bags are easier to manage once you land. Italian trains accept bikes — Trenitalia regional services have dedicated bike spaces, but availability is limited and reservations are advisable. Some riders ship their bike ahead using specialist services and fly with hand luggage only.

**Driving:** Italian motorways are toll-based, well-maintained, and fast. Mountain roads to event starts like Bormio and La Villa are beautiful but take longer than the map suggests — hairpins, single lanes, and the occasional herd of cattle. Leave early. Allow extra time.

**Road culture:** Italian drivers are, in my experience, better with cyclists than their reputation suggests. The cycling culture runs deep — drivers in the Dolomites, Tuscany, and Romagna are accustomed to sharing roads with large groups. Standard road awareness applies, but the hostility you sometimes encounter on British or American roads is rare.

**Accommodation:** Book early. Event towns like Cesenatico, Bormio, and Gaiole are small, and twelve thousand gran fondo riders arriving in the same week depletes every hotel, Airbnb, and agriturismo within range. Booking at the same time as registration is not too early.

## When to Go: The Italian Sportive Calendar

The Italian sportive season runs from April through October, but the events cluster in two windows.

**May and June** are the peak months. Nove Colli falls in mid-May. The Stelvio runs in early June. These months offer warm temperatures in the valleys (22-30°C), long daylight hours, and — crucially — snow clearance on the high passes. The Stelvio and the Dolomite passes are typically not rideable until late May at the earliest due to snowpack, and some years the Stelvio road does not open until the week before the event.

**July** brings the Maratona dles Dolomites on the first Sunday — the passes are reliably clear, the days are long, and the Alta Badia region is at its most spectacular. July also brings heat in the lower elevations and crowds at the major tourist destinations, but the Maratona's organisation handles the logistics well.

**October** belongs to L'Eroica. Tuscany in autumn is one of the most beautiful places in Europe. Temperatures are mild (15-22°C), the light is golden, the vineyards are turning, and the tourist crowds have thinned. The risk is rain — an October shower on unpaved strade bianche turns the white roads into mud, which is either an adventure or a catastrophe depending on your temperament.

**April and September** offer shoulder-season opportunities for smaller events and self-organised riding. April can be wet and the high passes are closed. September is excellent in the Dolomites and the south.

For a first Italian sportive trip, May or June gives you the best combination of weather, daylight, pass accessibility, and event quality.

## Fuelling Italian-Style

This is where Italy wins. Not by a small margin — by a distance that no other country has seriously attempted to close.

The night before a gran fondo in Italy, you eat properly. Not a pre-packaged pasta meal in a conference room. A three-course dinner in a restaurant where the pasta is made that day, the sauce started that morning, and the bread came out of the oven an hour ago. Cesenatico the night before Nove Colli is twelve thousand cyclists filling every trattoria in town, eating pappardelle and piadina and drinking a controlled glass of red wine, and the restaurants treat it as a point of professional pride. I have eaten pre-race meals in the UK and France and Spain. Italy is a different league.

On the bike, Italian feed stations serve real food. Espresso — actual espresso, from a machine, in a ceramic cup — at multiple stops. Rice cakes. Fruit. Panini with prosciutto. Parmesan. Biscotti. The contrast with the gel-and-water-bottle stations at most northern European sportives is stark. You can, and should, supplement with your own nutrition strategy (run the [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) for your target carb intake), but the Italian approach to feed stations means you are less reliant on carrying everything in your jersey pockets.

After the event, recovery is gelato. This is not a joke and it is not a lifestyle choice. Gelato delivers fast-absorbing sugars, some protein from the dairy, and cold relief for a core temperature elevated by six to twelve hours of effort. The fact that it tastes like a reward rather than a recovery protocol is the point. Italian cycling culture understands that the food is part of the event, not a logistical requirement to be optimised away.

Plan your nutrition using the [fuelling calculator](/tools/fuelling) and [hydration calculator](/tools/hydration), but leave room for the feed station espresso. You have earned it.

## Building Your Italian Sportive Season

A well-planned Italian sportive season can cover two events in a single trip, depending on your calendar flexibility and recovery capacity. Nove Colli in mid-May followed by the Stelvio in early June gives you a three-week window — ride Nove Colli, recover for a week in the Italian countryside (a hardship), then travel north to Bormio for the Stelvio. The Maratona in July works as a standalone trip built around a week of riding in the Dolomites.

For riders new to Italian sportives, I would start with Nove Colli or L'Eroica. Nove Colli is a proper gran fondo — 200km, serious climbing — but the terrain is forgiving compared to the Alpine events and the atmosphere in Cesenatico is unbeatable. L'Eroica is entirely unique and has almost no fitness barrier at the shorter distances.

For experienced riders ready for a bucket-list event, the Maratona and the Stelvio are the two that will stay with you longest. The Maratona for the beauty. The Stelvio for the challenge. Both for the feeling of having ridden something that mattered.

Start with the event that excites you most, check the registration dates, and build your season backward from race day. Italy invented the gran fondo for a reason. No other country has the combination of roads, climbs, history, culture, and food that makes a long day on a bike feel like something more than exercise. Enter one. You will enter another.

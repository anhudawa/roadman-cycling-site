158 professional bike races. Eleven Tour de France stage wins. Seven at the Giro. Four at the Vuelta. Three national championships.

And every Tour de France stage bonus, given away. Split with the team. Every time.

That detail tells you more about sprint captaincy than anything you will ever read about lead-out trains.

[Listen to the full conversation on the Roadman Cycling Podcast →](/podcast/ep-2240-what-makes-a-sprinter-unbeatable-andr-greipel)

I sat down with [André Greipel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Greipel), one of the most prolific sprinters of the last twenty years and now retired into a different career — building helmets at UVEX, raising his kids, learning what the morning looks like when there is no training camp on the calendar.

The conversation is not really about sprinting. It is about what holds a sprint together, what burns it out, and what the modern WorldTour is asking of riders who have not yet figured out it is going to cost them.

## The Bonus Nobody Sees

Here is the thing nobody really tells you about a Tour de France sprint stage win.

There is a contract bonus attached. A real one. For a top sprinter at a top team, the number is meaningful. Greipel had one in his Lotto contract. Eleven Tour stages later, he had given every cent of it away.

He says it without theatre on the episode. Of course he shared it. The lead-out riders had killed themselves all day to put him into the last 200 metres. They had a bonus number in their contracts too — except theirs was zero. The maths was simple. They earned it. He earned enough.

That is sprint captaincy in one sentence. Not the team kit. Not the radio chatter. Not the diagram on the team bus the night before. The bonus.

The sprinter who shares is the sprinter who gets pulled back to the front on the bad days. The one who keeps the bonus is the one whose lead-out quietly stops believing. Same dynamic at any club squad.

## Kamikaze vs Gentleman

The other line in this episode worth printing on a wall.

Greipel divides the modern bunch into two groups. The "kamikaze" sprinters who will go for any gap, regardless of who is alongside them. And the "gentleman" sprinters who still want the win badly but will not take it at the cost of putting another rider into the barriers.

He places himself, fairly clearly, in the second category. He puts Dylan Groenewegen and Fabio Jakobsen in the first.

This is not a moral judgement. It is a description of how the bunch used to police itself.

When Greipel came up there were what he calls "respect persons" in the peloton. Cipollini. Petacchi. The riders who had been around long enough that if a young guy did something stupid in a sprint, a quiet conversation happened the next day. The threat of being put in the barrier yourself was the deterrent.

Most of those riders are retired now. What replaced them is a generation of young sprinters with enormous power, less experience, and no obvious authority figure telling them where the line is.

The fixable part of this is not the riders. They are doing what every ambitious young sprinter has always done. The fixable part is structural — race design, commissaires, and the willingness of senior riders to call out what they see.

Which brings us to Katowice.

## "I Just Never Dared to Sprint There"

Greipel finished plenty of editions of the Tour de Pologne. He never once sprinted in Katowice.

Eighty-five to ninety kilometres an hour, downhill, into a finish line, with a slipstream effect on top of that. He went to the commissaires every single year and told them the same thing — this sprint is insane, you cannot do this, this is not normal.

Nothing changed. Until 2020, when Groenewegen and Jakobsen collided, Jakobsen went into the barriers at the speed of a small motorbike, and the entire sport had a conversation it should have had years earlier.

The clean line on this is one Greipel and I both keep coming back to. You have to punish behaviour, not consequences. Because consequences are arbitrary. The same line by Groenewegen on a flat parcours at 60 km/h ends in nothing. The combination of his deviation and the race design at 90 km/h is what made it catastrophic.

If you only sanction the moments when someone gets hurt, you teach the bunch that the rule is "do not crash" rather than "do not deviate." Those are not the same lesson. One creates better racing. The other creates a lottery.

The race design point matters too. Putting a sprint at the top of that descent — making the climb the selection rather than the finish — would have been more interesting and significantly safer. Greipel had been telling commissaires for years.

## Six or Seven Years of "100 Percent of Everything"

The other big shift in the conversation is the one that should have every serious amateur listening to themselves a little harder.

Greipel says cycling has changed in the last six or seven years more than in the entire decade before. Not tactically. Professionally. Sleep monitored. Nutrition timed to the hour. Altitude camps stacked on training camps stacked on race blocks. Power meter on every ride. HRV in the morning. CGM if you are at a top team.

His view is brutal and simple. The body cannot do this for fifteen years. Neither can the head. He watched Tom Dumoulin walk away from the sport at the peak of his career and he understood it immediately. He does not think the current generation of Grand Tour winners will still be racing at 38 or 39. They will not need to — the earnings curve is so front-loaded now that financially they are set by 30, and the lifestyle they live to win is not one you sustain for a third decade.

The same dynamic plays out at the amateur level. The serious 45-year-old who tries to bolt every WorldTour habit onto a 12-hour week ends up in the same hole. Tracking everything. Sleeping badly. Stressed about a missed session. Riding tired. Performing worse. We have written about this in the [biggest training mistakes from the coaches we interview](/blog/biggest-training-mistakes-from-coaches) and the [80/20 grey zone breakdown](/blog/80-20-cycling-training-the-grey-zone-trap). Full gas, all the time, is not a strategy. It is a countdown.

Greipel's framing is the one to borrow. You need the balls — his word, not mine — to put a bit of distance between yourself and the bike sometimes. Not because you are not committed. Because you are.

## How Lotto Ended

The way Greipel left Lotto is the most ruthless small story in the episode.

He had finally made it over the Poggio at Milan-San Remo, his white whale, and crashed on the descent. Broken collarbone. Twelve weeks out. The same evening he came off the bike, a manager told him they would be in touch about a contract for the following year.

The contract never came. Twelve weeks later he was back. Won two stages at the Tour of Denmark. Finished second overall by one second. Two days before the Tour de France, he finally got an offer. One third of his previous salary, with a clause excluding Grand Tours and Monuments — the races he had built his entire career on.

He said no. He left. Caleb Ewan had already been signed without anyone telling him.

The lesson for amateurs is not about contracts. It is about what loyalty inside a sport actually buys you, which is sometimes less than you think. And it is about Greipel's response — he did not sulk. He went to a French team for a season, then to Israel, and finished his career on his own terms with the same number of bonuses given away as before.

A career is the long game. Individual contract years are not.

## The Helmet Conversation

A short note on the helmet section, because it matters.

Greipel's post-cycling work is with [UVEX](https://www.uvex-sports.com/), and the thing he keeps coming back to is how little incentive the major helmet brands have had to innovate around safety rather than aero or aesthetics. Most of the marketing energy goes into a 3-watt drag claim. Almost none of it goes into how the foam performs in a rotational impact at sprint speeds.

If you ride hard enough that a crash is a real possibility, your helmet is not the place to save USD 80.

## What Amateur Cyclists Can Take From This

You are not winning a Tour stage. You do not have a lead-out train. The principles still translate.

**1. The strongest rider rarely wins the most.** It is the one who builds the team that delivers them. If you race in any kind of squad — club, gravel team, tri club crit team — share the wins, share the prime money. The maths comes back over a season.

**2. Decide which type of sprinter you are, and stick to it.** If you are not comfortable with a 75 km/h gap that closes in half a second, do not chase one. Greipel won 158 races without ever being the most aggressive elbow in the bunch. The result on a Tuesday club run is not worth three months in plaster.

**3. Senior riders set the tone of the bunch.** If you are the most experienced rider on a club run, you are the respect person. Quietly correct the dangerous moves at the next coffee stop, not in the moment.

**4. "100% of everything" is not a strategy at any level.** Pick the few things that move the needle. Drop the rest. The [comeback cyclist 12-week return plan](/blog/comeback-cyclist-12-week-return-plan) is built on this principle.

**5. Build a career, not a season.** When Lotto offered Greipel one third of his salary he had every reason to take it. He said no and finished on his terms.

Everyone talks about FTP. It's the number that gets pinned to group ride invites, whispered at cafe stops, and entered into training apps like a credit score. Higher is better, obviously.

Except it isn't. Not always. Not even most of the time.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about the riders who consistently perform well in races, who finish sportives feeling strong rather than destroyed, who seem to get faster year after year while the rest of us plateau. They didn't just raise their ceiling. They raised their floor.

Your watt floor is the power you can hold almost indefinitely with a stable heart rate, minimal fatigue accumulation, and a perceived effort that barely registers. It's the pace where your body runs on fat, clears lactate as fast as it produces it, and doesn't start dipping into glycogen reserves that you'll need later. It sits at the top of your genuine zone 1 or the lower boundary of zone 2, depending on which model you follow.

And it matters far more than most cyclists realise.

## Why the Floor Matters More Than the Ceiling

Think about how a race or a long sportive actually plays out. You don't ride at FTP. Not even close. The vast majority of the event is spent at moderate to low intensity — sitting in the bunch, rolling through flat sections, recovering between climbs. The hard efforts — the attacks, the steep pitches, the final sprint — come in bursts that total maybe fifteen to twenty minutes across a four-hour event.

So what determines whether you survive those bursts and still have legs at the finish? It's not your FTP. It's how much the easy sections cost you.

A rider with an FTP of 280 watts and a watt floor of 140 is paying a completely different physiological price to cruise at 180 watts than a rider with the same FTP and a watt floor of 190. The first rider is already working above their efficient aerobic range just to hold the bunch on flat roads. Heart rate creeping. Glycogen burning. Fatigue accumulating, hour by hour, drip by drip. By the time the road tilts up at kilometre 120, they're already cooked.

The second rider? Sitting at 180 watts barely registers. Heart rate stable. Running predominantly on fat. Glycogen stores still topped up. When the road kicks, they've got something to give.

Same FTP. Completely different race.

Professor Stephen Seiler has been saying this for years. The floor of fitness — the sustainable aerobic base — is what underpins everything above it. You can have a sky-high FTP, but if your floor is low, you'll burn through your matches before the race even gets interesting. Seiler's polarised training model wasn't built to raise FTP directly. It was built to raise the floor, and let the ceiling follow.

## Aerobic Decoupling: The Number That Tells You Where Your Floor Sits

If you're not tracking [aerobic decoupling](/blog/aerobic-decoupling-cycling-cardiac-drift), you're missing the single most useful metric for understanding your watt floor.

Here's how it works. You ride at a steady, comfortable power for 60 to 90 minutes. You split the ride into two equal halves. You compare the ratio of power to heart rate in the first half versus the second half. If your heart rate stayed stable relative to your power output, the ratio holds. If your heart rate drifted upward while power stayed flat, the ratio drops. That drop, expressed as a percentage, is your aerobic decoupling.

Less than 5% over 90 minutes? Your aerobic base is doing its job at that intensity. Your watt floor is at or above the power you held.

Between 5% and 10%? The base is developing but not yet locked in. You're close to your floor but sitting slightly above it.

Above 10%? You've ridden above your watt floor. The aerobic system can't sustain that pace without cardiovascular drift, which means you're burning more glycogen than you need to, accumulating more fatigue than you need to, and paying a cost you shouldn't be paying.

The beauty of this metric is that it costs nothing. No lab. No lactate meter. Just a power meter, a heart rate strap, a route where you can ride steady, and the honesty to hold the effort constant rather than surging every time someone passes you.

TrainingPeaks calculates this automatically on any ride with power and heart rate data. But you can do it with a pen and a calculator if you want. The maths is simple.

Here's where it gets really interesting. Track your decoupling monthly at the same power output. As your watt floor rises, you'll see the percentage drop. The number shrinks because your cardiovascular system is becoming more efficient at supporting that workload. More capillaries delivering oxygen. More mitochondria processing fuel. Better stroke volume from the heart. The aerobic machinery is literally rebuilding itself to handle the load with less strain.

That shrinking number is the most honest measure of base fitness I've ever tracked. More honest than FTP. More honest than CTL. Because it tells you what your body can actually sustain, not what it can produce for twenty minutes before collapsing.

## Fat Oxidation: The Fuel Switch Nobody Talks About

Your body has two primary fuel sources during exercise. Fat and glycogen. Fat is almost unlimited — even a lean cyclist carries tens of thousands of calories of stored fat. Glycogen is limited — maybe 1,600 to 2,000 calories stored in the muscles and liver, and once it runs out, you bonk. Legs stop. Brain fogs. The ride is over.

The question is not whether you burn fat or glycogen. You always burn both. The question is what proportion of each you burn at a given intensity. And that proportion is directly tied to your watt floor.

Inigo San Millan — the physiologist behind Tadej Pogacar's training at UAE Team Emirates — has built his entire testing protocol around this concept. His lactate testing identifies two thresholds. The first, which he calls FatMax or the aerobic threshold, is the point of maximal fat oxidation — the intensity where your body burns the highest absolute amount of fat per minute. The second is the familiar lactate threshold, where lactate accumulation accelerates and you can't sustain the effort much longer.

Here's what San Millan found when he started testing elite versus amateur cyclists. The gap between those two thresholds told him almost everything about an athlete's long-duration potential. In Pogacar, that gap was remarkably narrow. His FatMax power sat very high relative to his threshold. Meaning even at significant absolute intensities, Pogacar's body was still burning primarily fat. His glycogen was being preserved for the moments when he needed it — the attacks, the final kilometres, the accelerations that win races.

In most amateur cyclists, that gap is enormous. FatMax might sit at 150 watts. Threshold at 280. That means the entire range between 150 and 280 is increasingly glycogen-dependent. Every time the group pushes the pace to 200 watts on a flat section, the amateur is dipping into a fuel source that cannot be replaced mid-ride. The pro is barely touching it.

Raising your watt floor is, in practical terms, pushing your FatMax power upward. You're training your body to oxidise more fat at higher intensities. The result is that you arrive at hard efforts with more glycogen in the tank, and you recover from those efforts faster because the baseline cost of riding is lower.

This isn't marginal. San Millan's research suggests that improvements in fat oxidation capacity are one of the strongest predictors of performance improvement over a training block. Riders who increased their FatMax power by even 10-15 watts saw disproportionate improvements in race-day performance because the downstream effects — glycogen preservation, lower heart rate, reduced perceived effort — compound over the hours.

## Durability: Why the Last Hour Is Where Races Are Won

There's a concept that's gained serious traction in sports science over the last few years, and it slots perfectly into the watt floor conversation. Durability.

Mark Burnley and his research group have defined durability as the resistance to fatigue-induced deterioration of physiological parameters during prolonged exercise. In plain language: how much does your performance drop as you get tired?

Most of us know this feeling intuitively. Fresh off the start line, an FTP effort feels hard but manageable. After three hours of riding, the same power output feels impossible. That's not just perception. Your actual physiological capacity has degraded. Your critical power drops. Your W prime — your anaerobic capacity above threshold — shrinks. Your lactate threshold shifts downward. The engine doesn't produce as much as it did when you were fresh.

And here's the critical finding from Burnley's work: the rate of that degradation is directly linked to the metabolic cost of the preceding hours.

Riders who spent those preceding hours working above their watt floor — burning glycogen, accumulating lactate, driving heart rate higher than necessary — showed dramatically faster performance decay than riders who kept the intensity below their aerobic ceiling.

Same total kilojoule expenditure. Same time on the bike. But the rider who stayed beneath their watt floor arrived at the decisive moments with a physiological system that was still close to baseline. The rider who floated above their floor arrived depleted.

This is why you see experienced racers sitting in the bunch, seemingly not working, hour after hour, and then producing devastating accelerations in the final climb. They weren't being lazy. They were being intelligent. They were riding below their watt floor, preserving durability, and timing their glycogen expenditure for the moments that mattered.

For the amateur riding a gran fondo or a sportive, the lesson is identical. The pace at hour one determines your legs at hour four. And the biggest single thing that determines how much that early pace costs you is where your watt floor sits.

## How to Test Your Watt Floor

You've got three options, ranging from free to expensive.

**Option 1: The decoupling test.** This is the one I use most often and recommend to most riders. Ride 90 minutes at a pace that feels properly easy — you should be able to hold a conversation without gasping, probably somewhere around 65-72% of FTP. Track [aerobic decoupling](/blog/aerobic-decoupling-cycling-cardiac-drift). If it's under 5%, that power is at or below your watt floor. Bump the power up by 5-10 watts next time and test again. Keep moving the needle until you find the point where decoupling starts to exceed 5%. That's your approximate watt floor.

**Option 2: The morning heart rate trend on long rides.** This is cruder but still informative. On a long steady ride, note the power at which your heart rate first starts to drift upward after an hour. Below that power, things are stable. Above it, the drift begins. The inflection point is a rough proxy for your watt floor.

**Option 3: A formal lactate test.** San Millan's protocol involves riding at progressively higher intensities while blood lactate is measured at each stage. The first notable rise in lactate — typically from around 0.8 to 1.5 mmol/L — marks the aerobic threshold, which corresponds closely to your watt floor. This gives you the most precise number and also identifies your fat oxidation crossover point. It costs money and requires a qualified tester, but if you're serious about tracking this over a season, one test at the start and one at the end of a base block tells you exactly how much the floor moved.

Whichever method you use, the number you get is your starting point. Write it down. It's going to change.

## The Training Approach: How to Actually Raise It

Here's where most cyclists go wrong. They know their easy pace matters. They've read about zone 2. They've heard Seiler talk about the 80/20 split. And then they go out and ride their "easy" rides 20 watts too hard, every single time.

I've done it. You've done it. Everyone has done it. The group ride starts easy and then someone pushes the front and suddenly you're at 85% of FTP telling yourself this still counts as zone 2. It doesn't. It never did.

Raising the watt floor requires riding below it. Not at it. Below it. The stimulus that drives mitochondrial biogenesis, capillary development, and improved fat oxidation happens at intensities where the aerobic system is the dominant energy pathway. That means zone 1 and the lower end of zone 2. For most riders, that's 60-72% of FTP. Maybe lower if your base is underdeveloped.

Let me break this down into a practical framework.

**Volume:** 3-4 rides per week at properly easy intensity. One of those should be your longest ride of the week — 2 to 3 hours if your schedule allows. The others can be 60-90 minutes. Total easy volume should represent 75-80% of your weekly training time.

**Intensity ceiling:** Below 75% of FTP on every easy ride. If you don't have a power meter, keep your heart rate below 75% of your lactate threshold heart rate. If you don't have that number, use the talk test — full sentences without gasping, no breaks in conversation flow.

**Consistency over heroics:** Six weeks of disciplined easy riding beats one week of massive volume followed by three weeks of being too tired to train properly. The adaptations are cumulative. Mitochondria don't grow overnight. Capillaries don't sprout in a week. The stimulus has to be repeated, regularly, for months.

**Hard days stay hard:** This isn't about eliminating intensity. Your 1-2 hard sessions per week — VO2max intervals, threshold work, race-pace efforts — stay in the programme. They drive the ceiling upward. But they can only do that if the easy days have protected your recovery enough to actually hit those sessions properly. If you're arriving at your Tuesday intervals with a heart rate that's already elevated from a too-hard Sunday ride, you're undermining both adaptations.

**Progression:** Every 4-6 weeks, retest your aerobic decoupling at the same power. If the number is dropping, the floor is rising. Once you consistently show less than 5% decoupling at your current easy pace, you can nudge the power target up by 5 watts and start the cycle again.

This is patient work. It's not glamorous. There's no suffering involved, which is precisely why it's so hard for competitive cyclists to commit to. We're wired to believe that effort equals progress. In this case, the opposite is true. Restraint equals progress.

## The Common Mistakes That Kill Your Watt Floor

I see the same errors, over and over, in the riders I work with and the questions that come through the [Roadman community](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling). Five patterns that keep the watt floor pinned down.

**Riding too hard on easy days.** This is the big one. The grey zone trap. You set out for an easy ride and end up averaging 78% of FTP because the road tilted up, or a mate appeared, or you just felt good and pushed on. Every minute above your aerobic ceiling on an easy day is a minute that doesn't build base and does accumulate fatigue. The adaptation requires discipline, not effort.

**Not enough total volume.** You can't build an aerobic base on three hours a week of easy riding. You can improve, but the adaptations are dose-dependent. More easy volume — to a point — produces faster results. If you're stuck at 4 hours a week, make sure every one of those hours is truly easy. If you can find 6-8 hours, the floor will rise noticeably faster.

**Inconsistency.** Two weeks of perfect zone 2 riding followed by a week of racing every group ride followed by another attempt at discipline. The aerobic adaptations need sustained, repeated stimulus. Four months of consistent easy riding will move the floor more than twelve months of sporadic attempts.

**Ignoring fuelling on easy rides.** This is counterintuitive for riders trying to lose weight, but riding easy while under-fuelled shifts your substrate utilisation in ways that undermine the adaptation. You need carbohydrates available for the aerobic system to function at its best, even on easy days. A banana and some water isn't enough for a three-hour base ride. Fuel the ride. Manage the deficit elsewhere in your day if weight loss is a goal.

**Expecting results in two weeks.** Mitochondrial density increases measurably in 4-6 weeks. Capillary development takes longer. Fat oxidation improvements are visible in 6-8 weeks. A meaningful shift in your watt floor — 10 to 20 watts — takes a committed 12-16 week base block. If you abandon the process after a month because you didn't see your FTP jump, you've missed the point entirely.

## What a Rising Watt Floor Actually Feels Like

Before we get into the race-day implications, I want to describe what this looks like from the saddle, because the numbers only tell half the story.

The first thing you'll notice, usually around week 4-6, is that your easy rides feel easier at the same power. Heart rate is lower. Breathing is quieter. You finish the ride and don't feel the need to collapse on the sofa. You could do the same ride again tomorrow. That's not fitness in the traditional sense — it's your aerobic system becoming more efficient at supporting the workload.

The second signal is that your hard days get better. The Tuesday intervals that used to leave you shattered now feel intense but recoverable. You're hitting the target numbers more consistently. The last rep is closer to the first rep. That's not because your VO2max improved. It's because you arrived at the session with lower residual fatigue, because the easy days are no longer draining you.

The third signal — and this is the one that matters most for racing — is that the back end of long rides changes. The final hour of a four-hour ride no longer feels like survival. Your pacing is more consistent. Your power doesn't fade in the last 30 minutes the way it used to. You finish and think "I could have gone longer." That's durability. That's the watt floor doing its job.

## The Race-Day Payoff

Let me paint the picture with a practical example.

You're riding a 140-kilometre sportive with 2,000 metres of climbing. The pace through the valleys is 180-200 watts. There are three significant climbs, each requiring 250-280 watts for 15-20 minutes. Total ride time: roughly five hours.

Scenario A: Your watt floor is 140 watts. Every time the bunch rolls at 180 watts, you're working at 40 watts above your efficient aerobic range. Your heart rate is elevated. Glycogen is being burned at a steady rate. By the second climb at hour three, your legs are heavy, your heart rate is higher than it should be at the same power, and you're reaching for gels because you can feel the fuel running low. The third climb is pure survival. You finish, destroyed, wondering why the fitness isn't there.

Scenario B: Your watt floor is 190 watts. The same 180-watt valley pace sits below your aerobic ceiling. Heart rate barely nudges above resting baseline. Fat is the dominant fuel source. You're essentially coasting while the bunch works. By the second climb, you feel the same as you did on the first. Glycogen stores are still healthy because you haven't been burning through them on the flats. The third climb is hard but manageable. You finish with something left.

Same rider. Same FTP. Different floor. Different race.

This is what Seiler means when he talks about the floor underpinning the ceiling. The ceiling is what you produce in a one-off maximal effort. The floor determines how much of that ceiling is available to you after four hours of riding. A rider with a lower FTP but a higher watt floor will often outperform a rider with a higher FTP and a lower floor over any event longer than an hour.

## The Connection to Long-Term Development

Here's the good news. Raising the watt floor isn't just a short-term performance play. It's the foundation for every other adaptation you want to build.

A higher watt floor means you can absorb more training volume without breaking down. Your recovery between sessions is faster because the easy rides actually cost you less. Your risk of overtraining drops because the autonomic stress from training is lower relative to your capacity. You can add intensity blocks on top of a strong base and see better returns from those blocks because the aerobic foundation supports the work above it.

San Millan's work with Pogacar illustrates this perfectly. The base-building blocks — months of high-volume, moderate-intensity training — weren't designed to raise Pogacar's FTP or VO2max directly. They were designed to push his aerobic threshold power upward so that when the race-specific intensity blocks came, his body could handle more work, recover faster, and produce peak performances at the moments that mattered.

The same principle applies to a 45-year-old amateur targeting their first Marmotte. The base block raises the floor. The intensity block raises the ceiling. But without the floor, the ceiling has nowhere to stand.

Joe Friel has written about this for decades. The base period isn't something you rush through to get to the "real" training. The base period is the real training. Everything that follows — build, peak, race — depends on how solid the foundation is. Skip it, and the house crumbles when you ask it to hold weight.

## Building Your Watt Floor Plan

I'll give you a practical 12-week framework. This isn't a full training plan — it's the watt floor component that sits alongside your existing programme.

**Weeks 1-4: Establish the baseline.**
- Test your decoupling at your current easy pace. Note the number.
- Ride 3-4 times per week below 72% of FTP. One long ride (2-3 hours), two to three shorter rides (60-90 minutes).
- Keep one hard session per week. No more.
- Focus on discipline. The only goal is staying below the ceiling on easy days.

**Weeks 5-8: Build consistency.**
- Retest decoupling at the same power. Compare to week 1.
- If decoupling has dropped below 5%, nudge your easy ride power up by 5 watts.
- Add 15-20 minutes to your longest ride if schedule allows.
- Maintain one hard session per week. Add a second if recovery supports it.
- Track morning resting heart rate. A downward trend confirms adaptation.

**Weeks 9-12: Consolidate and progress.**
- Retest decoupling again. You should see a meaningful drop from week 1.
- If your watt floor has risen by 10-15 watts, your aerobic base has measurably shifted.
- You can now begin layering in more intensity — two hard sessions per week — because the floor can support it.
- Continue the easy riding at the new, higher watt floor target.

The key metric across all 12 weeks is the decoupling number at a given power. Everything else — FTP, weight, Strava segments — is secondary. The floor is what you're building.

## The Patience Problem

I'll be honest. This is the hardest sell in cycling coaching. Riders come to me wanting to raise their FTP by 20 watts in 8 weeks. They want VO2max intervals and threshold sessions and race simulations. They want to suffer, because suffering feels like progress.

And sometimes that's exactly what they need. If you've got a solid base and your limiters are above threshold, then yes, intensity is the answer.

But most of the riders I talk to don't have a solid base. They have a high ceiling and a low floor. They can produce impressive numbers for 20 minutes and then fall apart in the fourth hour of a race. They've been chasing FTP for years while their watt floor has barely moved.

The fix is simple. Not easy, but simple. Ride slower. Ride longer. Ride more consistently. Track the numbers that actually tell you whether the base is growing. And give it time.

Twelve weeks of genuine base work will change how you ride. Not just how fast you ride — how the riding feels. The effort at 200 watts that used to leave you breathless will feel conversational. The long ride that used to require a day on the sofa will leave you ready to go again tomorrow. The climbs that used to blow you up will feel manageable because you arrived at them with a full tank instead of an empty one.

That's what a higher watt floor gives you. Not a bigger number on a test. A better experience on the bike, every single ride, for the rest of your cycling life.

The good news? Every rider has room to raise their floor. Every single one. Whether you're brand new to structured training or you've been racing for twenty years, the aerobic system responds to the right stimulus. The adaptations are real, measurable, and — here's the word I keep coming back to — fixable.

You don't need a coach. You don't need a lab. You don't need expensive equipment. You need a power meter, a heart rate strap, the discipline to ride easy when everything in you wants to push, and the patience to let twelve weeks of consistent work do what twelve weeks of consistent work always does.

Raise the floor. The ceiling will follow.

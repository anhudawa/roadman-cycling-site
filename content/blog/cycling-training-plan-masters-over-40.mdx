The cycling training plan most masters riders are following was not written for them. It was written for a 28-year-old, then scaled down — fewer hours, easier intervals, an extra rest day taped on the end. That is not a masters plan. It is a generic plan with the volume turned down.

A useful masters plan is built around the current rider. Recovery may need more space, strength counts toward total load, and intensity distribution should reflect the event and available hours. None of those decisions can be made from age alone.

The schedule below is a coaching starting point used with riders in [Roadman coaching](/coaching) and the [Not Done Yet community](https://www.skool.com/roadmancycling). It is not a validated universal week. If you want the general-purpose version first, the [training plan built from Friel, Lorang and Johnson](/blog/cycling-training-plan-build-friel-lorang-johnson) lays out the underlying framework, while the [complete guide to cycling over 40](/blog/cycling-over-40-complete-guide) covers the wider context. Use this as a template, then adjust it to your event, history, available time, health context and response.

## The weekly architecture

First establish the rider brief: goal, recent consistent volume, training age, current capacity, available days, injury and health context, and the signals that will trigger a change.

For an experienced rider who already tolerates structured training, one example looks like this:

| Day       | Session                                        | Why                                 |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Monday    | Recovery ride or full rest                     | Cleans up Sunday's load             |
| Tuesday   | Hard session 1 — VO2 or threshold              | Body is rested, intensity available |
| Wednesday | Endurance + strength session 1                 | Build aerobic base, protect muscle  |
| Thursday  | Easy ride or rest                              | Two-day buffer before Saturday      |
| Friday    | Endurance + strength session 2                 | Aerobic load, second strength dose  |
| Saturday  | Hard session 2 — long endurance with intensity | The signature ride of the week      |
| Sunday    | Long endurance, conversational pace            | True zone 2, no grey-zone drift     |

This example sits between 8 and 12 hours, uses mostly low-intensity riding, has two quality days and includes two strength sessions. Those are example inputs, not masters requirements. [Stephen Seiler's review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20861519/) described successful high-volume endurance athletes doing most sessions at low intensity; it did not prove an 80/20 time split for masters cyclists. A [2025 individual-participant meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/) found no overall polarised-versus-pyramidal winner for its stated outcomes.

If Sunday becomes a hard ride, move or remove the Tuesday quality session according to the rider's response. If you have fewer hours, protect the sessions most specific to the goal and preserve enough easy work or rest to execute them. If strength is new, begin with one tolerable exposure. The schedule serves the rider; the rider does not owe the schedule every box.

## Why start with one or two quality sessions

Quality work has a high opportunity cost: it must be specific enough to move the goal and recoverable enough to repeat. One or two sessions is therefore a conservative starting range for many experienced riders, not an age-specific threshold.

A [review of recovery and ageing](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18268815/) found plausible mechanisms for differences, but also limited athlete-specific evidence, activity confounding and few studies using normal training stimuli. It does not establish a universal 48-to-72-hour clock. Decide whether to repeat quality from execution, unusual symptoms, soreness, sleep, mood and life stress compared with the rider's own baseline.

When quality stops being repeatable, remove or reduce the least goal-specific demanding session and keep easy days genuinely easy. [Dan Lorang's](/guests/dan-lorang) Roadman interview provides an elite-coaching perspective on protecting session purpose; it is not masters-cyclist trial evidence.

Some event-specific blocks may contain a third demanding exposure, including a race, but it should have a clear purpose and a pre-planned reduction if execution or recovery deteriorates. There is no automatic two-week dose or mandatory recovery-week formula.

## Volume targets and the role of zone 2

The purpose of an easy session is to add tolerable aerobic work or promote movement without compromising the next priority. That purpose matters more than defending a label on the head unit.

A practical low-intensity starting point is a pace where breathing remains controlled and conversation is comfortable. Fixed percentages of maximum heart rate do not identify physiological thresholds accurately for every rider, so use tested thresholds when available and cross-check heart rate, power and perceived effort.

The [HR zones tool](/tools/hr-zones) and [FTP zones tool](/tools/ftp-zones) provide estimates from your inputs, not laboratory thresholds. Use them as starting ranges and keep the session aligned with its purpose rather than assuming a 10-watt change switches one adaptation off.

Long endurance rides can include event-specific work when that is the priority, but those efforts still count toward the week's demanding load. Keep the following day easy or take it off if the long ride compromises execution. Group or solo is a practical choice, not a physiological rule.

## Strength as a load, not an extra

Strength belongs in the load calculation rather than being added after the bike week is full. [Sarcopenia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30427277/) describes age-related loss of muscle mass and function, but a population rate is not a diagnosis and it does not prove one cyclist programme.

A [2025 meta-analysis of 17 studies and 262 adult cyclists](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40632222/) found that heavy strength training improved some cycling outcomes, found no VO2max effect and rated the evidence low certainty. It did not establish an over-40 subgroup effect or one optimal programme. One or two sessions can be a starting range. Choose movements and load from competence, injury and medical context, equipment, experience and the surrounding bike work.

Strength can sit on an easy day, a hard day or a separate day depending on the goal and schedule. Whichever pattern you choose, preserve the priority session and change the dose if technique, bike execution or recovery worsens. The [strength evidence guide for masters cyclists](/blog/new-study-confirms-heavy-strength-training-beats-more-miles-after-40) has a longer breakdown.

Roadman's [strength plan for cyclists](/strength-training) provides structured variants for riders who want a ready-made starting point. It still needs to be fitted to the rider's experience and total load.

## Choosing a recovery-week rhythm

There is no masters-only build-to-recovery ratio established by the evidence. A 2:1 or 3:1 calendar can organise a first block, but the calendar is a hypothesis to test.

A recovery week commonly reduces volume and demanding work while keeping some familiar movement. The useful reduction depends on the prior load and response; it is not automatically 30% or the same for cycling and strength.

Review several signals against the rider's baseline: unusual symptoms, deteriorating session execution, persistent soreness, disrupted sleep, mood and life stress. HRV, resting heart rate and the [masters recovery score tool](/tools/masters-recovery-score) can add context, but one number is not a diagnosis. A Performance Management Chart in [TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com) models training load; it does not directly measure readiness or prove when a masters rider must back off.

After a high-stress block, hot-weather camp, illness or race, resume progression only when the wider pattern supports it. Persistent or concerning symptoms require qualified medical advice.

## Periodisation across the year

An example annual outline for a rider with a summer target might look like this:

- **October to December — base 1**: Emphasise tolerable aerobic volume, movement competence and progressive strength.
- **January to February — base 2**: Add goal-relevant quality gradually while preserving repeatability.
- **March to April — build**: Increase event specificity and adjust strength or volume to protect priority sessions.
- **May — taper and prep**: Reduce volume by 20-30 per cent, retain intensity, sharpen race-specific work.
- **June to August — race season**: Maintain intensity, lower volume, race regularly. Strength stays at one session per week.
- **September — transition**: Unstructured riding, commute volume, light gym, mental reset.

The dates, phase lengths and load distribution shift with the event and the rider. The [structuring a cycling training plan guide](/blog/how-to-structure-cycling-training-plan) has more detail on build-and-peak mechanics, and the [structured masters training plan over 40](/blog/masters-cycling-training-plan-over-40) turns the outline into three adaptable 12-week examples for a gran fondo, masters racing or a comeback.

For an event-specific plan — a 12- or 16-week build for a sportive — start from the [training plans hub](/topics/cycling-training-plans), then apply the same rider brief and response rules.

## Where this plan goes wrong

Three failure modes account for most of the masters plans that break down inside a season.

The first is volume creep. The plan starts at 10 hours, drifts to 12, then 14, because each additional hour feels productive in the moment. Fix: log weekly hours, ask whether each addition serves the goal and reverse the change if execution or recovery deteriorates.

The second is intensity creep on easy days. Sunday's three-hour ride starts at zone 2, drifts into zone 3 because the legs feel good, finishes at sweet spot because someone else turned up. Repeated weekly, this hollows out the entire polarised structure. Fix: solo on Sunday, calibrate the upper edge of zone 2 with the [HR zones tool](/tools/hr-zones), and accept that the easy ride is supposed to feel underwhelming.

The third is treating strength as invisible load. When work or family demand rises, decide explicitly whether to reduce cycling, strength or both. A short, familiar strength exposure may preserve continuity for some riders; others should remove it temporarily. The rider's goal and response decide.

## The version of you that is not done yet

A plan that works at 45 is not the plan that worked at 25, scaled down. It is a different shape, with different ratios, written for a body that responds differently. Run your numbers through the [age grade calculator](/tools/age-grade) to see how your current power stacks up against your age-adjusted ceiling — the gap is usually wider than you think. The [masters FTP benchmark](/tools/masters-ftp-benchmark) adds the peer comparison — where your FTP sits relative to other riders in your age bracket. The riders who get this right are not the most talented or the most disciplined. They are the ones whose plan matches their physiology.

Heat training is most credible when it has a specific job: preparing a cyclist
for conditions that will be hotter than normal training. Repeated, progressive
exposure can reduce the shock of riding or racing in heat. It is not a guaranteed
FTP shortcut, and discomfort is not proof that the dose is right.

## What Heat Acclimation Is

**Heat acclimation** uses simulated hot conditions. **Heat acclimatisation** happens
in the natural environment. Both aim to make the target conditions less novel.
Common adaptations can include an earlier sweat response and lower cardiovascular
or perceptual strain at a comparable workload in the heat, but their size and speed
vary between riders.

Expert consensus commonly describes repeated exercise-heat exposure across roughly
one to two weeks. That is a planning range, not a universal daily schedule or
permission to copy a professional's temperature target.

## What the Evidence Does—and Does Not—Show

The strongest use case is hot-event preparation. Evidence for a general FTP or
cool-condition performance gain is mixed. A meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials
found a possible time-trial benefit but no significant pooled improvement in
VO2max, plasma volume or several other proposed markers. Most participants were
men.

The frequently cited haemoglobin result came from 18 male elite cyclists after an
altitude camp. Heat sessions helped that group maintain altitude-related
haemoglobin mass; the study did not show that every amateur gains 20 or 30 watts.

Sauna and hot-water research is also small and mostly male. These methods may
change some physiological markers, but pooled performance effects are uncertain.
There is no evidence-based public bath, sauna or space-heater prescription that
fits every cyclist.

## Planning for a Hot Event

Start with the event, not the method:

1. Review expected temperature, humidity, sun, wind, start time, aid stations and
   the organiser's emergency plan.
2. Compare those demands with the rider's recent training environment and health
   history.
3. Use natural acclimatisation where practical or ask a qualified professional to
   design progressive simulated exposure.
4. Protect key workout quality. Heat is additional stress and should not simply be
   stacked onto hard intervals.
5. Set cooling, communication and stop rules before the session begins.
6. Rehearse pacing, clothing, sunscreen, fluid access and fuelling in representative
   conditions.

The [hot-event acclimation guide](/blog/cycling-heat-acclimation-protocol-guide)
explains how to make those decisions without presenting one unsupervised dose as
safe for everyone.

## Indoor Cycling and Cooling

For ordinary indoor training, strong airflow usually helps protect workout quality.
Turning off a fan raises thermal strain, but that fact alone does not make a useful
heat protocol. A closed room, extra clothing, a heater and a consumer core sensor
do not reproduce a supervised environmental laboratory.

If simulated heat is justified, separate the purpose of the exposure from the
purpose of the workout. Review the rider's recovery, illness status, hydration and
next session. Consumer temperature wearables may show trends but are not medical
diagnostic devices and should never overrule symptoms.

For trainer-room comfort and normal workout quality, use the [indoor heat-management guide](/blog/indoor-cycling-heat-management-trainingpeaks-virtual).

## Hydration Is Individual

Both inadequate and excessive fluid intake can cause problems. Sweat rate changes
with the rider, workload, environment and acclimation. Use thirst, representative
pre/post body-mass observations, conditions and access to set a range; do not force
one bottle count or drink enough to gain body mass.

Sodium belongs inside the complete food-and-fluid plan. It does not make excessive
drinking safe. Use the [cycling hydration guide](/blog/cycling-hydration-guide) and
[hydration calculator](/tools/hydration) to organise field observations, and seek a
qualified sports dietitian or clinician when the decision is high stakes.

## Heat Illness: When the Session Ends

Stop riding and move to a cooler place for faintness, weakness, dizziness, nausea,
headache, unusual irritability or deteriorating coordination. Do not finish the
interval.

Confusion, loss of coordination, seizure, collapse or loss of consciousness during
heat exposure is an emergency. Call local emergency services immediately and begin
rapid cooling while following dispatcher or trained medical instructions. Do not
give fluid to someone who is not fully conscious and able to swallow.

Sweating status alone cannot diagnose heat stroke. Neither can a consumer wearable.
The [cycling heat-illness guide](/blog/cycling-heat-illness-prevention-guide) covers
recognition, response and the limits of self-assessment.

## Masters and Individual Risk

Age alone does not create one safe protocol. Health, fitness, previous heat
exposure, medication, pregnancy and prior heat illness can all change the decision.
Do not begin deliberate heat exposure while ill, feverish, dehydrated or unusually
fatigued. Seek medical advice when a condition or medicine may affect heat tolerance
or fluid balance.

## Roadman's Position

Specific preparation for a hot event is credible. A guaranteed watt claim is not.
The best plan matches the environment, preserves the wider training programme and
has a clear safety response. The goal is to arrive prepared—not to prove how much
heat a rider can tolerate in a spare room.

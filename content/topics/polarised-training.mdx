This is the Roadman learning path for **polarised training in cycling**. It is a navigation hub, not a second complete definition. Start with the main evidence-led guide, then follow the branch that matches the decision you need to make.

## 1. Understand the model

Begin with [Polarised Training for Cycling: The 80/20 Model Explained](/blog/polarised-training-cycling-complete-guide).

That guide owns the broad question. It explains:

- the three-zone model used in training-intensity-distribution research;
- why “80/20” is a description rather than an exact weekly law;
- how session-goal and time-in-zone calculations can classify the same training differently;
- what current cyclist-specific reviews can and cannot establish; and
- how to audit a programme without prescribing a universal number of hard days.

The essential distinction is between **predominantly low intensity** and a perfect percentage. Name the zones and counting method before interpreting the chart.

## 2. Compare training distributions

If the decision is between two models, use a comparison page rather than the broad guide:

- [Polarised or pyramidal training?](/answers/polarised-or-pyramidal-training) explains the difference between a smaller high-intensity share and a larger moderate-intensity share.
- [Polarised vs sweet spot training](/blog/polarised-vs-sweet-spot-training) compares two different programming questions without calling either universally best.
- [Sweet spot vs threshold vs polarised](/blog/sweet-spot-vs-threshold-vs-polarised-comparison) helps separate workout intensity from whole-programme distribution.
- [The WorldTour training-distribution study](/blog/polarised-training-cycling-world-tour-prescription) examines what professional cyclists recorded across phases rather than turning elite observations into an amateur prescription.

Polarised and pyramidal programmes both contain mostly low-intensity work. Current comparative evidence does not justify promising one winner for every rider, phase and outcome.

## 3. Set the zones you will actually use

Research Zone 1, Zone 2 and Zone 3 are not the same labels as the seven power zones on a cycling computer. Use these resources to avoid mixing systems:

- [Cycling training zones explained](/blog/ftp-training-zones-cycling-complete-guide)
- [Zone 2 cycling: heart rate vs power vs RPE](/blog/zone-2-cycling-heart-rate-vs-power-vs-rpe)
- [Home lactate-threshold testing](/blog/lactate-threshold-home-test-cyclists)
- [FTP zone calculator](/tools/ftp-zones)
- [Heart-rate zone calculator](/tools/hr-zones)

Record the threshold method and keep it consistent. A precise-looking distribution built from unstable boundaries is still unstable.

## 4. Choose the hard work by purpose

“High intensity” is a distribution category, not one interchangeable workout. Choose the session from the adaptation and event demand:

- [VO2max intervals for cycling](/blog/cycling-vo2max-intervals)
- [Threshold intervals for cycling](/blog/cycling-threshold-intervals-guide)
- [Cycling interval session builder](/tools/interval-builder)

Count races, hard group rides, demanding strength work and other sports before adding another hard cycling session. Do not manufacture intensity merely to make a weekly chart equal 20%.

## 5. Investigate the grey-zone claim

Moderate training is not an empty physiological space. The useful question is whether its dose serves the event and phase, and what it displaces.

- [80/20 cycling and the grey-zone trap](/blog/80-20-cycling-training-the-grey-zone-trap) looks at accidental intensity drift.
- [What cycling podcasts got wrong about polarised training](/blog/what-cycling-podcasts-got-wrong-about-polarised-training) separates memorable slogans from evidence.

If moderate work repeatedly damages recovery or high-intensity quality, reduce the unplanned dose. If it is deliberate, event-specific and well tolerated, its existence does not make the programme a failure.

## A simple decision sequence

1. Define the three-zone model and thresholds.
2. Choose session goal or time in zone and state it.
3. Audit several representative weeks, including races and other sports.
4. Identify the actual problem: excess unplanned moderate work, poor hard-session quality, missing specificity or poor recovery.
5. Change the smallest useful variable.
6. Judge the result through repeatable performance and recovery, not ratio compliance.

**Cycling cadence is pedal revolutions per minute (rpm). It changes the torque required on each stroke, the number of pedal cycles, cardiovascular cost and how gearing translates effort into road speed. There is no universal ideal cadence. Start with the rhythm you select naturally, then test a nearby range for the power, terrain and duration that matter.**

## The short answer

Many trained road cyclists ride steady terrain somewhere in a broad 80–95 rpm range, but that observation is not a target every rider must hit. Cadence normally moves with power, gradient, gearing, fatigue and body position. Sustained climbing is commonly lower than flatter riding. Sprint and track cadences solve different force–velocity and gearing problems.

Small studies of experienced and elite cyclists generally support self-selected cadence as a useful starting point. They do not establish 90 rpm as a universal optimum. The [general cadence guide](/blog/cycling-cadence-optimal-guide) shows the current evidence and a repeatable comparison process.

## What high and low cadence change

At the same external power:

- higher cadence reduces crank torque per stroke but increases leg speed and the number of cycles;
- lower cadence increases torque per stroke but reduces the number of cycles;
- cardiovascular, muscular and coordination costs do not change identically;
- a drill cadence is not automatically the best all-day cadence.

The useful question is therefore not “Is high or low cadence better?” It is “Which cadence serves this rider and this task without creating a larger cost elsewhere?”

## Low-cadence training: promising, protocol-specific evidence

A 2024 PLOS ONE trial compared two eight-week polarised programmes in 24 well-trained female cyclists. The group performing high-intensity and sprint intervals at 50–70 rpm improved several aerobic outcomes more than the freely chosen-cadence group. That is a useful result from a small, specific population and programme—not proof that every rider should grind at low cadence.

An earlier 12-week study in 22 trained veteran cyclists found no benefit from twice-weekly moderate intervals at 40 rpm compared with added freely chosen-cadence training. Intensity, cadence range, sex, training status and the rest of the programme differed.

Roadman's position is to publish the exact protocol and population, preserve conflicting evidence and avoid turning a relative group result into guaranteed percentage gains for an individual.

## Climbing cadence is also a gearing decision

Cadence often falls on a climb because road speed drops and the drivetrain may run out of lower gears. Before prescribing a leg-speed change, check whether the bike has a gear that allows the intended cadence at the rider's power and gradient.

Use the [gear-ratio calculator](/tools/gear-ratio) to compare chainring and cassette combinations. The [climbing cadence guide](/blog/best-cadence-for-climbing) covers the narrower performance decision.

## Cadence after 40

There is no validated table that assigns a different target rpm to riders in their 40s, 50s or 60s. Age can change health, strength, recovery and training history, but it does not prove that every masters rider should add 5–10 rpm.

Higher cadence can reduce some per-stroke knee-load measures, while newer research shows that cumulative exposure also depends on power, cycle count and the individual joint. Cadence is not a universal treatment for knee pain or osteoarthritis.

## A practical process

1. Record the rider's natural cadence at a repeatable moderate power.
2. Check gearing before treating a forced low cadence as a technique problem.
3. Compare a small range—roughly five rpm below, at and above the baseline—under consistent conditions.
4. Record heart rate, breathing, perceived muscular cost, control and symptoms.
5. Repeat before changing a default cadence.
6. Use high- or low-cadence drills only for a named training purpose.

This is a field process, not a laboratory or medical diagnosis. Persistent pain, swelling, weakness, numbness or loss of function needs appropriate assessment.

## Suspension Setup: Fork and Shock

Suspension setup begins with product identity, not a discipline-wide percentage
table. Confirm the fork and shock model, generation, travel or stroke, bicycle
tune and every limit. Take starting pressure and target sag from the exact
bicycle/component source; then measure sag in full riding kit.

Sag in millimetres equals fork travel or shock stroke multiplied by the target
percentage. Rebound and compression come after spring pressure and sag. Their
click counts depend on the installed damper and must be counted from the exact
manual's reference position—not copied from another rider or a generic range.

## Tyre Pressure and Tyre Choice

Tyre pressure is a separate system. Complete rider/bike load, measured mounted
width, casing, rim, insert, tube/tubeless configuration, terrain and the permitted
tyre-rim range all matter. A rider-weight-only table cannot establish a safe or
optimal pressure, and tubeless is not mandatory for every rider or wheel system.

Confirm tyre/rim compatibility and manufacturer limits first, calculate a
starting point, then test one small front or rear change at a time. Stop lowering
if the tyre squirms, bottoms, burps or loses support.

## Dropper Post Setup

The dropper is the single most-impactful component since the dual-suspension frame. Setup decisions:

- **Travel:** match to your inseam — drop at full extension should put your knee at a comfortable, slight bend.
- **Height:** climbing position is the same as a fixed seatpost; descending position is fully dropped.
- **Lever placement:** under the bar, accessible without changing grip.

Riders new to droppers under-use them. Drop fully on every steep descent, every technical drop, every loose corner; raise back for any sustained climb.

## The MTB Skills Hierarchy

Not all skills transfer equally. The order they pay off:

1. **Body position fundamentals** — neutral and ready stance, weight off the saddle on descents, low body on flat corners.
2. **Braking** — front-brake-heavy braking before turns, off the brakes through the corner.
3. **Cornering** — outside foot down, look through the turn, lean the bike not the body.
4. **Pumping rolling terrain** — generating speed without pedalling.
5. **Manuals and bunny hops** — clearing trail features without slowing down.
6. **Drops and small jumps** — committing to the lip, landing both wheels.

The amateur shortcut is paying for one weekend MTB skills coaching. It compresses six months of self-taught fumbling into a day.

## Maintenance Fundamentals

The maintenance schedule that keeps an MTB rolling:

| Frequency | Task |
|-----------|------|
| Every ride | Tyre pressure check, chain lube on wet rides |
| Weekly | Full bike wash, drivetrain degrease and re-lube |
| Monthly | Brake pad check, suspension air pressure check, headset/bearing check |
| Product interval | Suspension service and approved lubrication tasks from the exact component manual |
| Setup-dependent | Tubeless sealant, bearing and dropper checks from the relevant manufacturer guidance |

Use the service interval and procedure for the exact fork, shock and riding
conditions. If there is leakage, damage, abnormal noise or unexpected pressure
loss, stop riding and arrange qualified inspection rather than waiting for a
calendar interval.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the most important MTB setup change?**
Make the setup traceable: identify the exact product, use the correct source and measure the result. For suspension, sag is the first measurable target after a supported starting pressure.

**What tyre pressure should I run on a mountain bike?**
Use complete system load, measured tyre width, casing and terrain to calculate a starting point, then remain inside the lowest tyre/rim limits and test small changes. Rider weight alone is not enough.

**Do I need a full-suspension bike?**
Not automatically. Terrain, intended use, skill, comfort, maintenance capacity and budget all matter. A hardtail and a full-suspension bike solve different trade-offs; neither format is required for every trail rider.

**How much travel do I need on my fork?**
Use only travel approved for the frame and fork. The right amount depends on bike geometry, intended use, terrain and handling priorities; changing travel can alter geometry and loads, so confirm compatibility with the frame and fork manufacturers.

**Are tubeless tyres worth the hassle?**
They can remove the inner-tube pinch-flat mode and permit useful pressure choices, but compatibility, bead retention, casing support, sealant maintenance and roadside repair still matter. They are not automatically the right answer for every wheel and rider.

**How fit do I need to be for MTB?**
Fitness requirements depend on route length, climbing, technical difficulty, weather and the pace of the group. Choose a route with a conservative bailout option, ride within control and build duration and technical exposure progressively.

**Cycling nutrition is built on three layers: daily fuelling that supports the training week, in-ride carbohydrate matched to the session, and recovery nutrition that supports adaptation. For longer performance rides, 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour is a common starting range; events beyond roughly 2.5 hours may use up to 90 g/h when the plan has been practised. Higher intakes are an advanced, event-specific option rather than a default target.**

Cycling nutrition advice has moved away from one daily prescription toward fuelling for the work required. Carbohydrate during exercise, daily energy availability, protein distribution, recovery time and sleep all depend on the rider and the training demand. This hub routes each question to its distinct guide and separates established ranges from developing high-intake practice.

## Daily Fuelling: Eat to Train, Train to Eat

The single most important shift in modern cycling nutrition is the move from a fixed daily calorie target to *[fuelling for the work required](/blog/fuel-for-the-work-required-fftwr-explained)*. The idea is simple: eat to support tomorrow's training, not to enforce an arbitrary deficit.

**Practical baseline for an amateur training 8-12 hours per week:**

| Macro | Daily Target |
|-------|-------------|
| Carbohydrate | 4-6 g/kg on easy days, 6-10 g/kg on hard/long days |
| Protein | 1.6-2.2 g/kg every day (split across 4-5 meals) |
| Fat | Fill the rest — focus on whole foods, omega-3s, olive oil |

Energy availability — calories in minus exercise calories burned, divided by lean body mass — should sit at or above 30 kcal/kg/lbm/day. Drop below 30 and you start to lose hormonal function, recovery capacity, and (eventually) bone density. Most under-fuelled cyclists cluster between 20 and 28 — fast in week one, broken by week eight.

## In-Ride Nutrition: How Much, What, When

Start with the demand of the ride rather than the intake reported by another athlete:

| Ride Duration / Intensity | Carbohydrate Target |
|---------------------------|---------------------|
| Easy or short ride | A gel may be unnecessary when the rider starts adequately fuelled |
| Longer or harder endurance work | 30–60 g/hour is a common evidence-based range |
| Event beyond roughly 2.5 hours | Up to about 90 g/hour can be useful when practised |
| Selected high-performance event | Intakes around 120 g/hour are developing practice for prepared athletes, not a universal target |

At higher intake rates, combining glucose or maltodextrin with fructose can increase exogenous carbohydrate delivery compared with glucose alone. Neither a 2:1 nor 1:0.8 ratio guarantees tolerance or performance by itself. The [cycling gels guide](/blog/cycling-energy-gels-guide) explains how to read a packet and count every carbohydrate source in the same hourly plan.

**[Practise the event plan.](/blog/gut-training-cycling-absorb-more-carbs)** Repeated feeding practice may improve tolerance, but responses are mixed and there is no evidence-backed schedule that guarantees a particular dose. Record carbohydrate, fluid, intensity, heat and symptoms, then change one variable at a time.

## Recovery Nutrition: The 0-4 Hour Window

The biggest recovery wins are won in the four hours after a hard or long session. The structure that works:

- **0-30 minutes:** 1.0-1.2g/kg carbohydrate plus [20-40g protein](/blog/protein-for-cyclists-complete-guide). Liquid is fine if appetite is suppressed.
- **30-120 minutes:** A balanced meal — carbohydrate, protein, vegetables. This is the meal that does the structural work.
- **2-4 hours:** A second carbohydrate-rich meal, especially before back-to-back hard days.

Sleep is the second half of recovery and is non-negotiable. 7-9 hours, with the same wake time daily, beats every recovery supplement on the market.

## Race Weight Without Losing Power

The cyclists who get to race weight cleanly do four things:

1. **Pick a realistic target.** A 2-4% drop in body weight without losing power is the achievable end of the curve. More than that risks performance and health.
2. **Lose weight in the off-season, not in race build-up.** A small daily deficit (200-400 kcal) over 8-12 weeks moves the dial without compromising key sessions.
3. **Protect protein and fuelling around hard rides.** Cut carbs on rest days; never on quality days.
4. **Strength train through it.** Heavy lifting protects lean mass and maintains the power side of the W/kg equation.

If your weight loss strategy is "eat less, ride more", you're optimising for short-term scale movement and long-term plateau. The smarter play is body composition — fat down, muscle stable, power preserved.

## Hydration: Measure the Ride, Keep a Safe Range

There is no universal bottle-per-hour rule. Short rides in cool or moderate
conditions can often be managed by thirst. Long, hard or hot events may justify
a measured plan built from representative pre/post body mass, drink intake,
urine, conditions and duration.

- **Measure:** use the [cycling sweat-rate calculator](/tools/hydration) and
  repeat the observation in the conditions that matter.
- **Plan:** treat sweat rate as an upper boundary, not an automatic instruction
  to replace every millilitre.
- **Audit:** avoid both substantial deficits and drinking enough to gain body
  mass during prolonged exercise.
- **Separate:** fluid, carbohydrate and [sodium](/blog/electrolytes-sweat-rate-cycling)
  interact, but one does not determine the others automatically.

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia is linked primarily to drinking beyond losses;
sodium-containing sports drink does not make excess fluid safe. Neurological
symptoms, collapse or worsening illness require urgent medical help rather than
another bottle or an improvised salt dose.

## Cycling Cramps: Symptom, Not Diagnosis

A cycling cramp does not prove dehydration or a sodium, potassium, magnesium or
carbohydrate deficiency. Exercise-associated muscle cramp is best treated as a
multifactorial pattern: fatigue and altered neuromuscular control, previous cramp
history, pace, event demand, environment and fluid-electrolyte losses may
interact.

For an uncomplicated cramp, stop somewhere safe, reduce the load and gently hold
the affected muscle in a static stretch until it eases. Confusion, collapse,
seizure, severe weakness, dark urine or worsening whole-body symptoms require
medical assessment rather than another bottle or supplement.

## Magnesium: Essential, Not an Automatic Supplement

Cyclists need adequate magnesium, but training hours and sweat do not create one
universal supplement dose. European guidance sets adult adequate intakes at 350
mg/day for men and 300 mg/day for women from all sources. Food is the default;
the dietary target is not an instruction to add the same amount as a pill.

Routine magnesium supplementation has not consistently improved performance in
magnesium-replete active people. It has not been shown to prevent
exercise-associated cramps, sleep evidence is uncertain or modest, and no
single blood or red-cell test gives a definitive tissue-status answer. When a
supplement has a real indication, check elemental magnesium, local supplemental
limits, kidney health, medicines, gut effects and batch testing.

## Common Nutrition Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Under-fuelling hard sessions.**
Quality intervals deserve quality fuel. 60g/hour minimum on any session over 75 minutes; more on intervals.

**Mistake 2: Treating every ride identically.**
Recovery rides need very little fuel. Long Saturday rides need a lot. Match the food to the work.

**Mistake 3: Chasing low body fat in race week.**
The pre-race diet has done its job by the start of taper week. Race-week intake should top up glycogen, not strip body fat.

**Mistake 4: Sourcing nutrition from supplement marketing instead of evidence.**
Most "cycling-specific" supplements have no published performance data. Caffeine, beetroot juice, creatine (yes, even for endurance), and adequate protein cover the [evidence-backed ground](/blog/supplements-cyclists-what-works-guide).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many carbs per hour should I eat on a long ride?**
60-90g/hour for most rides over 90 minutes; 90-120g/hour for racing or ultra. Use a glucose:fructose mix in gels and drinks to access multiple gut transporters. Build up gradually — your gut needs to adapt.

**What should I eat the night before a hard ride?**
A balanced meal with familiar carbohydrate sources — pasta, rice, potatoes — plus a moderate protein portion. Avoid high-fat or high-fibre meals that slow gastric emptying. Top up with a small carbohydrate snack [2-3 hours before the ride](/blog/pre-ride-breakfast-cyclists-guide).

**Can I lose weight while cycling without losing power?**
Yes — with a small daily deficit (200-400 kcal), high protein intake, and strength training. Lose weight in the off-season, not during peak training. Fast weight loss almost always costs power.

**What's the best post-ride recovery food?**
A meal with 1.0-1.2g/kg carbohydrate and 20-40g protein within 30-60 minutes of finishing. Whole-food options work as well as recovery shakes for most amateurs. Sleep does most of the rest.

**Do I need to take supplements?**
Not automatically. Start with an adequate diet and match any supplement to a specific evidence-based use, deficiency or public-health recommendation. Magnesium belongs behind a food and risk audit, not in every cyclist's recovery stack; the [magnesium evidence guide](/blog/magnesium-cyclists-recovery-performance-guide) explains the boundary.

**Is intermittent fasting good for cyclists?**
For weight loss in some athletes, yes. For performance, almost never. Fuelling around training and protein distribution across the day matter more than meal timing. If IF makes either worse for you, it's costing you fitness.

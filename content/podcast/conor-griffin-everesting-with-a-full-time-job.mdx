faq:
  - question: How do you train for Everesting while working a full time job?
    answer: >-
      Conor Griffin structured his training around a nine-to-five schedule by
      working with coach Sean McKenna to build a compliance-driven programme
      with a fixed goal date. The key is converting a vague ambition into a
      specific, dated target and then committing to the sessions that fit around
      work and family, rather than waiting for ideal conditions. Accountability,
      whether to a coach or a publicly stated goal, is what separates those who
      attempt it from those who only think about it.
  - question: How long does it take to prepare for an Everesting attempt?
    answer: >-
      For an amateur cyclist like Conor Griffin, several months of structured,
      periodised training are required before an Everesting attempt is
      realistic. The preparation phase is consistently underestimated because
      most riders focus on the event itself rather than the sustained compliance
      required in the weeks and months beforehand. Cutting the build short is
      one of the most common reasons amateur attempts fail or end in withdrawal.
  - question: What fitness level do you need to complete an Everesting?
    answer: >-
      Conor Griffin completed his Everesting as a category-four amateur cyclist,
      which shows the feat is achievable without elite fitness. What matters
      more than raw ability is arriving at the attempt with well-developed
      aerobic endurance, practised pacing, and enough training consistency to
      handle the volume. A structured coaching relationship helps a rider at any
      level identify the specific weaknesses that need addressing before the
      attempt.
  - question: Does having a cycling coach help with Everesting preparation?
    answer: >-
      Working with a coach, as Conor Griffin did with Roadman coach Sean
      McKenna, transforms preparation from guesswork into a structured programme
      with clear milestones. A coach provides accountability, keeps training
      load appropriate, and ensures the athlete peaks at the right time rather
      than accumulating fatigue in the final weeks. For time-pressed athletes
      balancing work and family, coached structure also prevents the common
      mistake of cramming too much training in too late.

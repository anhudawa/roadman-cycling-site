faq:
  - question: Why are group rides so dangerous?
    answer: >-
      Group rides become dangerous when there is no clear leadership and riders
      act on individual instinct rather than collective awareness. Common
      hazards include wheel overlaps, surging pacelines, and riders accelerating
      through traffic gaps without warning those behind. Without a designated
      ride leader setting and enforcing rules, these risks compound quickly.
  - question: How should a group ride be organised?
    answer: >-
      Every group ride needs a clearly designated leader who is responsible for
      decisions, pace, and the behaviour of the group on the road. Rules should
      be defined in advance and communicated to newcomers before the ride sets
      off. Leadership should be based on competence and experience, not on who
      is the strongest rider on the day.
  - question: What are the rules for riding in a group on the road?
    answer: >-
      Riders should maintain consistent pace without surging, avoid overlapping
      wheels, and never split through traffic gaps without ensuring the riders
      behind can follow safely. Hazard signals should be used sparingly and
      clearly rather than creating constant noise that drowns out genuine
      warnings. Everyone in the group shares responsibility for how cyclists are
      perceived by other road users.
  - question: Who should lead a cycling club group ride?
    answer: >-
      The ride leader should be chosen for their ability to manage a group
      safely, communicate clearly, and make sound decisions in traffic, not
      simply because they are fast. Anthony Walsh argues that conflating riding
      strength with ride-leading competence is one of the central reasons group
      rides have become chaotic and unsafe. Clubs need to separate the two roles
      and treat leadership as a distinct skill that requires deliberate
      selection.

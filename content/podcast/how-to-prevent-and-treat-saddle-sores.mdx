  Host Anthony Walsh, of the Roadman Cycling Podcast, explains that saddle sores
  are bacterial skin infections resembling infected boils, not simple chafing,
  and outlines a prevention protocol built around hygiene rather than chamois
  cream. Key practices include wearing properly fitted bib shorts directly
  against the skin with no underwear, removing and hot-washing shorts

  warm water post-ride. Persistent sores often signal a bike fit issue,
  particularly saddle pressure distribution, warranting a professional
  pressure-mapping assessment.

faq:
  - question: What actually causes saddle sores from cycling?
    answer: >-
      Saddle sores are bacterial skin infections, not simple chafing. They
      develop when bacteria enter the skin through friction and moisture in the
      perineal area, forming infected boils. Poor hygiene after rides is the
      primary cause, not a lack of chamois cream.
  - question: How do I prevent saddle sores when cycling?
    answer: >-
      Anthony Walsh recommends a hygiene-first approach rather than relying on
      chamois cream. Wear properly fitted bib shorts directly against the skin
      with no underwear, remove them immediately after every ride, and scrub the
      perineal area thoroughly with soap and warm water. Hot-wash your shorts
      after every single ride without exception.
  - question: How do I get rid of saddle sores fast?
    answer: >-
      Keep the area clean and dry, and stop riding until the infection has
      cleared. Because saddle sores are bacterial infections rather than surface
      irritation, continuing to ride prolongs and worsens them. Severe or
      recurring sores warrant a visit to a GP who may prescribe antibiotics.
  - question: Why do I keep getting saddle sores even with chamois cream?
    answer: >-
      Persistent saddle sores despite using chamois cream usually indicate a
      bike fit problem, specifically poor saddle pressure distribution. Anthony
      Walsh recommends a professional bike fit with pressure-mapping assessment
      to identify where excess load is being placed on the perineal area.
      Chamois cream does not resolve an underlying fit issue.

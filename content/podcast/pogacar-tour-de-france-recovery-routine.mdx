Right now at the Tour de France, a rider has just finished a five-hour stage. He's burned north of 5,000 calories, emptied himself completely — and tomorrow morning he does it again. Twenty-one times, back to back, and the best of them get *faster* into the third week. You or I do one hard five-hour ride and we're wrecked for two days.

Everyone assumes it's genetics, or worse. And look — the engines on these riders are freakish, no argument. But the recovery isn't magic. It's a checklist. And to be transparent: nobody outside the team bus knows Pogacar's exact routine off his soigneur. What we do know is that nearly every WorldTour rider runs the same post-stage checklist, because the science underneath it is solid. The interesting question is which pieces are worth copying for your Sunday club run — because some of this really moves the needle for an amateur, and some of it is a waste of your money at best and actively counterproductive at worst.

## Key Takeaways

The idea that sorts the entire list: **the pros are optimising to perform again tomorrow; you're optimising to get fitter over months.** In a Grand Tour the next stage is 18 hours away — that's the one scenario where obsessive recovery optimisation pays. On a normal training week, with your next hard ride two or three days out, half this checklist flips from helpful to harmful.

## The Clock Starts Before The Shower

The first thing separating pro recovery from yours has nothing to do with technology. It's timing. The second a pro crosses the line, they're eating — before the bus, before the shower. Not ritual: glycogen. Hard stage racing drains muscle carbohydrate close to empty daily, and the whole game of a Grand Tour is refilling the tank in the window before tomorrow's start. Refill it and you've got legs. Don't and you don't.

The number, from my conversation with [Dr Sam Impey](/podcast/sam-impey-most-cyclists-fuel-wrong) — author of *Fuel for the Work Required* — is 1 to 1.5 grams of carbohydrate per kilo of bodyweight in the first hour after a hard or long ride. For a 75 kg rider, call it 75 to 110 grams: a big bowl of pasta or rice, a recovery drink, a banana. Don't confuse it with on-the-bike fuelling — that's a separate number, and Impey's advice for amateurs is to start around 60 g per hour and build, not to leap at the 120 g the pros talk about.

This is the piece I'd copy without hesitation — *when the context matches*. Back-to-back big days, training camps, a sportive tomorrow: hit the window. Normal week, next hard ride on Wednesday? Your ordinary meals refill the tank with room to spare. Same science, completely different urgency.

## Cherry Juice And Cold Water: Race-Week Tools

The tart cherry shot has become the symbol of pro recovery — the soigneur handing it over at the line. The anthocyanins that make cherries dark are anti-inflammatory and antioxidant, and the evidence is strongest exactly where the pros use it: repeated back-to-back efforts where you must perform again tomorrow.

Here's what the supplement ads won't tell you: there's a live debate about whether you *want* to blunt inflammation on a normal training day. Inflammation is part of the signal that tells your body to adapt. Hammer it flat every day and you may be sanding down the very stimulus you trained to create. Mid-Tour, survival mode — cherry juice makes total sense. Mid-season build phase, trying to actually get fitter? I'd skip it and save it for your own stage races and big multi-day events. Our [tart cherry guide](/blog/cycling-tart-cherry-juice-recovery-guide) covers the dosing detail.

The cold plunge is the same logic dialled up. For next-day freshness in a congested schedule — and for getting core temperature down in this year's heat — the evidence is decent. But there's now a stack of research showing regular post-training cold-water immersion blunts long-term adaptation, most clearly the strength and muscle response. Freeze the soreness away and you freeze some of the gains with it. An ice bath after a Tuesday training session is a workout followed by the undo button. The full breakdown is in [our cold water immersion guide](/blog/cold-water-immersion-cyclists).

## The Massage Myth And What Actually Matters

Does the nightly massage flush lactate and speed recovery? Mostly myth — lactate is long gone within hours, and the measured performance benefits are small. So why does every rider still get on the table? Because it reliably makes them *feel* less trashed, and because half an hour of enforced stillness and human care, twelve stages deep, has real value even if the muscle fibres don't much care. For you: enjoy one when you can get it. But if it's a weekly sports massage versus an extra hour of sleep, sleep wins every time — and it's free.

Which points at the piece that outweighs every gadget: dinner. When [Chris Harper walked me through a rider's post-stage day](/podcast/ep-2538-this-is-exactly-how-pro-riders-fuel-a-stage-in-the-giro-with), the totals ran near 7,000 calories, with dinner alone around 200 g of carbohydrate and 50-odd grams of protein — on top of the shake and the bus meal. That scares amateurs, because we've been trained to fear eating big at night. The principle, from performance nutritionist Dr David Dunne: *periodise the evening meal*. Big day behind you or big day ahead, dinner is big. Rest days either side, it shrinks. Think in 72-hour windows, not calendar days. The classic amateur mistake is the exact opposite — under-fuel all day, then panic-eat at 9pm when willpower's gone — and the old French superstition about no food after eight just sends you to bed wrecked for tomorrow.

The add-on: [Dr Mike Ormsbee's research](/podcast/ep-27-protein-before-bed-builds-cyclists-muscles-faster-new-study) on pre-sleep protein — 30 to 40 grams of casein, Greek yogurt or cottage cheese about half an hour before sleep keeps muscle protein synthesis ticking overnight, and doesn't make you fat even on top of normal intake. For evening trainers especially, it's about the highest-return, lowest-effort habit on this list.

## What This Means For Your Riding

Copy the fuelling wholesale: the first-hour carbs when tomorrow is hard, the load-matched dinner, the bedtime protein. Most amateurs are leaving free recovery on the table by getting exactly that stuff wrong. Treat the anti-inflammatory kit — cherry shots, ice baths — as race-week tools, never build-phase habits, because on training days soreness is the signal and freezing it away freezes your progress. And when the choice is massage or an early night: it's sleep. Always sleep.

That's the real secret of pro recovery. Not a magic shot or a freezing tub — knowing which tool, in which situation, at what time.

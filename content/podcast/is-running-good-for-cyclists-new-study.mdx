Every winter the same argument breaks out — in the WhatsApp group, at the coffee stop, mid-ride. Someone announces they're going to start running to get faster on the bike, and two camps pile in instantly. Half the group: absolute waste of time, just ride your bike. The other half: it's the secret weapon. Both talking with total confidence, which is usually a decent sign that neither has actually looked it up.

This week — laid up injured myself — I looked it up. The reviews, the bone density studies, the muscle recruitment research, the lot. And the quick takeaway: running probably won't make your FTP go up, but it might still be the single best thing you can do for your cycling.

## Key Takeaways

The place to start is the myth that fuels the whole argument: that running miles somehow convert into bike watts. There's a 2026 meta-analysis in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living that looked at exactly this — pooled the randomised trials and asked, if you train in one sport, how much transfers to the other? The finding: no clear advantage. Run-plus-cycle and cycle-only groups came away with broadly comparable aerobic adaptations, every effect small, confidence intervals crossing zero. The specificity principle is real. The best training for cycling is cycling.

If the video stopped there, the just-ride-your-bike camp wins. But that's the boring half of the picture — because the reason running is worth doing has almost nothing to do with wattage.

## Engine And Chassis

The frame that makes everything click: your fitness is two completely different systems.

System one is the engine — heart, lungs, blood volume, mitochondrial density, VO2max. Central fitness. It lives in your cardiovascular system, not in any one muscle group, and it doesn't care whether you're pedalling or running. To your heart, an easy 5k is basically a Zone 1-2 ride. That's the part the meta-analysis confirms transfers almost wholesale.

System two is the chassis — muscles, tendons, bones, connective tissue, and crucially the specific patterns in which they fire. This is where the two sports stop even resembling each other. The research from Bijker and colleagues on muscle activity found the mechanics fundamentally different: cycling is tidy, concentric pushing, while running is dominated by a stretch-shortening cycle — tendons loading like coiled springs on landing and firing back, the calf and Achilles doing enormous elastic work that cycling never asks of them.

The engine transfers perfectly. The chassis doesn't transfer at all. That single distinction explains every benefit and every danger that follows.

## The Bone Density Case

Cycling is a non-weight-bearing sport — researchers call it non-osteogenic. Your skeleton is supported in the saddle, and your hips, pelvis and spine almost never get the impact signal that tells bone to stay strong. Bone is living tissue: load it and it strengthens, fail to load it and it atrophies.

The data is alarming. The episode goes through research finding cyclists several times more likely to have osteopenia of the spine than runners, and elite road cyclists with lower bone density than distance runners on comparable training loads. Combine that with a sport where most of us hit the deck at least occasionally, and it gets serious fast — an eight-year study of Tour de France withdrawals found nearly half were down to fractures, the single most common reason riders abandon. Low bone density in a high-crash sport is just a bad combination.

Running fixes exactly the thing cycling can't. Every stride puts two to three times bodyweight through the skeleton — precisely the osteogenic signal a lifetime in the saddle withholds. On its own, that's reason enough for a lot of riders — especially masters riders — to lace up. We've gone deep on this in [the bone density deep dive](/blog/bone-density-cycling-running-deep-dive).

## The Trap That Catches Fit Cyclists

Here's where cyclists get destroyed, and it comes straight back to the two systems: they adapt at completely different speeds. Cardiovascular and muscular tissue adapt in days to weeks. Tendon and bone take months — poorer blood supply, slower remodelling — and no amount of aerobic fitness speeds that clock up.

As a cyclist, your engine is elite-adjacent. You can head out for a 45-minute run barely out of breath while your Achilles, calves, shins and the small bones of your feet carry essentially zero adaptation to impact. The run feels easy. Then two days later the Achilles is screaming — and your cardiovascular system gave you no warning, because it was never the thing under strain. Every feedback signal you trust on the bike lies to you on foot.

This happens at the highest level. [Jimmy Whelan](/podcast/ep-3-how-to-run-as-a-cyclist-mistakes-to-avoid) — former EF Education WorldTour rider, now a professional trail runner — told me about pros wrecking themselves on training-camp runs, and one top rider whose season derailed for months over a tendon problem that started exactly this way.

## How To Do It Properly

The protocol maps to how tissue remodels, not how fit your lungs are. Prepare before you run: Whelan's number one recommendation wasn't a running plan, it was plyometrics — skipping and single-leg jumps, five minutes at the end of a gym session, teaching your tendons to land — paired with hip-stabiliser work for the glute medius that cycling leaves dormant. Cap every run at a conversational pace, the same Zone 2 discipline you already know from the bike, even when you feel amazing — especially when you feel amazing, because feeling great is the sensation that precedes injury. Start with walk-run intervals for the first two or three weeks. Obey the 10% weekly volume rule — the single most evidence-based guideline in running injury research. Leave 48 hours between runs, never running the morning after your hardest ride. And keep the first months on grass, trails or treadmill, in cushioned shoes without a carbon plate.

Aerobically it will all feel embarrassingly trivial. Correct. You're training tissue, not heart.

## The Verdict

Neither coffee-stop camp was right. If your case for running is a higher FTP, the research says no. But running does three things cycling structurally cannot: it builds the bone your sport erodes, it maintains your engine in a fraction of the time, and done right it builds a more robust, injury-resistant body underneath the cyclist. Treat it with respect — absurdly slow, tissue first, 10% rule — and running isn't a threat to your cycling. It's the thing that keeps you riding, with more durable bones, still in the game for years longer than the bike alone would manage.

answerCapsule: >-
  Host Anthony Walsh outlines a preseason planning framework for amateur
  cyclists built around real-life constraints rather than textbook
  periodization. The method starts by mapping fixed personal commitments
  (weddings, family trips, ski holidays) onto the calendar, then placing two to
  three A-priority races around them. Walsh then recommends preemptive
  physiotherapy assessments to catch weaknesses like hamstring imbalances before
  mid-season, and, citing Mark Cavendish's 2014 dental-related setback, a winter
  dental check to prevent in-season disruptions that derail months of training.
keyTakeaways:
  - >-
    Plot your non-negotiable life commitments first, then build your race
    calendar around them — not the other way round.
  - >-
    Pick two or three A-priority races maximum, and check each one sits far
    enough from holidays to allow a proper build and taper.
  - >-
    Book a physio assessment now to catch imbalances like weak hamstrings before
    they force you off the bike in May.
  - >-
    Get a dental check in winter — a small issue ignored now can wipe out weeks
    of racing at the worst possible time.
  - >-
    Sort any bike-fit changes in the off-season, when adapting to a new position
    won't collide with your race and taper schedule.

faq:
  - question: How do I plan a cycling season around work and family commitments?
    answer: >-
      Start by marking all fixed personal commitments on a calendar first,
      including weddings, family holidays, and ski trips. Anthony Walsh
      recommends building your race schedule around these constraints rather
      than ignoring them, then placing two to three A-priority races in the
      remaining windows. This approach prevents conflicts that force you to skip
      key training blocks or races mid-season.
  - question: How many A races should an amateur cyclist target per season?
    answer: >-
      Anthony Walsh recommends targeting two to three A-priority races per
      season for amateur cyclists. Focusing on a small number of key events
      allows you to structure your training blocks with clear peaks rather than
      spreading preparation too thinly across a packed race calendar.
  - question: Should I see a physiotherapist before cycling season starts?
    answer: >-
      Yes, a preseason physiotherapy assessment is strongly recommended before
      structured training begins. Anthony Walsh advises getting assessed during
      winter to identify weaknesses such as hamstring imbalances before they
      develop into injuries that disrupt months of preparation. Catching these
      issues early means they can be corrected during the off-season when
      training load is low.
  - question: Why do cyclists get dental check-ups before the season?
    answer: >-
      Dental problems can cause significant in-season disruptions, forcing time
      off the bike at the worst possible moment. Anthony Walsh cites Mark
      Cavendish's 2014 setback as a real-world example of how an unaddressed
      dental issue can derail a season that has taken months to build. A routine
      winter dental check costs very little time and removes an avoidable risk
      from your season.

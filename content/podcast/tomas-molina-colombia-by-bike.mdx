answerCapsule: >-
  Anthony Walsh speaks with Tomas Molina, founder of Colombia Cycling, about why
  Colombia is emerging as a premier cycling destination and what makes routes
  like the 80-kilometer Alto de Letras climb distinctive. Molina breaks down the
  climb's deceptive 4% average gradient, which masks pitches of 8–10% through
  each town, and advises riders to eat and drink heavily in the first half,
  since deficits surface roughly 40 kilometers later. The episode positions
  Medellín as a potential post-pandemic Girona equivalent for South American
  cycling tourism.

    every town on the route.
  - >-
    Start eating and drinking hard in the first 40 km — any deficit won't hit
    you until it's too late to fix.
  - >-
    Treat La Letras like a race day: good tires, no extra weight, and a clear
    head before you even start.
  - >-
    The stretch between km 60 and 73 is where rides fall apart — altitude above
    3,000 m makes it a mental fight as much as a physical one.
  - >-
    If you want a local cycling base near Medellín, look at El Retiro in the
    east of Antioquia rather than the city itself.

      Colombia is widely regarded as one of the best cycling destinations in the

  - question: How hard is the Alto de Letras climb in Colombia?
    answer: >-
      Alto de Letras is an 80-kilometre climb with an average gradient of around
      4%, but that figure is misleading. Tomas Molina explains that the road
      kicks up to 8-10% through each town along the route, meaning the effort is
      significantly harder than the average suggests.
  - question: What should you eat and drink on a long climb in Colombia?
    answer: >-
      Tomas Molina advises riders to eat and drink heavily during the first half
      of climbs like Alto de Letras, as any nutritional deficit only becomes
      apparent roughly 40 kilometres later when it is too late to correct.
      Front-loading your fuelling strategy is essential on climbs of this length
      and duration.
  - question: Is Medellín good for cycling tourists?
    answer: >-
      Medellín has become a strong base for cycling tourists, with easy access
      to major climbs, a well-established local riding community, and good
      amenities. Tomas Molina and Anthony Walsh discuss its potential as the
      South American equivalent of Girona, particularly as international travel
      has opened back up post-pandemic.

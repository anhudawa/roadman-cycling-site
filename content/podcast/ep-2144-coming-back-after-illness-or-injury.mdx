faq:
  - question: "How soon can I ride after being ill?"
    answer: >-
      A common guideline is the neck check — if symptoms are above the neck (congestion, sore throat, mild headache) and you have no fever, light activity is generally safe. If symptoms are below the neck (chest congestion, body aches, fever, fatigue), rest completely until symptoms resolve. After a fever, wait at least 48 hours symptom-free before any exercise, and return with easy riding only.
  - question: "How long does it take to get cycling fitness back after illness?"
    answer: >-
      For a mild illness lasting a few days, most riders regain their previous form within one to two weeks of structured return. For longer illnesses of a week or more, expect three to six weeks to fully return to pre-illness fitness levels. The duration depends on how long you were ill, how well-trained you were beforehand, and how patient you are with the rebuild.
  - question: "Should I train through a cold?"
    answer: >-
      Light activity with a mild head cold is generally fine, but listen to your body. If symptoms worsen after riding, rest. If you have any fever, body aches, or chest symptoms, do not train at all. Training through illness extends recovery time and can lead to more serious complications. A few days of complete rest typically costs less fitness than pushing through and being knocked out for two weeks.
  - question: "How do I come back to cycling after a crash or injury?"
    answer: >-
      Start with whatever pain-free movement you can manage — indoor trainer at low intensity, easy spinning with no hard efforts. Follow your physiotherapist's guidance on what movements to avoid. Rebuild duration before intensity, and do not return to group rides or racing until you can comfortably complete the expected duration solo. Confidence on the bike often takes longer to rebuild than physical fitness, and that is completely normal.
  - question: "Why do I feel worse in the second week back from illness?"
    answer: >-
      Many riders feel decent in the first few days back because the initial rides are short and easy. As they increase volume or intensity in the second week, they discover their body was not as recovered as those early rides suggested. This is a sign to pull back, not push through. Your immune system may still be compromised even though surface symptoms have cleared.

Nobody plans for time off the bike. It arrives uninvited — a virus that flattens you for a week, a crash that takes skin and confidence, a dodgy knee that finally says enough. And then the clock starts ticking in your head. How much have I lost? When can I get back? Will I ever feel the same?

## Key Takeaways

I have been through this more times than I would like. And the single biggest lesson I have learned is that the riders who come back slowest usually come back strongest.

Your first two weeks back should feel almost embarrassingly easy. Zone one, zone two, shorter than you want. I know that feels wrong when you are watching your fitness score slide. But your cardiovascular system recovers faster than your muscles, your connective tissue, and your immune system. Feeling fine on a 45-minute spin does not mean you are ready for threshold intervals.

After illness, keep an eye on your resting heart rate. If it is still 5–8 beats above your normal baseline, your body is still working. Adding training load while your immune system is engaged is how people turn a one-week cold into a three-week setback. Be boring. Wait until your numbers settle back to normal before you touch any intensity.

After injury, respect pain. Not discomfort — you will have to tolerate some of that. But sharp pain, pain that changes your movement pattern, pain that lingers after the ride. Those are signals worth listening to, not obstacles to push through.

Build back in blocks. Two easy weeks. Then a small bump in duration. Then a careful reintroduction of intensity. Each block earns the next one. If you are still dragging from the previous step, you are not ready to progress.

I know the temptation is to fast-track it. But every rider I know who rushed back ended up taking more time off than they would have needed if they had been patient from the start. The bike will be there. Your fitness will come back. Do it properly and it sticks.

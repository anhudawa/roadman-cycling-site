I have been wanting to get Dr James Morton on the podcast for a long time. As Head of Nutrition at Team Lidl-Trek, he is one of the people who actually shaped how professional cycling thinks about food and fuelling. The concept of fuel for the work required has filtered down from the WorldTour to club riders, but a lot of it has been lost in translation. This conversation sets the record straight.

## Key Takeaways

- The core idea is simple: eat for what the session demands. If you have got a four-hour endurance ride or a threshold session, your carbohydrate intake should be high. If it is a rest day or an easy spin, pull it back. You are not on a diet — you are periodising your nutrition the same way you periodise your training.
- Training with low carbohydrate availability has its place, but James is very clear that it is a tool, not a lifestyle. Used strategically, it can enhance fat oxidation and mitochondrial density. Used carelessly, it wrecks your ability to perform high-intensity work and puts you on the road to chronic energy deficiency.
- Protein does not change based on carb days. Whether it is a high-carb day or a low-carb day, your muscles still need amino acids to repair and adapt. James recommends 1.6-2.2 grams per kilogram of bodyweight, spread across meals.
- The amateur trap is taking the headline — "pros sometimes train low" — and applying it to every session. That is not what happens at WorldTour level. The planning behind each session is precise, and the nutrition supports that plan.

## Expert Quotes

> "The concept was never about restriction. It was about precision. You give the body what it needs, when it needs it, and you do not give it what it does not need." — Dr James Morton

> "If you cannot hold the power numbers in an interval session because you are glycogen depleted, you have missed the point of the session entirely." — Dr James Morton

> "Amateurs hear 'train low' and think 'eat less.' That is a completely different thing." — Dr James Morton

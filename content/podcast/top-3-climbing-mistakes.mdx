answerCapsule: >-
  Host Anthony Walsh, a cycling coach, identifies the three most common climbing
  errors he sees in amateur riders. The first is starting too fast; he
  recommends mentally splitting a climb into four quarters and
  negative-splitting each one. The second is poor fuelling strategy, since
  eating 60–80g of carbs per hour fails on steep terrain — solids should be
  consumed in the valleys, with gels reserved for the gradient. The third is
  wasted upper-body movement, which burns calories without producing forward
  propulsion. Smooth pedalling and timely gear changes preserve energy on
  sustained climbs.
keyTakeaways:
  - >-
    Split every climb into four quarters mentally and aim to negative-split each
    one — ease into it, finish strong.
  - >-
    Eat solids and bars in the valley or on gentle gradients; save gels for when
    the road kicks up.
  - >-
    Front-load your fuelling before a long climb starts, not after you're
    already deep in the effort.
  - >-
    Any movement in your upper body costs calories without turning the pedals —
    stay still, let your legs do the work.
  - >-
    When fatigue makes you fight the bike, shift down a gear and find your
    smooth pedal stroke again rather than grinding.

faq:
  - question: Why do cyclists start too fast on climbs?
    answer: >-
      Most amateur riders misjudge early effort because the first section of a
      climb feels manageable. Anthony Walsh recommends splitting the climb
      mentally into four quarters and negative-splitting each one, so effort
      builds progressively rather than peaking too soon and forcing a blow-up
      before the summit.
  - question: What should cyclists eat when climbing steep hills?
    answer: >-
      Eating solid food on steep gradients is inefficient because swallowing and
      digestion become harder under high exertion. Anthony Walsh advises
      consuming solid carbohydrates in the valleys before the gradient kicks up,
      then switching to gels on the climb itself. The target remains 60-80g of
      carbs per hour, but the format must match the terrain.
  - question: How do I stop wasting energy when climbing on a bike?
    answer: >-
      Excess upper-body movement is one of the most common energy leaks on
      sustained climbs. Anthony Walsh points out that rocking, swaying, or
      gripping the bars too hard burns calories without contributing to forward
      propulsion. Smooth pedalling technique combined with timely gear changes
      keeps effort directed solely into the drivetrain.
  - question: How should I pace myself on a long cycling climb?
    answer: >-
      Start conservatively and treat the climb as four separate sections,
      building effort through each quarter. Anthony Walsh recommends a
      negative-split approach, meaning each section is ridden slightly harder
      than the last. This prevents early lactate accumulation and leaves enough
      in reserve to push hard over the final section.

faq:
  - question: How should cyclists structure winter training?
    answer: >-
      Rob Hatch frames winter training around three variables: dose, frequency,
      and duration. The goal is building an absorbable aerobic platform rather
      than stacking unstructured high-volume weeks that quietly accumulate
      fatigue. Riders who stay fresh and absorbable through winter are far
      better placed to handle hard spring work than those who log hero volume
      weeks for Strava.
  - question: Why do endurance rides drift into moderate intensity during winter?
    answer: >-
      Most cyclists lack a defined intensity ceiling for easy rides, so effort
      creeps upward as terrain, group dynamics, or habit pull the pace higher.
      Rob Hatch identifies this drift as one of the main reasons winter training
      fails to build a clean aerobic base. Keeping endurance rides genuinely
      easy requires deliberate discipline, not just good intentions.
  - question: Why am I slow at climbing on the bike?
    answer: >-
      Rob Hatch outlines five fixable reasons cyclists climb slowly, most of
      which come down to poor power-to-weight ratio, inadequate aerobic base,
      and pacing errors rather than raw talent. Consistently riding at the wrong
      intensities through winter means the engine needed to sustain climbing
      efforts simply never gets built. Addressing these factors systematically
      during the off-season produces clear improvements by the time the roads
      turn upward in spring.
  - question: Is more training volume always better in winter for cyclists?
    answer: >-
      No. Rob Hatch argues that most riders fail not from doing too little but
      from accumulating unstructured hours that build fatigue without producing
      proportional fitness gains. The priority is staying absorbable, meaning
      your body can actually respond and adapt to the training you do. Volume
      only becomes an asset once intensity, recovery, and structure are properly
      managed within your programme.

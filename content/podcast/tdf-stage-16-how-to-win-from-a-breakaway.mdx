faq:
  - question: How do breakaway attacks win in the Tour de France?
    answer: >-
      A breakaway succeeds when the lone attacker commits completely to the
      effort, while the chasing group fractures through tactics. Each rider in
      the chase holds back to conserve energy for the sprint, so no one takes
      full responsibility for the pursuit, and the gap grows.
  - question: How far out can a solo breakaway win a Tour de France stage?
    answer: >-
      Patrick Konrad won Stage 16 of the Tour de France with a solo attack from
      35 to 40 kilometres out. Attacks of this range are viable when the peloton
      is disorganised or when GC teams have no interest in chasing for their
      leaders.
  - question: Why do Tour de France riders train on rest days instead of resting?
    answer: >-
      Most GC riders train on rest days to stop the body shifting into full
      recovery mode, which can leave legs feeling heavy and flat when racing
      resumes. A controlled ride keeps the engine ticking over without adding
      meaningful fatigue.
  - question: Why does a breakaway group stop working together late in a stage?
    answer: >-
      As a breakaway nears the finish, each rider starts bluffing, refusing to
      drive the pace so they arrive fresher for the sprint or final climb. This
      collective hesitation is often what allows a single attacker who has gone
      clear earlier to stay away and take the win.

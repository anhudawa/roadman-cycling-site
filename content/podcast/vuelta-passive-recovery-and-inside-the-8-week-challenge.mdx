faq:
  - question: Do you get fitter during a training session?
    answer: >-
      No. Walsh explains that a session is the damage or stimulus, and the actual gains in fitness happen afterwards while you sleep and recover. Training hard without recovering properly means you never fully realise the adaptation you have earned.
  - question: What is passive recovery for cyclists?
    answer: >-
      Passive recovery is deliberate rest away from training — quality sleep, lowering stress and letting the body adapt — rather than active recovery rides. Walsh treats it as a planned part of getting fitter, not wasted time between hard sessions.
  - question: Why does circadian rhythm matter for recovery?
    answer: >-
      Your circadian rhythm controls the hormones that send you to sleep and wake you up, so keeping it in tune protects sleep quality, which is where most recovery happens. Walsh points to morning movement and light as simple ways to reset it.

You don't get fitter during a session. The session is damage. You get fitter when you sleep and recover from it. That's the whole thing, and most motivated cyclists get it completely backwards.

## Key Takeaways

Marcel Kittel retired at 30 with 89 professional victories and 14 Tour de France stage wins. He cited loss of quality of life. That's what chronic under-recovery looks like at the highest level. We've had athletes come into A1 who've worked with other coaches and arrived in adrenal fatigue. Getting someone back from that is difficult. It starts impacting everything, not just watts, but getting up in the morning, family, work. The system we run is low-volume, high-intensity, and it only works if the recovery side is treated as seriously as the training side. If your zone 5 session is 20 watts lower than it should be because you didn't recover properly the day before, you're not getting the adaptation. You're just accumulating fatigue.

The cortisol piece is the one most people miss. Cortisol is the stress hormone your body produces in response to both physical and mental stress, and your brain doesn't distinguish between the two. Constantly elevated cortisol blocks you from getting into the deep sleep phases, Delta and deep sleep, where the actual muscular and cognitive regeneration happens. So if work or life is piling stress on top of training stress, you need to look at the load and reduce it. That's not a recovery week, that's just reading what your body is telling you. When your motivation to train isn't there in the morning, that's the signal. That's the one to pay attention to.

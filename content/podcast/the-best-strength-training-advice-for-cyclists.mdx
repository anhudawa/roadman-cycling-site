faq:
  - question: Should cyclists do strength training?
    answer: >-
      For most riders, a small dose of strength training is worth including.
      A 2025 meta-analysis of 17 studies found benefits for cycling
      performance, efficiency and anaerobic power, although the authors rated
      the evidence as low certainty. It matters more for riders over 40, who
      are losing muscle mass, and for anyone whose training is only on the
      bike.
  - question: Is it better to skip the gym and ride more?
    answer: >-
      Often not. Extra riding builds the same aerobic qualities you already
      train, while strength work adds force capacity and tissue resilience that
      the bike under-loads. The right answer depends on your hours: with very
      limited time, protecting your key bike sessions comes first, then adding
      a short strength session rather than a longer ride.
  - question: How often should a cyclist do strength training?
    answer: >-
      One to two sessions a week is the dose most advice and trials point to.
      In winter, when bike intensity is lower, two is realistic for many
      riders; in race season, one maintenance session is usually enough. More
      is not automatically better, because strength work has a recovery cost
      too.
  - question: What are the biggest strength training mistakes cyclists make?
    answer: >-
      Doing too much volume, choosing exercises that look impressive but do not
      transfer to pedalling, and placing hard gym sessions straight before key
      bike sessions. Using movements you cannot control, especially as an older
      rider, is a further risk. Keeping the plan small and repeatable avoids
      most of these.
  - question: Is strength training more important for cyclists over 40?
    answer: >-
      The case gets stronger with age. Adults lose roughly 3-8% of muscle mass
      per decade after 40, and cycling does little to slow that. Strength work
      is one of the few ways to keep muscle and force capacity, which is why
      the advice from Dr Andy Galpin and others stresses it for masters
      riders.
  - question: Do I need to lift heavy to benefit?
    answer: >-
      The research on cyclists is mostly on heavy resistance training, but
      heavy is relative to your experience. Build load gradually with movements
      you can control, ideally with coaching, rather than chasing big numbers.
      Roadman does not prescribe heavy barbell lifts for the 35-55 rider
      because the injury risk outweighs the gain for most people.

If you're thinking of skipping the gym this winter and just riding more, this episode is for you. I've been lucky enough to sit down with some of the best people in strength and sports science, and what I've done here is pull the best of their advice together in one place.

That includes [Dr Andy Galpin](/podcast/the-science-of-getting-faster-after-40-dr-andy-galpin), Art O'Connor and [Derek Teel](/podcast/ep-2183-strength-training-for-cycling-simplified-derek-teel), alongside the research on strength training for cyclists.

## What matters most

The consistent message across the people I've spoken to is that a small dose of strength work, done every week, beats a big plan you drop by November. One or two sessions. Movements you can control. A load that builds gradually. The detail of the programme matters less than turning up.

A [2025 meta-analysis of 17 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40632222/) covering 262 trained cyclists found benefits for performance, efficiency and anaerobic power. The authors rated the evidence low certainty and could not name a best programme, and nobody can tell you that a specific schedule is the answer.

## Where riders go wrong

Three mistakes come up again and again. Too much volume, so the legs never recover for the bike. Exercises chosen because they look impressive rather than because they transfer. And poor timing, with a hard gym session the day before the one ride that mattered.

I also want to be direct about load. For the 35-55 rider I don't prescribe heavy barbell lifts. The injury risk outweighs the gain for most people, and there are plenty of ways to load the legs and trunk heavily without them.

## The case against "just ride more"

Extra riding builds the same aerobic qualities you already train. Strength work adds force capacity and protects tissue the bike barely loads. After about 40, adults lose [roughly 3-8% of muscle mass per decade](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15192443/), and cycling does little to slow that. So skipping the gym isn't free. It's a trade, and for a lot of riders it's a bad one.

  On this episode of the Roadman Cycling Podcast, host Anthony Walsh outlines
  the framework he uses to plan every training ride, treating route design as a
  deliberate process rather than a default loop. He weighs ride duration first,
  then the prescribed session, then seasonal weather and wind direction,
  advising riders to plot routes that finish with a tailwind and avoid
  high-altitude roads in winter due to ice risk. He also recommends varying
  terrain and using intersecting loops to preserve the sense of exploration that
  monotonous repeat routes erode.
keyTakeaways:
  - >-
    Plan your ride duration first — there's no point plotting an ambitious hilly
    route if you only have 90 minutes.
  - >-
    Match your route to your session: sprints need 400m+ of straight, quiet
    road; threshold efforts need 20 minutes free of junctions and traffic
    lights.
  - >-
    Check wind direction before you head out and design your route so the
    tailwind is on the way home, not the way out.
  - >-
    Avoid high-altitude roads in winter — even at 4-5°C, climbing higher means
    ice risk and a much greater chance of crashing.
  - >-
    Use intersecting loops instead of one big out-and-back — they open up more
    exploration and keep riding feeling fresh rather than monotonous.

faq:
  - question: How do I plan a cycling training route?
    answer: >-
      Start with how long you have to ride, then match the route to the session
      type. Factor in wind direction so you finish with a tailwind, and vary
      terrain to suit the training prescription rather than defaulting to the
      same loop every ride.
  - question: Should I avoid hills in winter cycling training?
    answer: >-
      High-altitude roads carry a significant ice risk in winter and are best
      avoided. Plan routes that stay at lower elevations during the colder
      months and return to exposed or hilly terrain once conditions improve in
      spring.
  - question: How do I use wind direction when planning a bike ride?
    answer: >-
      Check wind direction before plotting your route and design it so the
      return leg is ridden with a tailwind. Riding into the wind when fresh at
      the start is far safer and more productive than battling a headwind when
      fatigued at the end.
  - question: How do I stop cycling training routes feeling boring and repetitive?
    answer: >-
      Use intersecting loops rather than a single fixed circuit so you can vary
      the roads you ride from session to session. Treating route design as a
      deliberate process rather than a habit preserves the sense of exploration
      and keeps training mentally engaging over a long season.

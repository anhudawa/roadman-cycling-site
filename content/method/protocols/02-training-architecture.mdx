## Where Week 1 left you, and where Week 2 takes you

You've done the audit. You know your intensity distribution. You know your real available hours. You know the leak. Now we build the structure that fixes it.

Most riders have heard of polarised training. Some can quote the 80/20 number. A few can name Professor Seiler. But almost nobody actually rides a polarised week — because the structure they've been given was built around an idealised athlete, not the cyclist with a 7am Monday meeting and two kids at school by quarter to nine.

This module fixes that. We're going to take the science of polarised training and translate it into a weekly architecture that fits the hours you actually have. By the end of this week, you'll have a template for your week that you can run for the next six weeks without touching it — and start to feel what 80/20 actually feels like in your legs.

## The three levers, in order

Seiler has a framework for building a plan that's worth tattooing on the inside of your sunglasses. He calls it the three levers: frequency, duration, intensity — and the order matters.

> "I'm going to begin with frequency. All I care about is you get out the door a certain number of times a week. Six weeks later, now let's stretch one of those workouts, start using duration, that second lever. And after 12 weeks, now I'm going to introduce that magic buzzword intervals and intensity. But not before. And I think that's one of the most common mistakes that's made — we use the intensity lever almost from day one."

Read that twice. Most amateurs flip the order. They start with intensity — Tuesday night threshold, Thursday VO2, group ride hammer-fest on Saturday — and never properly extend duration or stabilise frequency. The body doesn't get the substrate it needs to absorb the hard work.

For Week 2, your only job is the first lever: get out the door consistently, keep the easy work properly easy, and let one ride extend in duration. Intensity is the smallest part of the menu this week. We add more in later weeks.

## What polarised actually means

Seiler's research, replicated across rowers, runners, cross-country skiers and cyclists, found something striking in elite endurance training: the successful athletes weren't doing daily threshold work. They were either riding very easily — building mitochondria with low-stress duration — or doing structured high-intensity work with structured recovery. Almost nothing in the middle.

The middle is the trap. It's where the workload feels productive but produces less aerobic adaptation per minute than the easy work, and less peripheral and central adaptation than the hard work. Every minute you spend in the yellow zone is a minute that wasn't either deeply aerobic or properly stressful. The yellow zone is the busiest road to the smallest improvement.

So the architecture this week has two jobs: make the easy days truly easy, and make the one or two hard sessions truly hard. The middle gets emptied.

## The Roadman week — what it looks like

The downloadable templates give you four versions of the week — 6, 8, 10 and 12 hours. The structure is the same; the duration scales. The shape:

**The anchor: long Zone 2.** One ride per week that extends. Conversational pace. Heart rate flat after warm-up. Appetite at the end. Three to five hours depending on your bracket. This is the ride that builds the engine. If you only do one quality session this week, this is it.

**The midweek quality session.** This week — a second Zone 2 ride, slightly shorter, on a route that lets you stay in green. Not a sweet-spot session. Not a tempo loop. We're proving you can run two genuinely easy rides in a week before we add intensity in Week 5.

**Recovery / freshen-up rides.** Short. Flat. Active recovery, or absolute Zone 1 floor. These exist to keep frequency up without building load. Half an hour to 90 minutes.

**Strength.** Two 30-minute sessions, but we don't introduce the protocol until Week 4. This week, leave the gym slot in the calendar but use it as recovery if needed.

**Rest day.** One full day off the bike. Genuine rest. Not "active recovery just in case." Adaptation needs space.

## How to actually keep the easy days easy

Here's where most riders fail this week, and it's a fixable mistake. They go out intending to ride easy, see a hill, and unconsciously push it. The heart rate creeps. Suddenly Tuesday's "Zone 2" ride is averaging 150bpm with a 10-minute spike to 165 because the grade was 6%.

Three tools, in order of how well they work:

**1. The Seiler four-test on every easy ride.** Are you having a conversation with the rider beside you (or talking out loud to yourself) without it feeling difficult? Is your heart rate flat after warm-up? Are you finished with the ride and have an appetite? Could you go straight to the dinner table? If yes to all four, you're in green. If any are no, you're in yellow.

**2. A heart-rate ceiling alarm on your head unit.** Set it to your LT1 heart rate (the workbook has the calculation). When the alarm fires, you back off. No negotiation. The bike computer is the coach.

**3. Permission to ride the easy day stupidly easy.** This is the one Alistair Brownlee makes the strongest case for. He says the difference between 280 watts and 220 watts of Zone 2 is meaningless — but the difference in fatigue cost is huge. "You actually don't even really need to know where the top of Zone 2 is. You just need to know that you're way below it."

Way below it. Not on the threshold of it. The amateur version of Zone 2 lives at the top of the zone and tips into yellow when the road tilts. The pro version sits in the middle of the zone and stays there. Borrow the pro version this week.

## The session you skip is the one you don't recognise

There's a session most amateurs do every week and never name as a session: the group ride. It's "social." It's "fun." But heart rate data tells the truth — it's almost always a yellow-zone effort with bursts of red. It's not a recovery ride. It's not a Zone 2 ride. It's a quality session you weren't planning.

You don't have to give it up. You do have to count it. If your Saturday group ride is going to be three hours of mostly tempo with surges, that becomes your one quality session for the week and the rest of the week needs to flex around it. The mistake is to do the group ride AND a midweek threshold AND a long Z2 — three quality sessions in seven days, with no proper recovery, in a week with eight available hours. That's the polarised model thrown out the window.

This week, name the group ride if you do one. Add it to the audit. Treat it like a session, not free riding. We'll work with it.

## Common mistakes this week

**Adding intensity because you don't trust easy.** The temptation is to throw a sweet-spot session into the week to "make sure something hard is happening." Don't. The point of this week is to feel what proper polarised work feels like in the body. Threshold and VO2 come back in later modules.

**Riding fasted to "burn fat."** This is the next module. For now, fuel every ride. Skipping carbs to chase fat oxidation is one of the leaks we're plugging in Week 3.

**Trying to hit every hour in the bracket.** If the template says 8 hours and you have 6, ride 6. Frequency over volume. Lorang again: build the plan around the real working week, not a fantasy version.

**Letting the long ride drift to yellow.** The long ride is the most valuable session in the entire week and the easiest to ruin. Set the heart-rate ceiling. Stay under it. If your buddies surge, let them go. Wiggins used to ride the climb, not race his rivals — there's a reason.

**Counting strength as a rest day.** If you do a strength session, count the load. Even before we introduce the proper protocol in Week 4, gym work is real load. Don't double-up gym + intervals on the same day.

## The metric that matters this week

By Sunday night, look at your week and answer one question: what percentage of my riding time was in green?

If it's 70% or higher, the architecture is holding. If it's 50%, the easy days drifted. If it's 30%, you ignored the heart-rate ceiling and rode by feel — and feel will lie to you for another six weeks if you let it.

This number is the diagnostic for whether the architecture is working. Track it weekly. If it stays above 70% for the next four weeks, your aerobic engine is rebuilding under you, even on weeks where the legs feel a bit flat. That's how it's supposed to feel — quietly, not dramatically.

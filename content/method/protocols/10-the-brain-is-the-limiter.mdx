## Your biggest limiter isn't your legs

You've done the work. The training architecture is dialled. The fuelling is precise. The strength foundation is built. The power is developing. You're probably the fittest you've been in years — and now we have to address the factor that determines whether all of that fitness actually shows up on race day.

It isn't the legs. It's the head.

You know the moment. The road tilts up. You glance at the head unit and it starts showing those little red gradient bars ahead. The heart rate climbs. The breathing gets ragged. And before your body actually needs to stop, your brain tells you to back off. The wheel in front drifts away. Five minutes earlier you were strong. Now you're limit-state and saving for the rest of the climb that hasn't really started.

That moment is mental, not physical. Fixing it is often the fastest way to stop getting dropped — and it's the factor most training programmes pretend doesn't exist. This week we fix it.

## The principle most coaches won't tell you

Performance physiology has a model called the Central Governor — first described by Professor Tim Noakes of the University of Cape Town — the idea that the brain regulates effort based on perceived risk to the body, and almost always pulls the brake before the body is properly in trouble. The brain holds back roughly 20–40% of available capacity as a safety buffer.

You can't override the governor through willpower alone. You can shift it. You can train it, just like you train aerobic capacity. Riders who consistently outperform their numbers — who finish events stronger than their FTP suggests they should — have done the work to push their perceived-effort threshold higher. They feel the same internal signals you do, but they've taught their brain to interpret them differently.

This module is the toolkit for doing that work.

## Why amateurs collapse on climbs (when their numbers say they shouldn't)

Three predictable patterns we see across the Roadman community.

**1. Pacing without a plan.** Most amateurs head into a climb without a deliberate pacing strategy. They follow the wheel in front, react to the gradient, and discover their limit by exceeding it. The brain interprets the resulting metabolic distress as catastrophic and pulls hard on the governor.

**2. Negative self-talk.** "This is going to be brutal." "I always blow up here." "I'm not climbing well today." The brain takes these statements as direct instructions and pre-emptively pulls back. Self-talk is a coach voice — make sure the voice is on your side.

**3. Treating suffering as a signal to stop.** Suffering is a feature of high-end aerobic work. It's also reliable, predictable, and survivable. Riders who panic at the onset of suffering give up the watts they came to spend. Riders who recognise the suffering, name it, and ride through it complete the work.

We're going to give you tools for all three.

## The pre-ride mental preparation routine

Five minutes, before any quality session this week. Not a meditation. A briefing.

**1. Name the session.** Out loud or written: "Today is 4 × 4 minutes torque on the climb. RPE 7. Sustainable. Hard but not maximal."

**2. Visualise the difficulty.** Specifically. Not generally. Imagine the second interval — the one that always feels worst. Imagine your breathing getting harder. Imagine the brain saying back off. Imagine yourself holding the wattage anyway. Twenty seconds of deliberate visualisation.

**3. Pre-load a single anchor phrase.** One sentence you'll say to yourself when the brain pulls. "Stay in the box." "This is what I trained for." "Hold the wheel." Borrow one or write your own — but pick one and commit before the ride.

**4. Set the technical conditions.** Heart-rate alarm on. Power cap if you have one. Bottle in the right cage. Phone away. The bike is set up to support the head.

**5. Identify the meaning.** One sentence on why this session matters. Not motivational fluff — a real link to your goal. "This session is what gets me through the climb at the gran fondo without blowing up at the bottom." Concrete reason.

That's it. Five minutes. Run it before every quality session this week.

## The in-ride self-talk framework

When the suffering hits, you have roughly three seconds before the brain commits to either fight or fold. The framework:

**Name it.** "OK, this is the hard part." Not "I'm dying." Not "I can't do this." Naming the experience neutrally separates you from the feeling. The feeling is happening. You are observing it.

**Anchor.** Use the phrase you pre-loaded. Say it. Internally or out loud — out loud is often more powerful, even if you feel ridiculous on a quiet climb at 7am.

**Chunk.** Don't think about the whole interval. Don't think about the whole climb. Count breaths. Count pedal strokes. "Get to that next pylon." Break the suffering into three-pylon segments. The brain can handle three pylons. It panics at "another four minutes."

**Re-engage.** Posture check. Pull the elbows in. Drop the shoulders. Steady the breathing — five seconds in, five seconds out, even when it feels like you can't. The breath is the entry point to nervous-system regulation. Slow the breath, the brain follows.

Practise this on a session you usually struggle with this week. You'll be surprised how much capacity you already had — you just hadn't trained the brain to access it.

## The pacing plan — the document you write before, not during

Most amateurs pace by reaction. The pros pace by plan. The difference is the document. Before any meaningful event or hard session this week, write the plan in three lines:

- **The first third:** what wattage, heart rate or RPE you commit to no matter what the group around you does
- **The middle third:** how you respond to the situation around you (move up, hold position, sit in)
- **The final third:** when you're allowed to push (and what triggers the push)

Three lines. Written. Before. The reason this works isn't the document — it's that the brain knows the plan and stops asking "what should I do here" mid-climb. Decision fatigue is real. The fewer in-the-moment decisions you have to make, the more energy stays in the legs.

For the next event on your calendar, write the plan this week. Use the Pacing Cards in your downloads — they have templates for climbs, time trials, group rides, and sportives.

## Stress is real training load — the audit, again

We covered this in Week 5, but it lands differently in Week 10. By now you've done nine weeks of building. The cumulative load is meaningful. If your life stress has stayed flat, the body is absorbing the work. If life stress has climbed — work pressure, family event, financial worry — the body is paying for both at once.

Run the Stress Audit this week. Write down the three biggest non-cycling stressors of the last fortnight. Map them onto the training calendar. If you're hitting peak training load on a peak life-stress week, something gives. Almost always, it's recovery, then performance, then health, in that order.

Derek Teel was honest about it in our conversation:

> "I have three kids under four right now. I'm running my own business. There's days I leave and I'm so tense that I just need to breathe, I need to regulate my heart rate. I can't even think about having an effort right now."

You have permission to back off when life is heavy. The body doesn't separate sources of stress. Plan accordingly.

## The role of meaning — what Lachlan got right

Lachlan Morton's interview keeps surfacing across this course because he gives us the framework for sustainability. The mental game isn't only about willpower in the moment. It's about why you're doing the work in the first place.

> "It's basically just trying to follow true motivations. Sometimes stepping back and looking around and being like, ah, I'm not actually enjoying this. I'm pursuing this for the wrong reason. What's something that would be exciting for me and motivating for me and going to get me out stoked on my bike every day?"

Self-talk works when there's something true at the bottom of it. If you can't honestly say why you're suffering on a particular climb, the brain will pull the governor harder. Your why has to be present in your head when the suffering hits.

For the riders who took the Le Metier reflection in Week 6 seriously, this comes easier. You know why you ride. You know what the goal is connected to. The mental game gets easier when the meaning is clear.

## The 3-2-1 race prep protocol

If you have an event in the next two weeks, run this:

**3 days out.** Volume drops to 60% of normal. One short opener — 4–5 short surges of 30 seconds at race pace, well-recovered. Eat normally. Sleep 8 hours minimum.

**2 days out.** Light spin only — 60 minutes Z1, no efforts. Carb-load slightly more than normal. Hydrate. Get to bed early.

**1 day out.** 30–45 minute spin, very easy, with three short race-pace efforts of 30 seconds. The legs are reminded what fast feels like without taking any load. Keep the carbs high. Hydrate. Pre-pack the kit. Sleep.

**Race morning.** Pre-event meal 2.5–3 hours out. Caffeine if you tolerate it, 60 minutes out. Warm up properly — at least 20 minutes building from easy to a few short race-pace efforts. Run the pre-ride mental routine.

The protocol is in your downloads with a checklist version for the bike bag.

## Common mistakes this week

**Treating mental work as soft.** Every athlete you admire has a mental game. The riders who claim they don't are usually the ones who've internalised it deeply enough that they don't notice they're using it.

**Skipping the pre-ride routine because it feels embarrassing.** Five minutes of deliberate preparation before every quality session, every event. Not optional.

**Pacing by reaction.** Write the plan. Stick to it. Adjust by feel inside the framework, not outside of it.

**Letting one bad ride contaminate the next.** The brain remembers blow-ups vividly. After a bad ride, write down two things you did well and one thing you'll do differently next time. Don't carry the loss into the next session.

**Using "the brain is the limiter" as an excuse to ignore the body.** It's still real physiology. Adequate fuel, sleep and recovery underpin everything in this module. Mental tools are layered on top of a recovered, fuelled body — not a substitute for one.

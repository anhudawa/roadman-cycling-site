## The hardest sell in cycling

Here's the thing nobody tells you about getting faster: it doesn't happen during training. It happens between sessions. The training is the stimulus. The improvement is laid down in recovery.

That sentence is easy to nod at and impossible to live by. Most serious cyclists in this community are sabotaging their own adaptation by never truly recovering — not because they're lazy, but because they feel guilty about rest days and fill them with "easy" rides that aren't easy enough. Or because their idea of recovery is buying another gadget. Or because life stress is so high that the body is already running near capacity before training is added on top.

This week we change that. Not with a cold plunge. Not with a massage gun. With the cheapest, most powerful recovery intervention in the entire archive of this podcast — one that shows up across every single conversation with elite coaches and physiologists when the tape stops rolling and they get honest about what actually moves the needle.

## The single most powerful recovery insight in the archive

When Professor Seiler talks about recovery, he doesn't talk about supplements or recovery boots or hyperbaric chambers. He tells a story about Gustaf Iden and Christian Blomenfeldt's coaches — two of the best triathletes on the planet at the time:

> "Those two guys said look, we don't use any of these recovery modalities. The only one we use is sleep. And they said that we found out that if we avoid the massage and the cold plunge, we have more time to sleep. And sleep is our recovery weapon. That's just about as old school as you can get but that's physiology — if we go back a thousand years we didn't have massage pistols and cold plunges, but sleep has been there."

Sleep is the recovery tool. Everything else is rounding error. If you only do one thing in this module, sort your sleep.

That sentence is going to feel inadequate. I know — because every time I say it on the podcast, the email replies are some version of "but what about the cold plunge / sauna / recovery boots / glutamine / red-light therapy". The honest answer is that those are second-order interventions on a foundation of seven and a half to eight hours of consistent sleep. If the foundation isn't there, the interventions are marketing.

Seiler frames it the way a physiologist would:

> "Doing things right involves training in an ordinary way with great consistency — and amazingly, that prepares us for extraordinary performances."

Recovery is boring, daily, and compounds. That's why it works. That's also why it gets neglected — there's nothing to post about a normal Tuesday night where you went to bed at 10:30pm.

## What sleep actually does for the cyclist

Three things, and all of them matter.

**1. Adaptation consolidation.** Growth hormone release peaks in deep sleep. The repair work that turns yesterday's training stimulus into tomorrow's higher-performing tissue happens between roughly 11pm and 3am for most people. If you're up at 1am scrolling, you're missing the window.

**2. Glycogen restoration.** Sleep is when your liver and muscles refill the carbohydrate stores you depleted on the bike. Bad sleep, slow refill. Slow refill, bad ride the next day.

**3. Decision quality.** Tired brains make bad coaching decisions. Tired brains do the planned threshold session even though the legs are wrecked. Tired brains skip strength. Tired brains snack on rubbish at 9pm. Sleep doesn't just recover the body — it fixes the next-day choices.

Dr Sarah Berry's research, which we covered in the nutrition module, also closes a loop here: the same breakfast after a bad night's sleep produces a higher glucose peak than after a good night. Sleep is a nutrition variable. Recovery is a training variable. They're all one system.

## The readiness check — five minutes every morning

Most cyclists have heard of HRV. Fewer use it well. The piece that matters more than the absolute number is the trend, and the trend is something you can reproduce with a forty-second morning routine.

Every morning, before coffee, before phone, before anything, log four numbers in the readiness template:

1. **Hours of sleep last night** (real, not what your watch says you should have got)
2. **Subjective sleep quality** (1–10)
3. **Resting heart rate** (or HRV if you have a strap)
4. **Subjective leg feel** (1–10, how the legs feel in the first minute of standing up)

Add a single sentence about life stress.

Run this for seven days and you'll start to see your own patterns. Most people in this audience find: bad sleep → high RHR → poor leg feel → sub-quality session that day. That's the pattern Lorang describes when he talks about the 50/50 split between data and subjective feedback:

> "It could be that two athletes saying 'I'm tired' but it means something different. The scale of saying 'I'm tired' is different from athlete to athlete. This is something where you have to make some kind of calibration."

You're calibrating yourself this week. By Sunday, you'll have a reference baseline. By Week 8, you'll be making real-time decisions on training quality from your morning numbers.

## When to skip the session — the framework that gives you permission

The hardest part of recovery is admitting when to back off. Lorang has the cleanest decision tool I've heard from any coach:

> "If you are doing these intervals and you can stay in that frame, you continue. But if you drop in the first two intervals already — the quality at that point — then we skip and you just make an easy run. And we reset the program. The athlete is then sure: okay, it's fine that I'm tired. It's fine if I don't have my personal best session today."

Two intervals. If the first two are off the prescribed power or RPE by more than ~5%, you're not having an off day, you're having a wrong day for the session. Switch to easy. Don't grind through. Lorang's exact framing: it's not weakness, it's intelligent load management.

## Stress is training load

This is where most amateurs get blindsided. Derek Teel has been honest about it:

> "I have three kids under four right now. I'm running my own business. There's days I leave and I'm so tense that I just need to breathe, I need to regulate my heart rate. I can't even think about having an effort right now. There's no data to quantify it necessarily. But this is why it's so important to refer to data but to also be introspective and to really try and feel your body."

Life stress is real training load. Work pressure, parenting, financial worry, relationship tension — they all run through the same sympathetic nervous system that hard intervals run through. Stack a high-stress week on top of a hard training week and your body responds to the combined load, not the cycling load.

Lorang is direct with the riders he coaches: share the cycling plan with the people around you, build training around real life, and when stress spikes, lower the training load. Don't just power through. The body keeps a single ledger.

This week, audit the three biggest non-cycling stressors in your life. Map them onto the training calendar. If quality sessions are landing on the highest-stress days, move them. The schedule has to flex.

## What an actual recovery week looks like

We're 5 weeks in. Most plans would have you doing a recovery week around now — and most riders do it badly. They drop volume by 30%, keep all the intensity, and call it a recovery week.

A real recovery week:

- Volume drops by 30–50%
- Intensity drops to almost zero — one short opener at the end of the week, optional
- Sleep is protected harder than usual
- Strength: one short session at 50% load, not two
- Long ride is shortened, not skipped
- One genuine rest day with no exercise

This week is your first chance to run one. It's the right time. You've done four weeks of solid building. Take it. Resist the urge to "just add" one more quality session. Trust the process.

## Common mistakes this week

**Filling the rest day with an easy ride.** Real rest is full off. Save the easy spin for tomorrow. The body needs a 24-hour window to do the deep adaptation work.

**Treating recovery as something you do after training breaks you.** Recovery is something you do continuously — sleep, nutrition, stress management — so training never breaks you in the first place.

**Buying a recovery gadget instead of fixing sleep.** If you're below 7.5 hours of sleep on average, no gadget will help. Fix the bedroom, the bedtime, and the alcohol first.

**Skipping the readiness check.** Five minutes a day. The data you collect this week is the foundation for every smart training decision you'll make in the back half of the course.

**Assuming low motivation means you're being lazy.** Persistent low motivation is a recovery flag. Your body is telling you something. Listen.

**Sleeping under 7 hours and complaining the training plan isn't working.** It's not the plan. It's the sleep. Fix the sleep.

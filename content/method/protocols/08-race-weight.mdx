## The conversation nobody's having

Body composition is the hidden universal motivator in cycling. Almost every serious amateur in this audience thinks about it. Very few talk about it openly. And the approaches most cyclists take — restricting calories, skipping meals, fasted rides — are precisely the strategies that tank performance, destroy recovery, and lead to the 9pm fridge raids that undo everything.

This module addresses race weight head-on, without apology. Not through restriction. Through precision. The same principles that took me from 86kg to 79kg in 12 weeks while eating more food than I'd ever eaten — applied at the right phase of the training year, with the right protections in place.

Before anything else: you don't have to do this block. Some riders shouldn't. We'll talk about who should and who shouldn't. If you're already lean for your build, the right answer this week is to read the module, take the principles, and skip the deficit. If you're carrying weight that's costing you watts on the climbs, this is the week to start moving it — but slowly, deliberately, and never at the cost of training quality.

## The first decision — should you be doing this at all?

Three questions:

**1. Is this a Build phase or a Peak phase?** Body composition work belongs in the Build phase or transition periods. You don't chase weight in the four weeks before a peak event — you protect performance instead. We're in late Build. The window is narrowing but still open.

**2. Are your training and sleep dialled?** A nutrition deficit on top of broken sleep and inconsistent training is a recipe for hormonal problems, especially in male riders over 40 and female riders under-fuelled. If sleep is below 7.5 hours and training is irregular, fix those first. Body composition can wait.

**3. Is the gap meaningful?** If you're already at 8–10% body fat as a male rider or in the same proportional range as a female rider, the marginal gain on the bike from another two kilos lost is small and the cost in performance is high. Not every rider should chase race weight. The riders who benefit most are the ones still 5+ kilos above their ideal — usually with body fat in double digits.

If three yeses, this block is for you. If a no, take the principles forward without the deficit.

## Why "eat less, ride more" fails

Dunne is precise about why the popular formula doesn't work for serious cyclists, and the answer matters.

> "From both male and female athletes, there are some serious metabolic implications of not just underfuelling on an absolute level but actually when we start to look at the distribution of it throughout the course of the day. You can cause this additional stress where you have significant hours of being in this chronic deficit where your body is not getting enough fuel to maintain normal physiological processes."

The body works on what physiologists call a law of preservation. When the daily energy availability drops below a threshold — roughly 30 calories per kg of fat-free mass per day in the IOC's RED-S framework — the body starts shutting things down. Recovery suppresses. Hormones drop. Bone density declines. Immune function weakens. Performance plateaus or regresses.

The cyclist who wakes up, has a coffee, rides three hours fasted, eats a small lunch, smashes a Tuesday threshold session in the evening, and has a "light" dinner is putting themselves in chronic deficit for sixteen hours a day. The scale might come down for a few weeks. The performance comes down with it. And then the rebound comes — usually around weeks six to eight — when the body's preservation mechanisms force a binge cycle the rider can't control.

We're not doing that. We're doing precision deficits.

## The Roadman approach — deficit windows

The principle: most days, you're at maintenance or slight deficit. Hard days, you're at maintenance or surplus. Long ride days, you fuel the work. Recovery days, you eat slightly less. The deficit averages out across the week without ever being dangerous on any single day.

In practice:

**Easy / off days:** 200–300 kcal below maintenance. This is the deficit window. Carbs taper at dinner. Protein stays high. Fibre and vegetables fill the volume.

**Quality / hard days:** Maintenance. The session needs the fuel.

**Long ride days:** Maintenance or slight surplus. Fuel the ride properly (use the targets from Week 3) and refuel post-ride. Don't try to "save calories" by under-fuelling the long ride. You'll bonk and binge.

**Recovery weeks:** Maintenance throughout. Don't stack a deficit on top of reduced training — the body is already trying to absorb adaptation. Add deficit pressure and you'll erase the gain.

Across a week with three quality days, three easy days, and one off day, this typically averages out to roughly 1,000–1,500 kcal of weekly deficit. That's about 200g of body fat per week — half a pound. Slow. Sustainable. Protects training. Doesn't trigger the preservation response.

A 12-week block at this rate moves you 5–8kg without ever feeling deprived. That's the maths that worked for me. It works for the riders we coach because it doesn't ask anyone to white-knuckle through hunger.

## Protein is non-negotiable

If you take one number from this module, take this one: 1.8–2.2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight per day. Every day. Especially on deficit days.

Why protein matters more in a deficit:

- It's the most satiating macronutrient — keeps you full at lower calories
- It's the most thermogenic — your body burns more calories digesting it
- It protects muscle mass when you're in deficit (you lose fat, not lean tissue)
- It supports recovery from training that doesn't stop because you're cutting weight

Distribute it across four meals. Breakfast 30g (eggs, Greek yoghurt, protein shake — front-load it). Lunch 30–40g. Snack or post-training 25–30g. Dinner 30–40g. Most riders we work with are eating fine total protein but most of it at dinner. Spread it.

## Sarah Berry's free intervention, again

The meal-order trick from Module 3 matters more in a deficit phase. Same meal, lower glucose peak, slower gastric emptying — meaning you stay full longer on the same calories.

Eat the salad first. The protein next. Then the carbs. Five seconds of behaviour change. Smoother energy through the day, less hunger between meals, easier deficit to sustain.

## The alcohol audit

The single biggest body composition lever for most cyclists in this audience isn't food — it's alcohol. Three pints on Friday night plus two beers on Saturday plus a glass of wine on Sunday is roughly 1,500 calories a week with almost zero satiety, plus a nightly disruption to the deep sleep that drives recovery.

You don't have to go dry. But for the next four weeks, audit it. Know what you're drinking and when. Cut the weekday units to zero. Keep the social weekend if it matters to you. Most riders find dropping weekday alcohol alone produces visible body composition movement within three to four weeks without changing anything else.

If the social piece is important — and Lachlan Morton would tell you it is — protect the Saturday post-ride pint and lose the Tuesday weeknight glass. The trade is favourable.

## The decision tool, every meal

Apply the same training-to-train versus training-to-perform distinction from Module 3. Add one more layer for body composition work:

**Training to perform** (race-pace efforts, key sessions, long rides) — fuel hard. Don't deficit. Don't experiment. Performance days are not weight loss days.

**Training to train** (most Z2, steady aerobic work) — fuel adequately. Carbs in, but moderate. Don't bonk, but don't surplus either.

**Off / recovery days** — gentle deficit window. Protein high. Carbs lower. Vegetables and fibre to fill the plate.

Three buckets. Three different fuelling profiles. Apply them every meal this week.

## The metric that matters — and the one to ignore

The number to track: weekly average weight, taken at the same time of day for seven consecutive days, then averaged. Not daily weight. Daily weight bounces 1–2kg with hydration and gut content; weekly average shows the trend.

The other metrics:

- Waist measurement, fortnightly. Tape doesn't lie.
- Photos every four weeks. The mirror lies; photos don't.
- Performance numbers — power output on a stable benchmark session. If watts hold or improve while weight drops, you're doing it right. If watts drop, the deficit is too aggressive.

Ignore: the daily scale number. The number on social media. The "ideal" race weight calculator. Your weight relative to a pro who has different genetics and a different sport. None of those are useful inputs.

## What you should be feeling

If the deficit is calibrated correctly:

- Energy stays even
- Sleep stays solid
- Hunger appears about an hour before meals — not all day
- Training quality holds
- Mood is stable

If you're experiencing:

- Constant hunger
- Poor sleep
- Crashing afternoon energy
- Power dropping on stable benchmark sessions
- Mood swings or persistent low motivation

The deficit is too big. Bump up to maintenance for a week. Reset. Restart at a smaller deficit. This is not a competition with yourself. The slowest rider to lose the weight wins.

## Common mistakes this week

**Going too hard, too fast.** Aggressive cuts produce rebounds. The 200–300 kcal deficit on easy days only is the floor of what works and the ceiling of what's safe.

**Cutting carbs across the board.** Carbs aren't the enemy. Untimed carbs are the enemy. Move them around the day, don't eliminate them.

**Skipping the long ride fuelling.** Under-fuelling the long ride is the fastest way to wreck the entire deficit block. Fuel the ride, deficit the dinner.

**Stacking a deficit on top of broken sleep.** If you're under 7 hours, fix sleep first. Body composition cannot be rushed against a broken recovery foundation.

**Comparing yourself to a 24-year-old WorldTour rider.** They have different genetics, different load, different recovery, and different decade. Your race weight isn't theirs. Find your own.

**Talking about it constantly.** The riders who chase race weight quietly tend to land it. The riders who post about it daily tend to stall.

## Welcome to Week One

Here's the thing nobody tells you about getting faster: most cyclists who plateau aren't training too little. They're training without knowing where they actually are.

You know your FTP. You know your weekly hours. You probably know your weight to within a kilo. But ask the harder question — what is the real intensity distribution of your last four weeks, what is your honest sleep average, what is your actual available training time once you subtract the commute and the kids' bedtime — and the answer gets fuzzy fast.

That fuzziness is the problem. Every coaching decision you'll make over the next twelve weeks depends on a real picture of where you are right now. Not the version you'd present to a club mate. The honest one. This week is the audit.

## Why this week matters more than the training week that follows

When Professor Seiler walks through how he'd build a plan from scratch, he doesn't start with intervals. He doesn't even start with duration. He starts with a baseline of what the athlete is actually doing — how often they get out the door, what their week looks like, where the load sits. Everything builds from that.

Dan Lorang describes the same thing in different language. When he coaches an amateur, the first thing he does is sit them down with their calendar and ask them to be honest about the working week — not what they'd like the week to look like, but what it really looks like. He's blunt about the consequence of skipping that step: "What people try in general is they get a training plan and try to fit it in somehow and then it just ends up in pure stress and sickness is just a consequence of it."

Sickness is the consequence of training a fictional version of your life. That's why this week comes before the architecture. The architecture has to be built around the real numbers, the real time, and the real you.

## The Entry Assessment — what you're actually measuring

The Entry Assessment Workbook (in your downloads) covers six domains. Not because we love spreadsheets — because each domain is a place performance leaks if you don't know your starting point.

**1. Power baseline.** A current FTP. If you have a recent test (within the last 8 weeks) and you trust the protocol, validate it with a 20-minute effort and use 95% of average. If it's older, retest. The protocol guidance is in the workbook — pick the one that fits your kit and your time. The number isn't the prize. The number is the reference point.

**2. Real training load.** Pull your last four weeks of completed rides from your head unit, your training app, or wherever your data lives. Don't summarise. Don't average. List them — date, duration, average heart rate, perceived effort. We need the texture of the last month, not the headline.

**3. Body composition baseline.** Weight on a normal scale, first thing in the morning, no clothing, after the bathroom. Estimated body fat from waist-to-hip ratio (the workbook has the simple table). Photos from the front, side, back. This is uncomfortable. It's also the only honest baseline. The scale moves around. Tape measures and pictures don't lie.

**4. Sleep average.** Your honest seven-night average for the last fortnight. Not your idealised number. Not what your watch claims. Your real average — bedtime to wake-up, minus the time you were lying there scrolling. If you're below seven hours, write down why. We come back to this in Week 5.

**5. Real available hours.** Block your calendar — the way Lorang describes for the riders he actually coaches. Work blocks. Commute. Family time. Sleep. Showers. The session that takes 90 minutes plus a 20-minute change and shower and a bit of cool-down conversation is not a 90-minute session. It's a two-hour block. Map your real available time. The number that comes out is almost always smaller than people thought, and that's fine — you're going to train inside reality.

**6. The goal.** One primary 12-week goal. Two supporting goals. A single measurable target you'll come back to in Week 12. The "One-Two-Twelve" framework. We'll use the supporting goals as the structure, not the primary goal — because most riders write a primary goal that's emotional and a set of supporting goals that are actually trackable. We work with both.

## Your intensity distribution — and why it's almost certainly wrong

This is the part most riders fight on the way through. Pull your last four weeks of rides and calculate, by minute, the percentage spent in three buckets:

- **Green** — easy, conversational, heart rate flat after warm-up, leaves you with an appetite at the dinner table. This is what Seiler calls Zone 1 in his green-yellow-red model.
- **Yellow** — sustained moderate work. Climbs you push a bit, group rides where you don't want to be the one off the back, threshold sessions that drift up.
- **Red** — actual high-intensity intervals where the work is structured and the recovery is structured.

The polarised model — what almost every successful elite endurance program converges on — is roughly 80% green, very little yellow, the rest red.

The amateur model is, on average, something like 30–40% green, 40–60% yellow, and a smattering of red. Most age-group cyclists ride about 50% too hard when they think they're riding easy.

Don't take that on faith. Calculate it for yourself this week. The number you produce becomes the single most important diagnostic in this entire course. If your yellow zone is bigger than your green zone, you don't need more training — you need different training. We build that in Week 2.

## What you'll likely find — and why that's good news

Most people running this audit honestly for the first time find some version of the following:

- The intensity distribution is upside-down. The yellow zone is the biggest bucket.
- The sleep average is below seven hours and they didn't realise.
- The real available training time is meaningfully less than the time they'd been trying to schedule into.
- They've been doing the same week for months — same routes, same group rides, same Tuesday session — without progression.
- There's no recovery week in the last 12 weeks of training.

If three of those five describe you, you've already learned something more useful than another six weeks of effort. The reason your FTP isn't moving isn't that you don't train hard enough. It's that the training isn't shaped right for adaptation. That's a fixable problem. It's the entire reason this course exists.

## Common mistakes to avoid this week

**Re-writing history to look better.** The audit only works if it's honest. If you didn't ride three Tuesdays in a row because work blew up, write that down. The audit is for you, not for a coach you're trying to impress. The version of you in Week 12 is the audience. They need the truth.

**Re-testing FTP and going too hard.** If you do test this week, treat it as a baseline, not a personal best. A bad test won't sink the course — but a wrecked recovery will eat into Week 2. If the test is questionable, take a recent number and revalidate at the end of the block.

**Building the goal around someone else's calendar.** Your goal has to be yours. Not your buddy's gran fondo. Not a number a coach put on a plan three years ago. One primary goal, two supporting goals, one measurable target. Yours.

**Treating the audit like admin.** The audit is the training this week. The reason coaches charge what they charge is that this is the part most riders skip and it's also the part everything else hangs off. Take it seriously.

**Skipping the body composition baseline because it feels uncomfortable.** It's uncomfortable because it matters. We talk about race weight properly in Week 8. We need the Week 1 number to make Week 8 honest.

## The lie the cycling internet keeps telling

The cycling internet is going to tell you that weight loss is simple. Calories in versus calories out. Burn more than you eat. Ride more. Eat less. This advice is so outdated it's actually making you slower — and in many cases fatter — than the day you started taking it seriously.

Dr David Dunne, who has spent his career working with World Tour riders and the kind of amateurs we serve in this community, opens the conversation with a sentence that lands hard if you've spent years counting calories:

> "What if losing weight is actually making you slower on the bike? He says we've been doing it all wrong."

This module is the reset. Not a diet. Not a meal plan. A fuelling system — the same one that took me from 86 to 79 kilos in 12 weeks while eating more food than I'd ever eaten in my life. Power didn't drop. Energy went up. The 9pm fridge raids stopped. We're going to walk through why that worked when "eat less ride more" failed for years before it.

## Why most cyclists are under-fuelled and don't know it

Dunne is precise about why amateurs carry excess weight despite training ten or twelve hours a week. The popular framing is that they're eating too much. The honest answer is more uncomfortable.

> "If somebody is carrying excess mass or they're deliberately wanting to reduce weight and they're not reducing weight, typically it's going to be because they're not in an energy deficit. But the equation itself is not just your exercise energy expenditure. Your body is going to burn a certain amount of energy at rest, then if we look at that — you're not resting all day — there needs to be an adjustment done based on the type of job you're working at."

The full energy equation has four components: rest, daily activity, training, and the cost of the food itself. If you're sedentary at a desk, jet-fuelling protein at every meal, and not training as hard as you think you are, you can absolutely be in surplus on what looks like a moderate intake. Equally — and this is the trap most serious cyclists fall into — you can chronically under-fuel by trying to "save calories" around training, which puts you in survival mode where the body refuses to release fat and starts protecting itself instead.

Dunne is direct about what happens when you under-fuel for months at a time:

> "You can cause this additional stress where you have significant hours of being in this chronic deficit where your body is not getting enough fuel to maintain normal physiological processes. Your body works off this law of preservation — if we don't have enough energy, we'll start to shut off other processes."

What gets shut off? Recovery. Hormone production. Bone density. Sleep quality. Adaptation. Everything that turns training into improvement.

## The decision tool — training to train, or training to perform?

Before we get to specific numbers, you need a decision framework you'll use every day for the rest of this course. Dunne gives it to us in one question:

> "The first thing I'd ask them to distinguish is: are you training to train — so are you trying to facilitate an adaptation — or are you really training to perform, where it is about going as fast as you can for as long as you can? Once we have that identified then we know the overall aim of the session."

Apply that on every ride this week.

**Training to train** — most Zone 2 sessions, easy spins, structured aerobic work where the goal is to build the engine. Fuel adequately so the work is sustainable, but the carb-per-hour target is moderate (30–60g/h depending on duration).

**Training to perform** — race-pace efforts, your highest-stakes group ride of the week, threshold or VO2 sessions, key event simulations. Fuel hard. 60–90g/h, sometimes more. The goal is to execute the work to the highest possible quality. Adaptation is the byproduct, not the target.

Most amateurs reverse this. They under-fuel hard sessions because they want to "stay lean" and over-fuel easy sessions because they treat every ride the same. The result: the hard sessions are sub-quality and the easy sessions are over-loaded. Reverse it. Hard fuelled hard. Easy fuelled clean and adequate.

## Daily fuelling — the architecture, not the diet

Forget calorie counting. We're going to organise your day around four simple anchors.

**1. A real breakfast on training days.** This is the single biggest change for most riders in this course. A coffee and a banana is not breakfast. A proper plate — oats with milk, fruit and yoghurt, eggs and toast, whatever fits your taste — protein and carbs together, in the morning. If you train fasted because you've been told it builds fat oxidation, here's the trade: you'll bonk on the long ride sixty kilometres from home and hate your life for the next three hours, and the marginal fat-adaptation gain is dwarfed by the loss of training quality. Eat breakfast.

**2. Protein front-loaded across the day.** A useful target — 0.4 grams of protein per kilo of body weight at four meals across the day. For an 80kg cyclist, that's roughly 32g protein per meal, four meals a day. Most riders we work with eat almost no protein at breakfast (toast and jam), modest at lunch (a salad), and a big lump at dinner. The body can't store protein. Spread it out.

**3. Carbs matched to the day.** On hard training days, carbs are the centre of the plate — rice, pasta, potatoes, bread, fruit. On easy or off days, they reduce. Not to zero. Reduce. This is what Dr Dunne calls carbohydrate periodisation: your fuelling should adjust day by day and meal by meal in line with the demands of the activity.

**4. A proper post-training meal within an hour of the long ride.** This is when the body is asking for protein and carbohydrate. Skip it and you've wasted half the long ride.

## In-ride fuelling — the number you've been getting wrong

Here's where the gap between amateur and pro practice is widest, and where the easiest gains live. World Tour riders routinely target 90–120 grams of carbohydrate per hour during racing, and some are now training their guts to tolerate up to 140g/h.

Most amateurs come in around 30–60g/h on long rides — and many do nothing for the first ninety minutes because they're "fasted training."

Alistair Brownlee, no soft touch on training, says it cleanly:

> "There is no doubt that effective fuelling allows you to do more work, to train and then obviously allows you to race longer, faster, harder. Going from 'have a gel when you feel like it' to 'everyone probably can push that envelope up to 90 or even 120 grams an hour' — there's no doubt that allows people to do more work."

The targets for this week, by session type:

- **Short Z2 (under 90 min):** 30–40g/h. Bottle with carb mix or one bar.
- **Long Z2 (90 min–3hr):** 50–70g/h. A mix of bottle carbs, a banana or rice cake, half a bar.
- **Long endurance (3hr+):** 70–90g/h. Carb mix in the bottle plus solid food on the hour.
- **Quality / race-pace:** 80–120g/h. This is where you train your gut to handle the pro range. Build this gradually — start at 60g/h and add 10g/h every week or two.

The gut needs training. If you've been on 30g/h for years, jumping to 90 will produce a gastric event you'd rather not write home about. Work up to it.

## Sarah Berry's free intervention

Dr Sarah Berry's work on personalised nutrition produces one of the most useful insights for cyclists: meal order changes glucose response without changing meal content.

> Eat fibre, fat or protein BEFORE the carbohydrates. Same meal, lower glucose peak. Slows gastric emptying. Free intervention — food order doesn't change food content.

Try it this week. Salad before pasta. Eggs before toast. Yoghurt before granola. Five seconds of behaviour change. Smoother energy through the morning, easier afternoon riding. This is not a fad — it's the cheapest performance intervention in the entire course.

## Common mistakes this week

**Treating fuelling as restriction.** The whole point is the opposite. We're going to add food, especially around training. The leanness comes from training quality and consistency, not from cutting calories.

**Skipping breakfast on training days.** This is the single biggest fuelling mistake we see. Stop.

**Doing fasted "fat-burner" rides.** The fat-adaptation argument doesn't survive contact with the data. Fuel the work.

**Front-loading carbs at dinner.** Most amateurs are carb-stacked at the wrong end of the day — light all day, big plate at 8pm. Move some of those carbs to breakfast and lunch. Sleep improves. Morning training improves.

**Buying twelve different supplements before fixing breakfast.** Whole food first. The supplement aisle is the last 5%. Get the architecture right before you buy the protein powder.

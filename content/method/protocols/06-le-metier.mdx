## Why we stop here

You're at the halfway mark. Five weeks of architecture, fuelling, strength, and recovery — the science of getting faster. Now we step away from the science for a week, because cycling isn't only science. The French call it *le metier* — the craft. The accumulated wisdom of how to ride well that doesn't fit in a spreadsheet.

This module does three things. It runs a mid-course progress check so you can see what six weeks of disciplined work has actually produced. It teaches the pacing and positioning skills that turn fitness into performance. And it reconnects you to why you're doing any of this in the first place — because if you've forgotten why you ride, the next six weeks will feel like a chore instead of what they should feel like, which is a build toward something you actually want.

## The mid-course check — what to measure, what to ignore

Before we get to craft, we measure. Open the Mid-Course Progress Check Worksheet — same six domains as your Week 1 audit:

1. **Power baseline.** A short retest, or validation if you have honest data from the last two weeks of training.
2. **Real training load.** Pull the last six weeks. What's your intensity distribution actually been? Compare it to your Week 1 distribution.
3. **Body composition.** Same scale, same conditions, same time of day. Tape measure on the waist. Optional progress photos.
4. **Sleep average.** Seven nights of honest data from the readiness check you started in Week 5.
5. **Subjective.** Energy, mood, motivation, leg feel — same 1–10 scales as Week 1.
6. **Goal progress.** Are you on track for your Week 12 target? Ahead? Behind?

Write the numbers down. Don't massage them. The Roadman Method works because the numbers are honest. If your Week 6 power is the same as Week 1, that's important information. If it's up by 15 watts, that's also important information. We treat both seriously.

## What the data usually shows at Week 6

In our experience working with riders through this system, what you're likely to see at the mid-course mark:

- **Intensity distribution has shifted.** Your green-zone percentage is usually up by a meaningful chunk — sometimes from 35% to 70% over six weeks. This is the foundation. The aerobic engine is rebuilding even if power hasn't moved yet.
- **Power is flat or up modestly.** Six weeks isn't long enough to see big FTP jumps in most riders, and that's fine. The architecture is what's been laid down. The performance gains come in the back half.
- **Body composition has moved.** Two to three kilos for most riders, even though we haven't been chasing weight. The fuelling change does a lot of the work without pain.
- **Sleep is up.** Whatever the average was in Week 1, it's usually up by 30–60 minutes by Week 6 because the readiness work has surfaced where the leak was.
- **Energy and mood are up.** Subjective scales drift in the right direction. Riders feel less hammered between sessions.

If your data shows three of those five, the system is working. If it doesn't, the recalibration tools are in the workbook — and the Not Done Yet community is where you post for diagnosis. Don't suffer in silence.

## When to recalibrate the 12-week goal

Most goals set in Week 1 hold up at Week 6. Some don't. Two situations where you should recalibrate:

**The goal was too easy and you're already there.** Some riders set conservative goals because they didn't know what was possible. If you've already hit your Week 12 target by Week 6, raise it. Pick the next stretch goal that scares you a bit.

**The goal was wrong-shaped.** A few riders set goals that turn out to be the wrong type of target — they thought they wanted FTP +20w but what they really wanted was to drop a kilo and ride a faster sportive. The data will reveal it. If you set a power goal but the joy is coming from longer endurance rides, swap it.

What you don't do is recalibrate downward because you're behind schedule. Behind is a signal to look at the inputs (sleep, fuelling, intensity discipline, life stress), not to lower the bar.

## Now to the craft

Lachlan Morton has been on the podcast more times than almost anyone, and the conversations always come back to the same theme: cycling isn't only about going fast. He left the World Tour at the height of his ability for a reason — and the reason is the heart of what *le metier* actually means.

> "I joined the World Tour when I was 19 or 20 with Garmin. Up until that point, all my cycling goals were based on winning road races and trying to be a professional in Europe. And then I sort of achieved that early and then just wasn't feeling super fulfilled with that pursuit."

He chased the highest version of competitive performance and got there, and the achievement felt hollow. Not because the racing wasn't good — it was — but because performance without meaning is a thin reward. So he pivoted. Alt-racing. Bike-packing. Long solo days where the meaning is in the ride, not the result.

The lesson for the cyclist sitting at home with a Premium subscription and a TrainingPeaks plan isn't "quit everything and go bike-packing." It's the question Morton asks himself constantly:

> "Sometimes stepping back and looking around and being like, ah, I'm not actually enjoying this. I'm pursuing this for the wrong reason. What's something that would be exciting for me and motivating for me and going to get me out stoked on my bike every day?"

If you're not enjoying it, something needs to change. Not the volume. Not the intensity. The relationship to the bike. This week is a deliberate pause to look at that.

## Pacing — the most undertrained skill in amateur cycling

Onto the practical craft. The single biggest tactical leak in amateur racing and sportives isn't fitness — it's pacing.

The pattern is depressingly consistent. The road tilts up at the bottom of a climb, the heart rate jumps, the rider stays with the front group out of pride, blows up halfway up, and watches the wheel they were on drift away. Five minutes earlier they were strong. Now they're doing limit-state damage control to the top.

Wiggins, when he was at his climbing best, didn't race the climb against his rivals. He rode the climb. His own pace, his own threshold, his own breathing. When you're a level above the field, you can do that. When you're not, you have to make the same decision deliberately, every climb, every time.

The Roadman Climbing Checklist (in your downloads) gives you the five fixable mistakes — pacing, weight-to-power, cadence, position, and the mental game. Of those five, pacing is the one that gives the biggest returns the soonest. Get it right and you can finish ten percent faster on the same fitness.

Three rules for pacing this week:

**1. Start the climb at 90% of your sustainable effort, not 110%.** Most amateurs hit the climb 20% over their sustainable wattage in the first minute. The first minute is where they wreck themselves. Start under, build into it, finish strong.

**2. Pace by RPE, not by what the wheel in front is doing.** If the wheel goes a watt or two above your sustainable, let it go. You'll catch them when they explode at the top. This requires confidence — see the mental game module in Week 10.

**3. Use a target heart rate ceiling on long climbs.** Set the alarm. If the heart rate goes above the ceiling, you back off. The bike is the coach.

## Group riding — read the wheel, don't fight it

Positioning in a group is its own craft, and most amateurs ride at the back because they're nervous. The energy cost of being at the back is significant — you're surfing surges and accelerations all day. The cost of being in the middle of a tight group, on the right wheel, is far less.

Three skills to practise this week if you ride in a group:

- **Read the road, not the wheel.** Looking at the road two riders ahead lets you anticipate. Looking at the wheel in front means you react.
- **Hold position through pulses.** When the pace surges briefly, don't let a gap open. Closing a gap costs three to five times more energy than holding the wheel.
- **Climb where you'll finish.** If the group is going to split on a climb, position yourself where the split happens before it happens — not after.

These aren't fitness gains. They're efficiency gains. Same engine, more outcome.

## The deeper sell

Lachlan Morton made a comment on the podcast that has stayed with me, and I'll borrow it because it sums up what *le metier* is really about:

> "Most people choose to drive a car. Most people choose to be overweight. Most people choose to not get outside in the fresh air. There's something countercultural in the essence of a bike."

Being a cyclist is already choosing differently. The community of serious amateurs you ride with is a community of people who have chosen something specific and harder than the default. That's worth remembering at Week 6, when the discipline of the system is starting to feel routine.

You're not just training. You're practising a craft. A craft has been there for a long time before any of us showed up, and it'll be there long after we're gone. The reason this module sits in the middle of the course is to remind you that the system is in service of the craft — not the other way around.

## Common mistakes this week

**Skipping the mid-course check because the numbers are scary.** They're not scary. They're data. Run them honestly.

**Using "the craft" as an excuse to skip the work.** Some riders read this module and use it as permission to dial back the training. That's not what it says. The craft is on top of the work, not instead of it.

**Ignoring the joy factor.** If you're miserable on the bike, the system isn't working. Joy is a metric. Track it.

**Doing a recovery week and a quality week at the same time.** This week is recalibration. Next week we ramp back up. Don't hit Tuesday's threshold session this week.

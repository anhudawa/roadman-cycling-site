## What you've been doing wrong without realising

Most amateurs do the same training week, month after month, and wonder why their FTP isn't moving. That isn't training. That's exercise. Training has direction. It builds. It progresses. It peaks.

That's periodisation, and it's the single biggest difference between how Dan Lorang builds a season for Primož Roglič and how most amateurs stumble through theirs. It's also the thing the cycling internet has actively confused — by selling you the idea that the same threshold workout repeated forever is "structured training." It isn't. It's just a session schedule.

This module gives you the framework. By the end of the week, you'll know what phase you're in, what comes next, and how to time your training so you arrive at your target event in the form of your life — not the form of your last gran fondo.

## Consistency first — Lorang's opening principle

Before we touch periodisation, Lorang's first principle:

> "If we talk about endurance sport, consistency is quite important. It's not about the one key session, about one big day. It's more about giving your body constantly a certain amount of load. And to realise this, load management is quite important — to avoid injuries, to avoid sickness, to avoid mental issues."

Consistency over heroics. The reason periodisation works is that it gives you a structure that maintains consistency while progressing the load. Without periodisation, riders chase peak sessions and burn through their sustainability; with periodisation, the load builds and the body keeps absorbing it.

Seiler reinforces this from a different angle, with one of the most quoted lines from the entire podcast archive:

> "I had a PowerPoint slide with a number on it like 532. This is a pretty important number what is it? And no, that's the number of sessions that athlete successfully achieved in that year. When athletes stand on the podium and you ask them what's your secret they'll say my secret is I don't have a secret. My secret is that I get the work done."

Five hundred and thirty-two sessions. Across a year. That's the sport at the top — not the perfect interval, but the relentless accumulation of completed sessions. Periodisation is the architecture that lets you keep getting the work done.

## The three-phase model — Base, Build, Peak

Joe Friel's foundational work on periodisation breaks training into three phases. We're going to use a simplified version that works for cyclists with jobs, families, and 8 to 12 hour weeks.

**Base.** The foundation. Lots of Zone 2. Strength is heavy. Intensity is minimal. Goal: build the aerobic engine, fix limiters, lay down volume the body can adapt to. Length: 8–16 weeks if you're starting fresh; 4–6 weeks at the start of a Roadman block.

**Build.** The work that creates performance. Sweet-spot work, threshold intervals, structured high-intensity. Volume drops slightly to make room for the harder work. Strength shifts to maintenance. Goal: convert aerobic capacity into power at the duration that matters for your goal.

**Peak.** The taper into the event. Volume drops further. Intensity stays sharp but reps drop. Strength drops to once a week. Goal: arrive at the start line fresh, sharp, hungry.

For The Roadman Method, you've spent Weeks 2–6 in a Base block. Week 7 starts the Build phase. Weeks 11 and 12 will be Peak / integration.

## Mesocycles — the unit you actually plan in

A periodised plan isn't a single linear arc. It's a series of three-to-four-week blocks called mesocycles. Each mesocycle has a structure:

- **Week 1:** Loading week — moderate progression of volume or intensity
- **Week 2:** Loading week — slightly higher than week 1
- **Week 3:** Peak loading week — the hardest of the block
- **Week 4:** Recovery week — volume drops 30–50%, intensity drops to almost zero

Three weeks loading, one week recovery. Then the next mesocycle starts at a slightly higher absolute load — more volume, harder intervals, or longer race-pace work, depending on the phase.

This is the rhythm of the plan from here forward. You ran your first recovery week in Week 6 (mid-course pivot). Weeks 7, 8 and 9 are loading. Week 10 is a recovery week. Weeks 11 and 12 are taper into the post-course transition.

If you're following the Premium TrainingPeaks plan, this structure is already built. If you're building your own using the Season Planning Template, lock in the 3:1 rhythm first. Don't let the loading phase drift to four weeks. The recovery week is non-negotiable.

## What changes in the Build phase

Practical week shape, this week:

**The long ride stays.** Duration is still doing the heavy lifting in early Build. Don't shorten it. If anything, extend by 15–30 minutes if your bracket allows.

**One sweet-spot session enters the week.** Sweet-spot work — 88–94% of FTP — is the first proper intensity work we've added. It's the highest aerobic load with the lowest neuromuscular cost, which is why it's the natural opener for the Build phase. The session in the workbook is 3 × 10 minutes at sweet-spot, building to 3 × 15 over the next two weeks.

**Strength stays at two sessions, stable load.** Don't increase the gym work as cycling intensity goes up. Hold maintenance.

**Recovery rides do not get harder.** This is where most riders sabotage themselves. They add intensity to the hard sessions and accidentally add intensity to the easy sessions. Keep the easy days easy. The polarised principle holds even harder when the hard work gets harder.

**Sleep gets MORE protected.** As intensity goes up, the recovery cost goes up. The 7.5–8 hour sleep target you've been working on becomes non-negotiable.

## Heart rate vs power — Lorang's preference for the daily metric

When the work gets harder, the question of how to control intensity gets more important. Lorang has a strong view here, and it's worth knowing why:

> "I'm honest, I'm a big fan of heart rate. If I would have to choose between one of both I would always take heart rate. Because heart rate is always the reaction of your body to something, to a load that you give. If you say the athlete you stay at 130 heart rate, that's still the same."

His point: heart rate integrates everything — fatigue, life stress, altitude, dehydration, illness, hormonal cycle. Power doesn't. If you blindly chase a power number on a day your body is at 80%, you do too much. If you use a heart rate cap, the body self-regulates.

For Build-phase intervals, the practical recommendation: prescribe by power, but cap by heart rate. If the prescribed sweet-spot is 240 watts but your heart rate climbs above your sweet-spot ceiling within the first interval, you're tired. Drop the wattage 5–10% for the day. Don't grind through.

## Periodise life, not just training

Lorang puts this on a level with periodising training, and it's an idea most amateurs ignore until it breaks them:

> "Share it with the people around you. When I coached age groupers — by sharing this is also a big motivation for them and it just takes people on board to your project. When you have stress with the family, with your girlfriend, your wife — this really has a big impact on your health, on how you feel, on your performance. So this is always something what you try to avoid if possible."

Look at your next 12 weeks. Where are the high-stress life weeks? End-of-quarter at work. School holidays. A move. A wedding. Map them onto the training plan now. If a peak loading week falls on a high-stress life week, swap the order — load when life is calm, recover when life is loud.

The body keeps a single ledger. Plan accordingly.

## What NOT to chase — Lorang on altitude camps

Worth flagging because some riders in this audience will be tempted: altitude training is generally a bad bet for amateurs.

> "If you have not so much time, if you basically just have two weeks, then in my opinion it makes more sense to go two weeks in the south making a proper training camp. In altitude there are so many things what you can do wrong that there's a high risk that the performance benefit will not be there."

A two-week training camp at sea level — in good weather, with company, eating well, riding consistently — will produce more performance gain for almost every amateur than a two-week altitude block where you're more likely to under-recover, under-fuel, and travel home flat.

If you have a window for a training camp during this course, plan it. Sea level. Good food. A pile of rides. Don't chase altitude.

## Common mistakes in the Build phase

**Adding too much intensity at once.** The temptation is to add sweet-spot AND threshold AND VO2 in the same week because the legs feel good. Don't. We add one new intensity per mesocycle.

**Skipping the recovery week.** This is the single most predictable failure point of any periodised plan. The recovery week feels like wasted time when you're flying. It isn't. It's where the adaptation lands.

**Letting the long ride drift to "moderate."** The long ride is still Z2. Don't accidentally turn it into a third quality session because you're feeling sharp.

**Ignoring the readiness check.** This is when the morning numbers matter most. If RHR is up, sleep is short, and legs feel heavy — back off. The plan flexes around the body, not the other way round.

**Building backwards from a target without backwards-counting the recovery weeks.** If your event is 8 weeks out, count the mesocycles backwards. Don't squeeze a 12-week plan into 8 weeks by skipping recovery.

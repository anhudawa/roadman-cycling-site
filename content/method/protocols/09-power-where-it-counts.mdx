## Why torque work deserves a precise prescription

You're nine weeks in. You've built the architecture, fuelled it, recovered properly, and run six weeks of strength work. Now we add the on-bike session that converts all of it into power on the road.

Low-cadence torque intervals are often discussed as if every big-gear effort were the same. They are not. Cadence, power, duration, recovery, frequency, rider history and the surrounding programme all change the dose. Professional coaches use torque work, but their practice does not make one protocol universally superior or appropriate.

A [2024 PLOS ONE trial](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0311833) gives us one promising, specific example. Twenty-four well-trained female cyclists aged 17–20 completed an intensive eight-week polarised programme. The low-cadence group performed maximal 30-second sprints at 50–60 RPM and four-minute intervals at 90–100% of maximal aerobic power at 60–70 RPM; the comparison group used freely chosen cadence above 80 RPM. Group-average VO2max changed by 8.7% versus 4.6%, and maximal aerobic power by 8.1% versus 3.0%, with significant group-by-time effects.

That result supports testing the complete protocol in a suitable population. It does not show that a single amateur torque session doubles gains, that 40–60 RPM is the study prescription for every interval, or that the result transfers unchanged to masters riders. The authors explicitly said their small sample was not representative of the general population of training female cyclists.

## What may explain the response

At the same power, fewer crank revolutions require more torque per stroke. That is the directly observable mechanical change. Possible physiological explanations include different motor-unit recruitment and neuromuscular demands, but the 2024 trial did not measure muscle-fibre recruitment, mitochondrial development or capillary changes directly.

**1. Higher torque per stroke.** The rider produces the target power in fewer revolutions. That creates the intended force-production challenge and is also why cadence and power must be progressed rather than chased.

**2. A different coordination task.** Lower-rpm work changes how force is organised across the pedal cycle. It may help a rider tolerate event-specific force demands, but there are no "free watts" without training and recovery cost.

**3. Protocol-specific aerobic loading.** The positive trial combined low cadence with sprint and high-intensity work inside a monitored polarised programme. It did not establish torque work as a time-saving replacement for endurance volume.

## Why the wider evidence stays mixed

Different studies used different riders and doses. In [22 trained veteran cyclists](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24550843/), 12 weeks of twice-weekly moderate intervals at 40 RPM produced no measured benefit. In [16 endurance-trained male cyclists](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27175601/), both high- and low-cadence groups improved after six weeks, with different changes across outcomes.

Those findings do not cancel the 2024 result, and the 2024 result does not make earlier null or mixed findings "wrong." Together they show that population, cadence, intensity and the full programme matter.

Coaching experience can generate a useful method. Research helps define what was tested, what changed and how uncertain the transfer is. The strongest prescription keeps both forms of information labelled.

## The two sessions — exactly what to do this week

**Session 1: The torque interval (climb-based)**

A conservative introduction for a pain-free rider:

- Find a climb with a sustained 4–7% gradient. You want the same gradient for every rep — not rolling, not flat, not super steep.
- Warm up properly. 20 minutes building from very easy to high Z2.
- Settle into the climb. Stay seated. Cadence 60–70 RPM. Use a controlled tempo-to-low-threshold effort rather than trying to reproduce the study's maximal-aerobic-power intervals.
- Hold for 4 minutes.
- Roll back down to recover for 4 minutes. Easy spin, around 90+ RPM, to flush the legs.
- Repeat 2–3 times.
- Cool down 15 minutes easy.

Total session time: roughly 60–75 minutes. Initial quality work: 8–12 minutes at low cadence under load.

Form notes: stay seated. Don't grind from the hips. Drive through the heel. Engage the core — if your lower back fires up, drop the gear one and re-engage your trunk. The Pallof press and dead bug work from the gym is exactly the trunk strength this session demands.

**Session 2: Flatter sustained torque (later in the week)**

Once you have tolerated several climb-based sessions, use a longer flatter version as an alternative progression:

- Find a flat or false-flat road. No traffic, no junctions, no stops.
- Warm up 20 minutes.
- Push into a higher gear than you'd normally use for the wattage. Cadence 50–65 RPM. Effort RPE 6–7.
- Hold for 8 minutes.
- 4 minutes easy spin recovery.
- Repeat 3 times.
- Cool down 15 minutes.

Total session time: roughly 80 minutes. Total quality work: 24 minutes at low cadence.

The flatter version emphasises sustained force production. Do not make it a second compulsory torque day; replace the shorter session and monitor the next-day response.

## Where these sessions fit in the periodised plan

We're in the back half of the Build phase. Power development is the priority. Sweet-spot from Week 7 stays in the schedule. Torque enters now. By Week 10, the body composition work is mostly done and the focus is fully on race-pace performance work into the taper.

The week, this week:

- **Long ride** (Z2) — duration extends if your bracket allows
- **Torque Session 1** (climb-based) — quality day; replace it with Session 2 only after progression
- **Recovery / easy ride**
- **Sweet-spot session** (from Week 7) — maintained at 3 × 15 minutes
- **Strength session 1** — Foundation/Build phase, hold load
- **Easy endurance or recovery** — do not add a second torque day by default
- **Strength session 2** — hold load, drop one set if cumulative fatigue is high
- **Off day**

This is the highest cumulative load week of the course so far. Sleep gets the most protection of any week. The readiness check is non-negotiable. If the morning numbers say you're tired, swap a torque session for an easy spin. We are not grinding through fatigue this week.

## Power profiling — know your limiter

The Power Profile Self-Assessment in your downloads asks you to test or estimate your power across four durations:

- 5 seconds (sprint)
- 1 minute (anaerobic capacity)
- 5 minutes (VO2 max)
- 20 minutes (threshold)

Plotted relative to body weight, you'll see your "shape." Riders typically fall into three rough categories:

- **Diesel.** Strong sustained, weaker on the kicks. Threshold-strong, sprint-weak. Usually long-effort cyclists, gran fondo specialists, time trialists.
- **Puncher.** Strong on 1–5 minute efforts, weaker on long sustained. Usually criterium and short-course riders.
- **Sprinter.** Strong 5-second peak, weaker everywhere else. Track and bunch-finish specialists.

Most riders in this audience are some flavour of diesel — and that's fine. But knowing your shape tells you what to train. A diesel doing constant threshold work is reinforcing a strength. Adding torque and short VO2 work fixes the limiter. A puncher doing short intervals only is the same trap in reverse.

The Premium plan has a personalised power-development block based on your profile. If you're running the Standard tier, the manual has guidance on which sessions to bias depending on which limiter you're addressing.

## The compound interest of S&C and torque

The reason we sequenced strength in Week 4 and torque in Week 9 is that they compound. The Foundation Phase strength work has built the underlying force capacity. The torque intervals on the bike force the body to use that strength in pedalling-specific positions. Strength alone produces gym-strong cyclists. Torque alone produces under-strengthened riders chasing recruitment they don't have. Together — proper compound interest.

Tim Kerrison, when he was head of performance at Team Sky / Ineos, was one of the first coaches to make this case publicly. The team's domestiques, the ones doing the long pulls on the front of the bunch, weren't just aerobic monsters — they were strong. Heavy lifting in the off-season, torque work on the bike year-round. The result was riders who could hold high sustained power up gradients for 20+ minutes when it mattered.

You're doing a smaller version of that. The principles are the same.

## Common mistakes this week

**Going too heavy on the gear.** Low-cadence work is uncomfortable, but it's not supposed to be a 1RM grind. If your knees are screaming or you're rocking in the saddle, the gear is too big. RPE 7 means hard but controlled.

**Doing torque on a hill that's too steep or too rolling.** Find the right hill. Sustained gradient. No surges in cadence. If the only hill near you is 12%, find a different one or use the flat version.

**Doing torque on broken legs.** This is a high-quality session. If you're in the deep fatigue end of the week, swap to easy. Quality over completion.

**Stacking torque on top of strength on the same day.** Don't. Two quality sessions. Two strength sessions. Plus a long ride. Plus a sweet-spot. That's a full week. Don't compound them.

**Letting the easy days drift.** With this many quality sessions in a week, the easy days have to be properly easy. The polarised principle is sharper now than ever.

**Skipping fuelling on torque days.** These sessions cost. Fuel them at race-pace levels — 60–90g/h on the bike. This is a training-to-perform session, not a training-to-train session.

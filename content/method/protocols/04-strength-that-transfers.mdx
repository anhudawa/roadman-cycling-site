## Why we're talking about the gym in Week 4

You're three weeks in. The training architecture is shaping up. The fuelling has clicked. And now I'm telling you to spend an hour a week off the bike, in the gym, lifting weights. If your reaction is some version of "I came here to ride faster, not look like a CrossFitter" — fair. Let me walk you through why every World Tour coach we've had on the podcast prescribes some version of this work, and why for cyclists past 35 it's not optional.

The first time strength training came up in the Roadman world properly was during a long conversation with Derek Teel, who has spent his career working with cyclists in the gym. He was unequivocal:

> "There's the obvious answer which is to increase your force production. But it's pretty undeniable at this point that strength training benefits just about everything on the bike with the exception of your VO2 max. Even longer efforts have been shown to be improved by strength training."

Even longer efforts. That's the line that should make you sit up if you've been resisting. Strength work doesn't just help you sprint or finish a punchy climb. It improves cycling efficiency — the watts you cost per heartbeat — across the entire intensity range. And a meta-analysis in the European Journal of Applied Physiology pooled 17 studies covering 262 cyclists and confirmed the picture: heavy strength training significantly improves cycling efficiency, anaerobic power, and time-trial performance, with no negative effect on VO2 max.

That's the evidence base. Now let's talk about why this matters more after 40 than at any other point in your cycling life.

## The masters case for the gym

After the age of 40, you lose roughly 8% of your muscle mass per decade. After 70, the rate nearly doubles to about 15% per decade. The fibres you lose fastest are the type 2 fibres — the ones responsible for explosive power, surges, the moment you stand up to close a gap on a climb.

If you do nothing, those fibres atrophy. Your sprints get worse. Your ability to respond to a kick on a group ride disappears. Your "good day" power numbers drift downward year on year, and you assume that's just age. It isn't. Some of it is. Most of it is detraining the type 2 system you stopped recruiting.

The solution isn't more zone 2. The solution isn't another set of carbon wheels. It's two strength sessions a week, year-round, lifting heavy enough to recruit type 2 fibres. That's the Roadman position, and it's not a controversial one — it's where every coach we've talked to over the last 12 months lands when the conversation turns to athletes over 35.

## The trade-off — 60 minutes off the bike, more gain

Time-crunched cyclists often resist the gym because they think they don't have the hours. Derek Teel makes the case directly:

> "Even if you were to cut 60 minutes out total of your riding per week and you dedicated that to two 30-minute strength training sessions spread throughout the week, I would be shocked if you did not feel significantly better opposed to just doing a little bit more of the same."

Trade an hour of bike volume — most likely an hour from the yellow zone, where it wasn't doing much for you anyway — for an hour of strength work. Two 30-minute sessions. That's the entire commitment.

If you've been resisting the gym because you thought it would cost you on the bike, the trade is favourable. Better engine, less injury risk, more durable through the season.

## The Roadman programme — what you actually do

The S&C Manual in your downloads has the full programme with photos, sets, reps and progressions across three phases — Foundation, Build, Maintain. We're keeping the structure compatible with cyclists in the 35–55 age bracket, which means we steer clear of the heaviest barbell movements that carry injury risk on imperfect form. Two principles:

**Principle 1: Cycling-specific transfer.** Every exercise is selected because it transfers to power output on the bike — primarily through the posterior chain (glutes, hamstrings, back) and the trunk. We don't waste sessions on bench press and bicep curls.

**Principle 2: Form before load.** Heavy enough to drive adaptation, light enough that you can hold form for every rep. If you can't hold form, the weight is too high. If you can do 12 reps comfortably, the weight is too low. The sweet spot for cycling-specific strength is 5–8 reps per set, near maximal effort, with full recovery between sets.

The Foundation Phase circuit — Week 4 to Week 8 — uses dumbbell, kettlebell, and bodyweight movements you can do at home or in any commercial gym. The exercises:

- **Reverse lunge, dumbbells in hand** — single-leg, posterior chain bias, transfers directly to pedalling force without the load and balance demands of a back squat
- **Dumbbell hip thrust or single-leg hip thrust** — the glute exercise most cyclists are missing
- **Step-ups, weighted** — single-leg power, mimics the climbing action
- **Single-leg dumbbell hip hinge** — hamstring development that fixes the compensation pattern Derek Teel highlighted, controlled and low-load
- **Push-ups, progressive** — upper body and core integration without barbell pressing
- **Pallof press / anti-rotation** — trunk stability that protects the lower back on long rides
- **Dead bug** — core activation that addresses the lower-back compensation most cyclists feel after 90 minutes in the saddle

Three to four sets per exercise. Five to eight reps. Full recovery — two to three minutes between heavy sets. The session takes 30 minutes if you stay focused.

## Why the hamstrings matter (and why your back keeps complaining)

Two specific compensation patterns Derek Teel calls out, both of which most cyclists in this audience will recognise.

**The hamstring leak.** Cyclists are dominantly quad-driven. Years of pedalling without correcting it means the hamstrings underperform. The hamstrings should pull through the back of the pedal stroke — and when they don't, your quads work harder for the same power.

> "If you don't have your hamstrings supporting them a little bit and working a little bit you're not going to be getting as much out of your legs as you should. You can train it in a very controlled place like the gym where you put yourself in positions where it's almost impossible for your hamstrings not to be the primary mover."

That's why the single-leg hip hinge and hip thrust work is non-negotiable in the Foundation Phase. They train the back of the leg in positions where the hamstrings can't hide — and they do it without the high-load spinal compression of barbell movements that aren't worth the risk for cyclists in this age bracket.

**The lower-back compensation.** Cyclists who feel their lower back light up after 90 minutes are almost always running an anti-pattern: weak abs, weak glutes, lower back trying to stabilise everything. Teel again:

> "If your lower back is getting fired up and it's compensating for what your core should be doing — it's trying to stabilise and work on overdrive because your abs are so inactive or you have no glute engagement. These are all the muscles that should be firing to protect your low back. How would you correct that on the bike? You really can't."

You can't correct it on the bike — you correct it in the gym. Pallof press, dead bugs, hip thrusts. The back stops complaining within four to six weeks.

## How to fit the sessions into the week

Two 30-minute sessions, spaced 72 hours apart. The simplest schedule:

- **Tuesday strength.** Light ride day or rest day. Strength session in the morning or early evening.
- **Friday strength.** Pair with an easy spin or full rest. Saturday's long ride still works — you're 18 hours out.
- **Avoid:** strength the day before a quality session, strength on the same day as the long ride, two strength sessions back-to-back.

The 24 hours after a strength session, your legs may feel heavy. That's not a sign the session was wrong. That's the work. Don't try to do a quality session on top of it. By 48 hours later, the legs are back. By 72, you've adapted.

## What strength training costs you that doesn't show up in TrainingPeaks

Worth flagging — Derek Teel has called it out, and it's important.

> "You just put so much damage on your system that you're going to have to recover from that you could feel for multiple days from those three reps. You would never ever get credit for a TSS score from something like that."

A heavy strength session won't show up as a meaningful TSS number. But it absolutely costs you recovery. Don't trust TrainingPeaks alone — your fatigue score will say you're fresh when your legs are still cooked from Tuesday's hip thrusts. Subjective check matters.

## Common mistakes this week

**Going too heavy in week one.** First session, you don't know what's going to break you. Start with weights you can confidently complete every rep with form. Add load in week two and three. By week four of the Foundation Phase, you'll be heavy enough.

**Pairing strength with quality cycling.** Don't put strength the day before a hard interval session. Don't put it the day of a long ride. We schedule for 72 hours between strength and key bike sessions.

**Treating it as cardio.** This is not a HIIT session. The rest periods between sets are the work — that's how you recruit type 2 fibres. If you're sweating like you just ran a 5k, you're going too light too fast.

**Skipping the protein meal afterwards.** 30g of protein within 90 minutes. This is when the muscle adaptation is laid down. Skip it and you've left half the gain on the gym floor.

**Quitting after three weeks.** Strength gains in cycling come around weeks 4–8 of consistent work. If you bail after three weeks, you got the fatigue without the adaptation. Stay with it.

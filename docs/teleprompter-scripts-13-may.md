## SCRIPT 3: UNDER-RECOVERED PROFILE (60–90 seconds)

So your diagnostic came back under-recovered. Here's the thing nobody tells you about that.

You're not undertrained. You're actually doing too much. Or more accurately, you're not recovering from what you're doing.

Professor Seiler's been saying this for years — adaptation doesn't happen during the session. It happens between sessions. If your body never gets the signal that it's safe to rebuild, it won't rebuild. You just accumulate fatigue and wonder why your FTP hasn't moved in eighteen months.

I see this constantly. Riders training ten, twelve hours a week, sleeping six and a half hours, working full-time, and wondering why the legs never come good.

Here's what I'd tell you to do this week. Two hard days max. Everything else is genuinely easy. Not tempo. Easy. And protect your sleep like it's a session — seven and a half hours minimum, consistent wake time, no screens past ten.

The riders who break through aren't the ones training hardest. They're the ones who recover properly between the hard sessions. That's not my opinion. That's what the research shows over and over.

If you want someone watching your numbers and calling the session before you dig yourself into a hole, that's exactly what we do inside Not Done Yet. But either way — back off this week. See what happens.

## SCRIPT 4: NO-MAN'S-LAND PROFILE (60–90 seconds)

Your diagnostic flagged polarisation — and honestly, this is the most common one we see.

Here's what's happening. Most of your riding is stuck in the middle. Not easy enough to recover from. Not hard enough to drive adaptation. Tempo, sweet spot, moderately hard — it all feels productive. It's not.

Stephen Seiler's research across twenty years of studying elite endurance athletes comes back to the same finding. Roughly eighty percent of training should be below your first ventilatory threshold — zone one, zone two. Properly easy. Heart rate in the one-twenties, nose-breathing easy. And then twenty percent should be properly hard. Zone four, zone five. The stuff that actually hurts.

Dan Lorang does the same thing with his athletes. They don't do more work than everyone else. They do the right work at the right intensity.

Here's what I'd change this week. On every easy ride, breathe only through your nose. The moment you need to open your mouth, slow down. For most riders this means dropping fifteen to thirty watts below where you normally ride easy. It feels embarrassingly slow. That discomfort is telling you how far into no-man's-land you've drifted.

Then pick one day for one properly hard session. Five by four minutes at a hundred and five percent FTP. That's your week. That's the fix.

Inside Not Done Yet we build this distribution into every training block so you don't drift back. But start with the nose-breathing test. You'll see it immediately.

## SCRIPT 5: STRENGTH GAP PROFILE (60–90 seconds)

So your diagnostic came back strength gap. Here's what that actually means.

Your aerobic engine is probably fine. The problem is what's underneath it. After about forty, we lose roughly one percent of muscle mass per year without deliberate strength work. Your cardio system still works. But the neuromuscular power that lets you follow an attack, finish a sprint, grind a ten-minute climb — that's quietly leaking away.

Phil Burt spent twelve years as Head of Physiotherapy at British Cycling. Worked with Wiggins, Froome, Kenny — the lot. His position is clear. Cyclists who don't do targeted strength work break down. It's not a question of if. It's when.

But this isn't just about not getting injured. Strength work drives power output. Research consistently shows two to three sessions per week of cycling-specific strength work can improve time to exhaustion at threshold by fifteen to twenty percent.

Here's what I'd start this week. Two gym sessions. Not bodyweight. Not bands. Proper resistance work. Split squats, hip hinges, single-leg deadlifts, hip thrusts, presses, core. Progressed gradually. And on the bike, add some neuromuscular work — ten-second maximal sprints, standing starts, short hills.

Inside Not Done Yet, the S and C roadmap sits alongside the bike work, not bolted on the end. But you can start the gym work yourself this week. Two sessions. See how the legs feel after a month.

## SCRIPT 6: FUELLING DEFICIT PROFILE (60–90 seconds)

Your diagnostic came back fuelling deficit. And I'll be honest — this one frustrates me more than any of the others.

Because the cycling internet has spent twenty years telling you lighter is faster. Eat less. Ride more. Chase the race weight. And that advice is doing more damage than almost anything else in the sport.

Here's the reality. Chronic low energy availability means your body can't build power. You're training, but every session is being paid for with tomorrow's adaptation. The body can't rebuild when it's managing a shortage. So you plateau, and it looks like a training problem. It's not. It's a fuel problem.

This is widespread in masters men and almost never diagnosed. Female cyclists hit it harder and earlier, but the pattern is the same.

Here's what I'd change this week. Eighty grams of carbs per hour on any ride over two hours. Non-negotiable. A proper meal within an hour of finishing — carbs and protein. And let go of the weight number for twelve weeks. Just twelve weeks. Watch what happens to your power.

The coaches who work with pros now fuel them aggressively. Dan Lorang's athletes eat. Properly. The days of starving yourself fast are over. The science has finally caught up with what the best coaches already knew.

Inside Not Done Yet, nutrition is built into the same plan as the training. But start by fuelling the work this week. You'll feel the difference within days.

## DELIVERY NOTES (ALL SCRIPTS)

Pace: Conversational. Coffee stop energy. Not rushed, not performative.

Energy: Calm authority at the open. Genuine frustration on behalf of the stuck rider in the middle. Confident and warm on the close.

Namedrops: Throw them away mid-sentence. Don't pause. Don't list them like a CV.

Eye line: Straight to lens. No looking off-camera. One take per script if possible.

What to avoid: Don't smile too much at the top. Don't speed up at the end. The close should feel like a favour, not a pitch.

If a profile script runs long: Cut the research stats. Keep the "here's what I'd do this week" section — that's the bit they'll remember.

# TELEPROMPTER SCRIPTS — THE ROADMAN METHOD (12 MODULES)

Twelve scripts. One per module. Record in order. Same setup as the diagnostic profile scripts — dark background, tight frame, straight to lens. Each script runs 60–90 seconds.

---

## SCRIPT 1: WHERE YOU ACTUALLY ARE (60–75 seconds)

Here's the thing nobody tells you about getting faster. Most cyclists who plateau aren't training too little. They're training without knowing where they actually are.

You know your FTP. You know your weekly hours. But ask yourself the harder question — what is the real intensity distribution of the last four weeks? What is your honest sleep average? How many hours do you actually have once you subtract the commute and the kids' bedtime?

That fuzziness is the problem. When Dan Lorang sits down with a new athlete — the guy coaching Pogacar — the first thing he does is make them map their real week. Not the week they'd like to have. The real one. Because training a fictional version of your life, as he puts it, ends in sickness.

This week is the audit. FTP, body comp, sleep, hours, intensity split. The Entry Assessment Workbook walks you through all six domains. It's uncomfortable. It's also the most important thing you'll do in this entire course. Everything we build over the next twelve weeks depends on numbers that are honest, not numbers that make you feel good.

Open the protocol. Download the workbook. Do the audit before the week is out.

## SCRIPT 2: BUILDING YOUR TRAINING ARCHITECTURE (60–75 seconds)

So here's what most cyclists are actually doing with their training. They're riding everything in the middle. Not easy enough to recover from. Not hard enough to drive adaptation. Tempo, sweet spot, moderately hard group rides — it all feels productive. It's not.

Professor Seiler's research across twenty years comes back to the same finding. Roughly eighty percent of training should be properly easy. Zone one, zone two. Heart rate flat, nose-breathing, appetite at the end. Then twenty percent should be properly hard. The stuff that actually hurts. Everything in between? That's the grey zone, and it's where most amateurs live.

This week you build the architecture that fixes that. We've got templates for six, eight, ten, and twelve hour weeks. You pick your bracket, lock the long ride in first — that's the anchor, not the leftover — place one quality session midweek, and fill the rest with genuinely easy riding. Set a heart-rate ceiling alarm on your head unit so you can't drift into yellow on green days.

The protocol walks you through every session type, every placement decision, every common mistake. Read it. Build your week. And then ride it honestly.

## SCRIPT 3: FUELLING THE ENGINE (75–90 seconds)

Here's something I wish someone had told me three years ago. Under-eating is probably the single biggest hidden plateau in amateur cycling.

The cycling internet has spent twenty years telling you lighter is faster. Eat less. Ride more. Chase the race weight. And that advice is doing more damage than almost anything else in the sport. Dr David Dunne — who works with World Tour riders — is blunt about it. Chronic low energy availability means your body can't build power. You're training, but every session is being paid for with tomorrow's adaptation. The body can't rebuild when it's managing a shortage.

I lost seven kilos in twelve weeks. Went from eighty-six to seventy-nine. And I did it while eating more food than I'd ever eaten in my life. I didn't download MyFitnessPal. I didn't start skipping meals. I fuelled the work and let the body composition follow.

This week, three things. First, eighty to a hundred and twenty grams of carbs per hour on any ride over ninety minutes. Non-negotiable. Second, a real breakfast on every training day — not just a coffee and a banana. Thirty grams of protein at that first meal. Third, eat within an hour of finishing the long ride. Carbs and protein.

The protocol covers the full framework — daily fuelling, in-ride targets, the difference between training to train and training to perform. Read it this week. Start eating like an athlete.

## SCRIPT 4: STRENGTH THAT TRANSFERS TO THE PEDALS (60–75 seconds)

I know what you're thinking. You signed up for a cycling course and now I'm telling you to go to the gym. Fair. Let me tell you why every World Tour coach we've had on the podcast prescribes this work, and why for cyclists past thirty-five it's not optional.

After forty, you lose roughly eight percent of your muscle mass per decade. The fibres you lose fastest are the type two fibres — the ones responsible for closing gaps, finishing climbs, responding to attacks. Your aerobic engine still works. But the neuromuscular power underneath it is quietly leaking away.

Derek Teel, who's spent his career working with cyclists in the gym, put it simply. Even if you cut sixty minutes off your riding and gave it to two thirty-minute strength sessions, he'd be shocked if you didn't feel significantly better. Two sessions a week. Dumbbells. Single-leg dominant. Split squats, hip hinges, hip thrusts, core. No heavy barbell work. No wrecked legs. Controlled, progressive, paired with easy ride days.

The S&C Manual and the Foundation Phase circuit card are in your downloads. Watch the demos. Schedule the two sessions. And post a form-check video in the community thread — that's what it's there for.

## SCRIPT 5: THE ART OF GETTING FASTER BY DOING LESS (60–75 seconds)

Here's the thing nobody tells you about getting faster. It doesn't happen during training. It happens between sessions. The training is the stimulus. The improvement is laid down in recovery. That sentence is easy to nod at and impossible to live by.

Seiler tells this story about the coaches behind Gustaf Iden and Christian Blumenfeldt — two of the best triathletes on the planet at the time. He asked them what recovery modalities they use. Cold plunge? Massage guns? Compression boots? They said no. They said the only one we use is sleep. Because they worked out that if they skip the massage and the cold plunge, they have more time to sleep. And sleep is the weapon.

That's the most powerful recovery insight in the entire archive of this podcast. Sleep. Not a gadget. Not a supplement. Seven and a half hours minimum, consistent wake time, no screens past ten.

This week you build the sleep protocol and the daily readiness check. Four numbers every morning, before coffee, before your phone. Hours slept, quality score, resting heart rate, leg feel. Run it for seven days and you'll start to see your own patterns. The protocol covers everything — HRV, deload weeks, life stress mapping. But start with sleep. Sort that and the rest follows.

## SCRIPT 6: LE METIER — THE CRAFT (60–75 seconds)

You're halfway through. Five weeks of architecture, fuelling, strength, and recovery. The science of getting faster. Now we step away from the science for a week, because cycling isn't only science.

The French call it le metier. The craft. The accumulated wisdom of how to ride well that doesn't fit in a spreadsheet.

When I had Lachlan Morton on the podcast, he talked about leaving the World Tour at the height of his ability. He'd achieved everything competitive cycling could offer and it felt hollow. Not because the racing wasn't good — it was. But performance without meaning is a thin reward. The question he kept coming back to was this — am I actually enjoying this? Am I pursuing it for the right reason?

That's your question this week too. Not are you training hard enough. Are you still enjoying the bike?

This module does three things. First, it runs a mid-course progress check. Same six domains as Week 1. You measure what's moved. Second, it teaches the pacing strategies that separate strong climbers from riders who blow up at the bottom. Third, it reconnects you with why you're doing any of this.

Plan one ride this week with no power meter visible. Just ride. The protocol and the Pacing Cards are in your downloads.

## SCRIPT 7: PERIODISATION — TRAINING THAT GOES SOMEWHERE (60–75 seconds)

Here's the difference between training and exercise. Exercise is repeating the same week, month after month, and wondering why your FTP hasn't moved. Training has direction. It builds. It progresses. It peaks.

Most amateurs do the same Thursday session fifty-two weeks a year. That's not structured training. That's a session schedule. And it's the single biggest reason your fitness plateaus even though you're putting in the hours.

Dan Lorang builds a season for Roglič using three phases. Base — lots of zone two, heavy strength, minimal intensity. Build — sweet spot, threshold work, volume drops slightly to make room for the harder sessions. Peak — the taper into the event. Sharp intensity, fewer reps, arrive at the start line fresh. Seiler backs the same model. Friel wrote the book on it.

You've been in a Base block for the first six weeks of this course. This week starts the Build phase. Three weeks of progressive load, one recovery week, repeat. We're adding the first sub-threshold sweet-spot session. The Season Planning Template in your downloads maps your next twelve weeks into mesocycles.

Read the protocol. Identify your phase. Map the plan. Training that goes somewhere starts with knowing where you're going.

## SCRIPT 8: RACE WEIGHT WITHOUT THE MISERY (75–90 seconds)

Body composition is the universal motivator in cycling that nobody talks about openly. Almost every serious amateur thinks about it. Very few address it properly. And the approaches most cyclists take — restricting calories, skipping meals, fasted rides — are precisely the strategies that destroy performance.

Here's where it gets really interesting. The solution isn't eating less. It's eating differently at different times.

The Roadman approach uses deficit windows. A small deficit — two to three hundred calories — on easy days only. Never on quality days. Never on long ride days. Never when sleep is broken. You protect every session that matters and create the gap on the days that don't cost you anything.

The protein floor is non-negotiable. One point eight to two point two grams per kilo of body weight, every day, regardless of whether you're in deficit or not. That protects the muscle. That protects the adaptation. That's what stops the body shutting down the way Dunne describes when you under-fuel chronically.

Before you start this block, answer three honest questions. Is this the right phase for a deficit? Are your sleep and training dialled? Is the gap between where you are and where you want to be meaningful enough to justify it? If three yeses, follow the protocol. If there's a no in there, take the principles forward and skip the deficit. Not every rider should chase race weight and this module is honest about that.

The full guide and tracking templates are in your downloads.

## SCRIPT 9: BUILDING POWER WHERE IT COUNTS (75–90 seconds)

Here's what nobody tells you about power development. The cycling internet has spent a decade dismissing low-cadence work as old school. Meanwhile, the coaches behind Tour de France yellow jerseys have been prescribing it for years.

When I had John Wakefield on the podcast — he coaches the Bora-Hansgrohe riders — he talked about the torque sessions he prescribes. Four by four minutes at forty to sixty RPM on a sustained climb. Hard but controlled. RPE seven out of ten.

The science has finally caught up. A study published in PLOS ONE compared low-cadence intervals against freely chosen cadence intervals. Same sessions, same total work, same effort. The low-cadence group improved VO2 max by nearly nine percent versus under five for the freely chosen group. Nearly double the result. The only difference — one number on the bike computer screen.

Here's why it works. At fifty RPM with the same wattage, the load per pedal stroke roughly doubles. Your slow-twitch fibres can't handle it alone. They recruit the fast-twitch fibres to help. Suddenly type two fibres are doing aerobic work, building mitochondria and capillaries they wouldn't build at normal cadence. You're developing the entire muscle, not just the part that was already trained.

This week you run two torque sessions. The interval cards are in your downloads — one climb-based, one on the flat. Fuel them properly. These are quality. And they're the sessions that convert everything you've built into power on the road.

## SCRIPT 10: THE BRAIN IS THE LIMITER (60–75 seconds)

You know the moment. The road tilts up. You look down at the head unit. The heart rate climbs. The breathing gets ragged. And before your body actually needs to stop, your brain tells you to back off. The wheel in front drifts away.

That moment is mental, not physical. Your brain holds back roughly twenty to forty percent of your available capacity as a safety buffer. You can't override it through willpower. But you can train it to interpret the signals differently.

Three things collapse most amateurs on climbs. First, no pacing plan. You follow the wheel in front, react to the gradient, and discover your limit by exceeding it. Second, negative self-talk. "I always blow up here." Your brain takes that as a direct instruction. Third, treating suffering as a signal to stop, instead of recognising it as a normal feature of hard riding.

This week you build the mental toolkit. A five-minute pre-ride preparation routine before every quality session. Name the session, visualise the hard part, pick one anchor phrase for when the brain pulls. And on the climb, three steps — name it, anchor, refocus. The protocol walks you through all of it, including the Stress Audit and the 3-2-1 Race Prep Protocol.

The legs are ready. This week we sort the head.

## SCRIPT 11: YOUR SYSTEM, INTEGRATED (60–75 seconds)

Here's the thing about the last ten weeks. You haven't just been following a programme. You've been learning a system. Architecture, fuelling, strength, recovery, power, the mental game — every pillar is in place. This week you learn to run it yourself.

The hardest skill in this entire course isn't running an interval at the right power. It isn't fuelling the long ride. It's making smart decisions when life happens. When you miss a session. When you get a cold. When work blows up. When the legs feel strange and you don't know if it's fatigue or fitness. That's self-coaching.

By Sunday night, you should be able to run the next twelve weeks of training without us. Here's how.

Fifteen minutes every Sunday. The Weekly Self-Coaching Review. Six questions. What did I actually do this week? What was the intensity distribution? Did I recover properly? What's the plan for next week? What needs to change? Am I still enjoying this?

The Decision Tree Cards in your downloads cover the three scenarios every self-coached rider faces — missed sessions, illness, travel. Walk through the ones you've actually dealt with. Then plan your first post-course mesocycle using the builder worksheet. Same rigour as the in-course weeks. Same structure. You're the coach now.

## SCRIPT 12: NOT DONE YET (75–90 seconds)

Twelve weeks ago you sat down with a blank workbook and put numbers on the page that probably weren't comfortable. Your FTP. Your weight. Your sleep. Your honest available hours. And then you did the work.

You built the architecture. Fuelled it properly. Added strength. Learned to recover. Developed power. Addressed the head. Learned to coach yourself. This week we measure what all of that produced.

The Exit Assessment is the same six domains as Week 1. Same conditions. Same scale. Same protocol. Same time of day. The number you produce here is the answer to the question that brought you to this course in the first place.

Here's what the data usually shows. Power up eight to twenty-five watts. Body composition shifted three to six kilos. Intensity distribution holding above seventy percent green. Sleep up thirty to sixty minutes from baseline. Power-to-weight improved five to twelve percent. That's the number that actually matters on the climbs.

If your numbers haven't moved as much as you'd hoped — that's still useful information. Twelve weeks is a meaningful block, but some adaptations take six to twelve months to compound. The system is what produces the long-term result. The discipline became a habit. The watts come.

After the assessment, build your ninety-day forward plan. Pick the single most important habit to carry into the next twelve weeks. Post your before and after in the community thread. And remember — this was always a start line, not a finish line. You're not done yet.

## DELIVERY NOTES (ALL SCRIPTS)

**Pace:** Conversational. Coffee stop energy. Not rushed, not performative. Slightly slower than you think you need — these are members, not strangers. They're already in.

**Energy:** Calm authority at the open. Genuine care through the middle — you're coaching, not selling. Warm and direct on the close.

**Namedrops:** Throw them away mid-sentence. "Dan Lorang — the guy coaching Pogacar —" not "Dan Lorang, who is the performance director at..." Don't pause on names. Don't list credentials.

**Eye line:** Straight to lens. One take per script if possible. No looking off-camera.

**Tone shift by module type:**
- Coaching modules (1, 2, 7, 11): Teacher energy. Clear, structured, you're laying out the plan.
- Nutrition modules (3, 8): Slight frustration on behalf of the rider who's been doing it wrong. The "I wish someone had told me this" register.
- Strength modules (4, 9): Enthusiastic. You're excited about what this does. The science-backed argument with genuine conviction.
- Recovery modules (5, 10): Quieter authority. These are the modules where less-is-more and the delivery should reflect that.
- Community modules (6, 12): Personal. Warmer. Slightly slower. These are the human moments in the course.

**What to avoid:** Don't smile too much at the top. Don't speed up at the end. The close should feel like a nudge from a coach, not a CTA. Never say "let's go" or "let's do this." The line is always "read the protocol" or "open the downloads" — specific, practical, low-key.

**If a script runs long:** Cut the research detail. Keep the "here's what to do this week" section and the close pointing to the protocol. The practical takeaway is the bit they'll remember.

**Personal experience:** Use the 86-to-79kg story only in Script 3 and Script 8. Don't repeat it elsewhere. One mention of a specific personal result per script maximum.

**The audience reminder:** These are members. They've paid. They're committed, intelligent adults with professional careers and limited time. Talk to them like a coach talks to an athlete, not like a YouTuber talks to a subscriber.

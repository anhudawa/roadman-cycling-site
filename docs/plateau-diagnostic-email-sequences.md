**Subject:** The nose-breathing test (and one interval session)  
**Preview text:** Two changes that fix your distribution  
**Delay:** +2 days after Email 1

Hey,

Right, here's the practical fix for your polarisation gap.

Two changes. That's it.

**Change 1: The nose-breathing rule for easy rides**

On every "easy" ride this week, breathe only through your nose. The moment you need to open your mouth, you're above your aerobic threshold. Slow down until you can close it again.

For most riders this means dropping 15-30 watts below where they normally ride "easy." It feels embarrassingly slow at first. That discomfort is telling you how far into zone 3 your easy rides have drifted.

Seiler calls this "talk test" intensity. If you can't speak in full paragraphs, you're too high. The nose-breathing version is harder to cheat.

Target: 3-4 rides this week, 60-90 minutes each, entirely nose-breathing. Heart rate will probably sit 15-20 bpm below where your "easy" rides have been.

**Change 2: One properly hard session**

Pick one day this week for this:

- 15 min warm-up, building to zone 2
- 5 x 4 minutes at 105-110% FTP, 3 minutes recovery between efforts
- 10 min cool-down

RPE should be 8-9 out of 10 on the efforts. If you finish the last interval thinking "I could do another," you went too easy.

That's your week: 3-4 genuinely easy rides and 1 session that properly hurts. The total training stress will probably be similar to what you're doing now. The distribution is completely different.

Dan Lorang's athletes don't do more work than everyone else. They do the right work at the right intensity. The polarisation model works because it respects the physiology — easy enough to recover from on the low days, hard enough to drive adaptation on the high days.

Track your power and heart rate on the easy rides. Within 2-3 weeks, you should see your pace at the same heart rate start to improve. That's your aerobic base responding to actual zone 2 stimulus instead of grey-zone fatigue.

Phil Burt spent 12 years as Head of Physiotherapy at British Cycling. He's worked with Wiggins, Froome, Kenny — the lot. His position is clear: cyclists who don't do targeted strength work break down. It's not a question of if, it's when. Lower back pain, knee tracking issues, hip flexor tightness — these aren't bad luck. They're the predictable result of spending thousands of hours in a fixed position with zero posterior chain work.

Here's the physiology: when you produce force on the pedals, you're recruiting motor units. Strength training increases the number and size of motor units you can recruit, and it improves your neuromuscular efficiency — meaning you produce more force per pedal stroke at the same metabolic cost.

Research consistently shows 2-3 sessions per week of cycling-specific strength work can improve time to exhaustion at threshold by 15-20% and improve cycling economy by 3-5%. For a rider with an FTP of 250w, that's the equivalent of adding 8-12 watts without touching your on-bike training.

### Email 2 of 3 — Actionable Fix

**Subject:** 2 sessions per week, 30 minutes each  
**Preview text:** The cycling-specific strength framework  
**Delay:** +2 days after Email 1

Hey,

Here's the strength framework. Two sessions per week, 30 minutes each, focused on the movement patterns that transfer to the bike.

**Session A — Lower body + core (evening after interval day)**

- Bulgarian split squats: 3 x 8 each leg
- Romanian deadlifts (dumbbells): 3 x 10
- Single-leg glute bridges: 3 x 12 each leg
- Copenhagen plank: 3 x 20 seconds each side
- Dead bugs: 3 x 10 each side

**Session B — Posterior chain + stability (evening after second interval day)**

- Step-ups (weighted): 3 x 8 each leg
- Nordic hamstring curls (assisted): 3 x 6
- Banded clamshells: 3 x 15 each side
- Pallof press: 3 x 10 each side
- Single-leg calf raises: 3 x 12 each side

**Key principles:**

1. **Unilateral work dominates.** Cycling is a single-leg sport. Your strength work should reflect that. Phil Burt has highlighted the bilateral strength imbalances cyclists develop — single-leg movements correct these.

2. **Progressive overload matters.** Start with bodyweight or light dumbbells. Add 1-2kg per week when you can complete all reps with proper form. If you're not progressing the load, you're just doing cardio with weights.

3. **Timing around rides.** Do your strength sessions on the same day as your hard intervals — ideally in the evening after a morning ride. This preserves your rest days for actual recovery. The research from Seiler's group supports this: concurrent training works when strength and cycling are separated by 6+ hours, so a morning intervals session and an evening gym session fits perfectly.

4. **Rep range: 6-12.** You're building strength and muscular endurance, not training for a powerlifting meet. Control the eccentric (lowering) phase — 2-3 seconds down, 1 second up.

Start this week. Track your weights in a notes app or a training log. In 6 weeks you should notice improved stability in the saddle, less lower back fatigue on long rides, and better power output in the last hour when everyone else is falling apart.

## Sequence 4: Fuelling Deficit

**Tag trigger:** `limiter:fuelling-deficit`

### Email 1 of 3 — Diagnose & Educate

**Subject:** Your diagnostic result: you're not eating enough  
**Preview text:** Under-fuelling is sabotaging your training

Hey,

Anthony here.

Your Plateau Diagnostic flagged fuelling as your primary limiter. This is the one that makes riders defensive, so I'll just be direct: you're almost certainly not eating enough to support the training you're doing.

Prof Sam Impey's research at Birmingham is some of the best work on endurance athlete nutrition. His "fuel for the work required" (FFTWR) model is built on a simple principle: match your carbohydrate intake to the demands of each session. High-intensity day? High carb. Recovery day? Lower carb. It's not low-carb. It's not high-carb. It's periodised, like your training.

Dr David Dunne has worked with professional cycling teams on exactly this problem. What he sees consistently: riders eating 40-50g of carbs per hour during hard sessions when the evidence supports 80-120g per hour for efforts over 90 minutes. They're leaving 20-30% of their performance on the table through fuelling alone.

Here's what a fuelling deficit looks like in practice:

- **Body composition stalled despite consistent training.** You'd think more riding = leaner. But chronic under-fuelling tanks your metabolic rate and your body holds onto everything.
- **Power fading in the last 60 minutes of long rides.** Your glycogen stores are empty because you didn't load properly or fuel during the ride.
- **Poor recovery between sessions.** Without adequate post-ride protein and carbs in the 30-minute window, muscle protein synthesis is compromised.
- **Getting ill more often.** Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) starts with immune suppression. If you're catching every cold going around, your energy availability might be too low.

The old cycling culture of "ride more, eat less, get light" has done enormous damage. The fastest version of you is the properly fuelled version, not the leanest one.

### Email 2 of 3 — Actionable Fix

**Subject:** Your fuelling numbers for this week  
**Preview text:** Exactly what to eat around your key sessions  
**Delay:** +2 days after Email 1

Hey,

Here's the practical fuelling fix. I'm going to give you the numbers for a typical hard training day, because that's where most of the deficit lives.

**Pre-ride (2-3 hours before):**
- 1-1.5g carbs per kg bodyweight
- For a 75kg rider that's 75-112g of carbs
- Examples: porridge with banana and honey, or rice with eggs
- If you train early morning, at minimum get 40-50g of carbs in — a banana and a gel, toast with jam, anything

**During ride (sessions over 60 minutes):**
- Target: 80-90g carbs per hour for hard sessions
- Mix of maltodextrin and fructose at a 1:0.8 ratio for best absorption
- Start fuelling at the 20-minute mark, not when you're already empty
- Prof Impey's research shows gut trainability — if 80g feels like a lot, start at 40g/hr and increase by 10g each week

**Post-ride (within 30 minutes):**
- 0.3g protein per kg bodyweight + 1g carbs per kg bodyweight
- For a 75kg rider: 22g protein + 75g carbs
- Recovery shake with a banana works. Rice and chicken works. It doesn't need to be complicated.

**Daily protein target:**
- 1.6-2.0g per kg bodyweight, spread across 4 meals
- Dr Dunne's recommendation for endurance athletes in heavy training blocks
- Most cyclists I audit are getting 0.8-1.2g. That gap is costing you adaptation.

**Easy/rest days:**
- Reduce carbs by 30-40% vs hard days
- Protein stays the same (recovery is still happening)
- This is the FFTWR principle in action — you're not eating less overall, you're matching intake to demand

**This week's challenge:**
Pick your two hardest sessions. Hit the pre-ride, during-ride, and post-ride targets above. Track how you feel in the last 30 minutes of those sessions compared to last week. Most riders notice the difference immediately.
